\section{Background and literature review}
\label{sec:bg}

This chapter attempt to give a brief context behind bipedal locomotion and more specifically the Reinforcement Learning framework that are used in the literature to solve this problem.

\subsection{Definitions}
\label{sec:bg-defs}

First a few definition as the following term will recur :
\textbf{Underactuation} is the excess of degrees of freedom over actuators. 
\textbf{Unilateral contact} means a foot can push but not pull, and only inside the \textbf{friction cone}. 
The point where the push applies is the \textbf{centre of pressure}, confined to the contact patch. 
\textbf{Duty factor} is the fraction of a step each foot spends in contact with the floor, and a \textbf{flight phase} is the interval with no foot down. 
The \textbf{sim-to-real gap} is the performance missmatch on transfer from a simulated plant to the physical one. Generally because the real plant is out of the distribution the policy was trained against.
In the context of this thesis, \textbf{Failure} is episode termination on body contact, or on leaving the measured workspace. 
\textbf{Stability margin}, in the context of reinforcement learning, is reported as a survivor count and a seed variance rather.
\textbf{Plant}, borrowed from control theory, this refer to the robot to be controlled.

\subsection{Abbreviations}

\textbf{RL} Reinforcement Learning
\textbf{CoM} Center of Mass


\subsection{Bipedal locomotion approach}
\label{sec:bg-hard}

The problem can be approached in different way.
The simplest case is for a robot with flat feet on a flat terrain that move slowly enough that the innertia can be neglected. This is what was done for the WABOT-1 in (1973) \cite{waseda2026wabot1}
It this case, making it stable boils down to keeping the projection of it's CoM inside the feet Polygon.

If one want to go faster, The second step is to account for the innertia and now, its not the CoM but the Zero Momement point that must be inside the feet Polygon. This was done by Asimo (2000) \cite{whatimakethis2020asimo}.
Running involve a flight phase which is not compatible with the previous approach as it need one feet on the floor at anytime.
To enable running, Raibert\cite{raibert1986} propose a foot placement method that satisfy the following equation :

\begin{equation}
  x_f \;=\; \tfrac{1}{2}\,\dot x\,T_s \;+\; k\,(\dot x - \dot x_d),
  \label{eq:neutral-point}
\end{equation}

$x_f$ is the fore-aft foot placement, measured from the hip at touchdown, in metres. \\
$\dot x$ is the current forward speed in metres per second. \\
$\dot x_d$ is the commanded forward speed, in metres per second.  \\
$T_s$ is the stance duration in seconds. \\
$k$ is a velocity feedback gain, also in seconds.  \\
The first term is the neutral point, which yield no net fore-aft impulse. 
The second term corrects for the speed : running faster than commanded places the foot further forward, so stance decelerates the body.
The lineage runs through BigDog~\cite{raibert2008bigdog} to Atlas.
The trick there is to realise that the gait can be stabilized just by plancing the feet at the right place and the right time. 
It allowed to make walking robot using point contact feet unlike the previous need for large flat feet.
It was further refined by being solved online and rebranded as Model predictive control approach. \cite{kuindersma2016atlas}
This is what made the early Boston dynamics possible.
This approach and the previous one require a complete and accurate model of the plant. 
But in practice the plant parameters are only partially known. 
Controller using classical control theory are only good for one plant and have an intrasec stability margin which related to how much variance from that optimal plant can be tolerated before the control is unable to stabilize it.
To solve that, RL instead train a neural network to act as the policy. One of the advantage is that the policy can be made arbitrarely robust to plant missmatch by variying the plant parameters during training (aka domain randomization).

To complicate it all, the contact with the ground are said to be unilated and saturating. This mean that the foot can only push against the ground and only within the friction cone otherwise, it slip.
The second factor that complexify it are the timescale. A legged robot can be seen as an inverted pendulum of leg length $h$.
If no command is applied, it will tend to diverges with a time constant of $\sqrt{h/g}$ or about 310\,ms for a 1 meter tall robot like ours. When running, the contact with the ground only last a few dozen ms.
In that timeframe, the robot must be able to get the feedback from the foot, compute the required command and apply it. This required actuator with decent bandwith.
In the spirit of foretelling, the robot which this thesis are about have a bandiwith of 0.8\,Hz pole in servo mode and 12 \,Hz in MIT mode. 
Attentive reader will thus notice the traquenard that will come back later.


\subsection{Reinforcement learning for locomotion}
\label{sec:bg-rl-loco}

\subsubsection{Actuator control architecture}

Several actuator-level control architectures have been used by the literature. 
The main one are Direct torque control, Direct position control, Impedance control.
Direct torque control let the policy command the actuator torque directly, which gives the policy the highest level of control authority.
Direct position control let the policy command a position target, which is then tracked by a low-level controller.
Impedance control let the policy command a desired mechanical behaviour through position, stiffness, damping, and/or torque-related parameters.
The last one a good compromise between control authority and need on the hardware in term of latency and bandwidth.

\textbf{Direct torque control} provides the policy with the highest level of flexibility, but also imposes the most demanding communication and control-loop timing requirements. 
In simulation, the policy can directly command joint torques at a high frequency.  ~\cite{hwangbo2019anymal}
On the physical robot, however, closing such a fast loop requires the policy, communication bus, actuator controller, and state estimation to operate with sufficiently low latency. 
In the present hardware architecture, this approach was not practical through the CAN communication bus at the required frequency.

\textbf{Direct position control} substantially reduces these requirements by delegating the low-level actuator dynamics to the motor controller.
Position targets passed to the joint controller are what many learned locomotion uses~\cite{tan2018sim2real,rudin2021}.
Two variants were investigated: conventional smart-servo position control and the MIT control mode with fixed compliance parameters~\cite{katz2019minicheetah}.
Both allow the RL policy to operate at a lower frequency while the actuator controller handles the fast inner loop.

\textbf{Impedance control} provides an intermediate level of control authority.\cite{bogdanovic2020impedance}
Instead of directly specifying the actuator torque or being restricted to only position, the policy can influence the desired mechanical behaviour through position, stiffness, damping, and/or torque-related parameters.
This avoid the issue of needing to close the torque loop at high speed over the communication bus while giving the policy greater freedom than pure position control.
It also expose the actuator dynamics to the policy and reduce sim2real gap as smart servo mode dont always expose the frequency and parameters of their inner loop.

\subsubsection{Gait prior}

The second major design dimension is the amount and location of prior structure imposed on the gait.

A strong prior can be introduced through a \textbf{reference trajectory}.
In this case, the policy is primarily trained to track an authored motion.This is one the thing that was attempted on the Cassie robot~\cite{xie2019}.
This strongly restricts the reachable set of motions and can substantially simplify learning.
However, the quality of the resulting behaviour is bounded by the choice of reference: there is no guarantee that the selected trajectory is optimal for the particular robot morphology and dynamics.

A less strict prior can instead be introduced through a \textbf{trajectory generator} whose parameters are modulated by the policy~\cite{pmtg2018}.
This preserves the assumption that locomotion should have a structured, periodic form while allowing the learned controller to search for a gait adapted to the plant.
Trajectory generators can themselves provide different levels of freedom about the gait parameters.

At the stricter end of this generator-based formulation, the policy only modifies a small number of \textbf{high-level gait parameters}, such as phase, frequency, stride length, or amplitude. ~\cite{cpgrl2022}
This is similar the first approach in the sense of tracking a referece trajectory but instead that trajectory is parameterised and the high level parameters are learned instead of letting the policy learn the low level joint command needed to mimmick the reference gait.
This provides a relatively small search space but limits the set of gaits that can be represented.

A more moderate prior allows the policy to modify the \textbf{complete trajectory}. Rather than predicting every trajectory sample independently, the possible trajectories can be represented using a low-dimensional basis, such as Fourier series~\cite{shafii2010tfs} or Bézier curves.
The policy then modifies the coefficients of this representation. This retains useful structural assumptions like periodicity and smoothness, while allowing the shape of the gait itself to become a learned variable.

It is also possible to do withouth a trajectory generator \textbf{nor an explicit gait shape prior}. 
The policy learns the complete joint trajectory~\cite{heess2017emergence}. 
Periodic locomotion can still be encouraged through reward shaping, but periodicity then becomes an emergent property rather than a constraint.
Such an approach provides a large reachable set but considerably increases the exploration and learning cost, and there is no guarantee that the learned solution will converge to a periodic gait.

Finaly, one should note that the prior, if any, can be introduced at different levels: through the \textbf{reward}~\cite{siekmann2021}, through a \textbf{reference motion}~\cite{peng2018deepmimic} or through an authored \textbf{trajectory generator}~\cite{pmtg2018,cpgrl2022}.
These choices determine how much of the gait is fixed and how much is available to the policy at run time at the expense of reward cost.

\subsubsection{Curriculum design} The third major design dimension is the curriculum used to train the policy.
To help the policy converge, the training environment can be modified to make the task easier at the start and gradually increase difficulty as the policy improves.
The literature has explored a variety of curriculum design choices, including the order in which different tasks are presented, the rate at which difficulty is increased, and the specific parameters that are modified to create the curriculum.

\textbf{Terrain and command curricula.} A common scheme starts every agent on flat ground with a narrow command range.
As its performance clears a threshold, the agent is promoted to rougher terrain or a wider velocity range, and demoted otherwise~\cite{rudin2021}.
Difficulty then tracks each agent's competence instead of a fixed schedule.
This approach is well suited for massively parrallel training, where each agent can be promoted or demoted independently.

\textbf{Automatic domain randomisation.} Rather than fixing the domain-randomisation range before training, the range can widen automatically once the policy clears a performance threshold at its current extremes~\cite{akkaya2019rubiks}.
The randomisation range itself becomes the curriculum parameter, expanding only as fast as the policy can tolerate.
The approach needs a reliable in-training performance signal.
A noisy signal lets the range grow faster than the policy can track, destabilising training.

Whether the curriculum targets terrain, command range or randomisation range, an adaptive schedule removes a manually tuned progression rate.
In its place, the design now needs a performance threshold, and a metric to compute it. Which come with the same set of issue as the design of the reward function where agent can learn to game it instead of the intended behaviour.
