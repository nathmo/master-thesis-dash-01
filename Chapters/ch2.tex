\section{Background and State of the Art}

\subsection{What makes bipedal locomotion hard}
\label{sec:bg-hard}

A legged machine is not a robot arm with feet. An arm is bolted to the world, so every joint torque
it produces is reacted by the floor through its base; a legged machine is connected to the world
only through intermittent, unilateral contacts. Three consequences follow, and they organise
everything in this chapter.

\textbf{It is underactuated.} A biped with six leg motors has twelve degrees of freedom, because the
body itself can translate in three directions and rotate about three axes with no motor attached to
any of them. The only way to influence those six \emph{base} degrees of freedom is indirectly,
through the ground. During a flight phase there is no ground, so the centre of mass follows a
ballistic arc that no amount of actuation can alter — the machine can only choose the posture it
will land in. This thesis makes the point structurally: the base degrees of freedom are released one
at a time as a training curriculum (\cref{sec:rl-cpg}), and the milestone at which the robot fails
tells you which one it cannot control.

\textbf{The control inputs are unilateral and saturating.} A foot can push on the ground but not
pull. It can only push within a friction cone, or it slips. The point of application of that push —
the \emph{centre of pressure}, CoP — can only move within the contact patch, which for a point foot
means it cannot move at all. And the most powerful input available, \emph{where to put the next
foot}, is not continuous: it is chosen once per step, and it is bounded by the leg's reachable
workspace. A controller therefore has a small number of discrete, bounded opportunities to act, not
a continuous authority.

\textbf{The timescales are short.} An inverted pendulum of leg length $h$ diverges with time
constant $\sqrt{h/g}$ — about 310\,ms for this robot. Ground contacts in running last tens of
milliseconds. Any correction must therefore be planned, commanded and \emph{mechanically executed}
inside a window measured in tenths of a second, which makes actuator bandwidth a first-class
constraint rather than an implementation detail. This project measured that the hard way: the
drive's position loop turned out to be a 0.8\,Hz pole, $\tau = 199$\,ms, against that 310\,ms
divergence (\cref{sec:plant-id-drive}).

Running adds one thing to walking. In walking the centre of mass \emph{vaults} over a stiff support
leg, rising at mid-stance; in running it \emph{bounces}, falling at mid-stance as the leg compresses
and rising again as it rebounds, with an aerial phase in which both feet are off the ground. The
distinction is not cosmetic — it changes which reduced model applies, which is the subject of
\cref{sec:bg-slip}, and it is why frameworks built for walking do not extend to running by simply
increasing the commanded speed.

Because the full dynamics are intractable to reason about directly, the field works with
\emph{reduced models}: the smallest system that reproduces the behaviour of interest. Three appear
repeatedly in this thesis. The \textbf{inverted pendulum} (a point mass on a rigid massless leg)
models walking and gives the divergence time above. The \textbf{spring-loaded inverted pendulum}
(SLIP — a point mass on a springy leg) models running. The \textbf{capture point}~\cite{pratt2006capture}
is the location at which a foot must be placed to bring the inverted pendulum exactly to rest, and
therefore the natural target for a foot-placement controller; it underlies both the hand-wired pitch
reflex of \cref{sec:rl-reflexes} and the scripted baseline of \cref{sec:scripted-baseline}. That
these toy models remain load-bearing on a real machine is not a simplification of convenience: the
free-topple time of the plain inverted pendulum turns out to \emph{be} the failure signature this
thesis spends a chapter diagnosing (\cref{tab:freefall}).

\subsection{A short history: six answers to one question}
\label{sec:bg-history}

The history of walking machines is more legible if it is read as a sequence of answers to a single
question: \textbf{where does the stability come from?} Every family below answers it differently,
and each answer buys something and costs something. They are not a progression from worse to better
— all six are in active use, and the choice among them is governed less by fashion than by what the
hardware can physically do, which is the subject of \cref{sec:bg-actuation}.

\subsubsection{Stability from geometry: static walking and the zero-moment point}
\label{sec:bg-zmp}

The first walking machines avoided the dynamics rather than solving them. WABOT-1, built at Waseda
University in 1973 and generally credited as the first full-scale anthropomorphic walker, moved
slowly enough that its own momentum could be neglected~\cite{lim2007waseda}: it was, in effect, a
statue that occasionally shifted its weight. Stability meant keeping the vertical projection of the
centre of mass inside the \emph{support polygon}, the convex hull of the points touching the ground.
Satisfy that at every instant and the machine cannot tip, by a purely geometric argument requiring
no model of the dynamics at all.

The dynamic generalisation is the \textbf{zero-moment point} (ZMP), introduced by Vukobratovi\'c and
Juricic in 1969~\cite{vukobratovic1969} and retrospectively surveyed by its
authors~\cite{vukobratovic2004}. The ZMP is the point on the ground about which the horizontal
components of the ground reaction moment vanish — equivalently, the point at which gravity and all
inertial forces can be replaced by a single vertical force. If the ZMP remains strictly inside the
support polygon, the foot does not rotate about its edge, and the contact behaves as though the
robot were bolted to the floor. One scalar condition thus converts the balance problem into a
geometric constraint a planner can check.

This framework carried the field for three decades. Honda's P2 and its descendant
ASIMO~\cite{hirai1998honda} made it famous; Kajita's 3D linear inverted pendulum
model~\cite{kajita2001lipm} and ZMP preview control~\cite{kajita2003preview} made it cheap, by
reducing the robot to a point mass constrained to a plane of constant height and solving for a
centre-of-mass trajectory whose ZMP tracks a planned footstep sequence — a problem small enough to
solve online on 1990s computers.

\emph{What it buys.} Transparency and a checkable guarantee. The condition is a single inequality, it
requires no contact model beyond the geometry of the foot, and because the foot is never permitted to
rotate, the approach tolerates a fairly inaccurate dynamic model. It is safe, and it was the only
thing that worked for a long time.

\emph{What it costs.} The guarantee is bought by never leaving the regime in which it holds. The
support polygon \emph{is} the foot, so the machine needs large flat feet and flat ground. Keeping the
centre of mass at constant height demands a permanently bent-knee posture, which is why ASIMO-class
walkers have a cost of transport near 1.6 against roughly 0.2 for a walking human. Toe-off and
heel-strike — the foot rotations that make human walking efficient — are forbidden by construction,
since they place the ZMP on the boundary. And most decisively for this thesis, ZMP is a
\emph{walking} framework: running contains an interval with no support polygon whatsoever, so the
central condition is not merely violated but undefined.

\subsubsection{Stability from the mechanism: passive dynamics}
\label{sec:bg-passive}

The opposite extreme arrived with McGeer's passive dynamic walkers~\cite{mcgeer1990}: a pair of legs
with no motors, no sensors and no computer, which walk stably down a shallow ramp powered only by
gravity. The gait is a \emph{limit cycle} — a closed orbit in state space that the machine returns
to on its own after a small disturbance — and it is a property of the linkage's geometry and mass
distribution, not of any controller. Collins, Ruina, Tedrake and Wisse~\cite{collins2005} later added
the minimum actuation needed to walk on the level (an ankle push-off replacing the slope) and
measured a cost of transport of about 0.20, comparable to a human and an order of magnitude better
than the ZMP walkers of the same era.

\emph{What it buys.} Efficiency that no other approach has matched, and a conceptual result that
outlives the machines: \textbf{morphology is control}. A well-proportioned leg performs most of the
stabilisation for free, and the actuators then fight nothing, because the commanded motion is what
the mechanism already wanted to do.

\emph{What it costs.} Authority. The basin of attraction is tiny — a centimetre of unexpected terrain
ends the gait — speed and heading are essentially not commandable, and each machine is tuned to a
single operating point. There is stability without controllability.

The lesson that transfers is the one this thesis re-derives from the opposite direction. If the
mechanism can carry the gait, a weak controller suffices; if the mechanism fights the gait, no
controller rescues it. \Cref{sec:hw-force-map} and \cref{sec:speed-ceiling} are that statement made
quantitative for DASH-01.

\subsubsection{Stability from elasticity: spring-mass templates}
\label{sec:bg-slip}

Biomechanics supplied the model that walking frameworks lack. Blickhan~\cite{blickhan1989} showed
that running, hopping and trotting — across animals spanning orders of magnitude in body mass — are
all described by the same \emph{spring-loaded inverted pendulum}: a point mass on a massless linear
spring. The leg compresses on touchdown, storing impact energy elastically, and returns it at
push-off. Full and Koditschek~\cite{full1999templates} named this class of model a \emph{template} —
the simplest system exhibiting the behaviour — with the physical animal or robot as its
\emph{anchor}, and argued that good legged design consists of building an anchor that behaves like
its template.

The engineering consequence is direct: a running machine wants a real spring in its leg, and a large
part of the controller's job is to keep the mechanical system near a spring-mass limit cycle rather
than to compute a trajectory from scratch. Hurst's ATRIAS~\cite{hubicki2016atrias} is the most
literal instantiation — a biped built to \emph{be} a SLIP, with fibreglass leaf springs in series
with its drives — and Cassie and Digit are its commercial descendants.

\emph{What it buys.} Correct physics for running rather than a walking framework pushed past its
validity. The spring recovers impact energy that would otherwise be dissipated as heat in the
gearbox, and — a point often missed — the spring rejects high-frequency disturbances at the speed of
sound in steel, which is faster than any feedback loop can react.

\emph{What it costs.} The spring rate is a \emph{design commitment}, chosen once, in hardware. It
sets the leg's natural frequency and therefore the narrow band of speeds at which the machine is
efficient, and it cannot be retuned in software. Series elasticity also inserts a low-pass filter
between motor and ground, capping force-control bandwidth. Both costs appear in this thesis in their
sharpest form: DASH-01's passive ankle spring is exactly such a commitment, and getting its
stiffness wrong is the central mechanical finding of the work
(\cref{sec:ankle-build,sec:hw-force-map}).

\subsubsection{Stability from foot placement: the hopping lineage}
\label{sec:bg-hopping}

Raibert's hopping machines~\cite{raibert1986} answered the question with the insight that reframed
the field: a running machine does not have to be balanced \emph{continuously}, it has to be balanced
\emph{once per step}, at the instant it commits to where the next foot will go. Control decomposed
into three nearly independent loops — hopping height set by thrust energy injected at
bottom-of-stance, forward speed set by placing the foot relative to a neutral point,
\begin{equation}
  x_f \;=\; \tfrac{1}{2}\,\dot x\,T_s \;+\; k\,(\dot x - \dot x_d),
  \label{eq:neutral-point}
\end{equation}
and body attitude corrected by hip torque \emph{during stance only}, when the foot is pinned by
friction and the leg can serve as a reaction strut. From the 1984 one-legged hopper this lineage runs
through BigDog~\cite{raibert2008bigdog} to the Boston Dynamics Atlas, and it remains the skeleton of
most classical running controllers — including the scripted baseline of
\cref{sec:scripted-baseline}, in which \cref{eq:neutral-point} appears directly.

\emph{What it buys.} Running, which is what ZMP cannot do. It needs no support polygon, so point feet
are permissible; it needs no accurate dynamic model, since the placement law has essentially one
empirical gain; and the three-loop decomposition is simple enough to implement on 1980s hardware.

\emph{What it costs.} Three assumptions, examined in \cref{sec:bg-raibert} because DASH-01 violates
all three. And no proof: the machines demonstrably worked, but the approach offers no certificate
that they will.

\subsubsection{Stability from a provable limit cycle: hybrid zero dynamics}
\label{sec:bg-hzd}

Passive dynamics gives a limit cycle one cannot command; Raibert gives commandable behaviour one
cannot prove. Hybrid zero dynamics (HZD) is the synthesis. Instead of tracking a trajectory indexed
by \emph{time}, the controller enforces \emph{virtual constraints}: relations among the joint angles
parameterised by the robot's own progress through the step. Enforcing them collapses the closed-loop
dynamics onto a low-dimensional surface — the \emph{zero dynamics} — whose remaining motion is a
single degree of freedom, and the orbital stability of the resulting gait can then be certified
through a Poincar\'e map. Westervelt, Grizzle and Koditschek~\cite{westervelt2003hzd} established the
framework, the accompanying monograph~\cite{westervelt2007book} systematised it, and
RABBIT~\cite{chevallereau2003rabbit} demonstrated it on a physical underactuated biped with point
feet.

\emph{What it buys.} The only genuine stability proof in this chapter, for an underactuated machine
with point feet — precisely the case ZMP cannot address. And an idea that has outlived its
framework: indexing a gait by \emph{phase} rather than by time, which reappears in essentially every
modern learned locomotion architecture, including the gait phase $\varphi$ of this thesis's own
generator (\cref{sec:rl-cpg}).

\emph{What it costs.} The proof is a proof about the \emph{model}. The gait itself comes from an
offline trajectory optimisation over that model, so accuracy requirements are severe and the
guarantee degrades silently when the plant is wrong — a failure mode this thesis encountered in a
different guise (\cref{tab:plant-id}). Each gait is a separate optimisation, so speed changes,
turning and terrain must be handled by scheduling among precomputed cycles rather than emerging from
the controller.

\subsubsection{Stability from re-solving the problem: optimisation-based control}
\label{sec:bg-mpc}

By the late 2000s computers were fast enough to solve the control problem online, repeatedly.
Wieber~\cite{wieber2006mpc} recast ZMP walking as \emph{model-predictive control}: at each cycle,
solve a constrained optimisation over a short future horizon, apply only the first input, discard the
rest, and re-solve at the next cycle. Because the constraint set is written down explicitly, friction
cones, torque bounds and kinematic limits are handled as constraints rather than as hoped-for
margins. Kuindersma et al.~\cite{kuindersma2016atlas} built the whole-body version that ran Atlas at
the DARPA Robotics Challenge, and Di Carlo et al.~\cite{dicarlo2018mpc} produced the convex
formulation on MIT Cheetah 3 that now underlies most classical legged stacks.

\emph{What it buys.} Constraints as first-class objects, genuine feedback at the level of the
trajectory rather than only at the joints, and commandability that passive dynamics and HZD lack —
the same controller walks at different speeds, turns, and recovers from pushes.

\emph{What it costs.} An accurate model, because the optimiser trusts it over the whole horizon and
errors compound; a solver fast enough to close a loop, which constrains the formulation to be convex
or nearly so; and — the requirement that decides this thesis — \textbf{high-bandwidth torque
control}, since the output is a force command that must reach the ground essentially unfiltered.
Choosing \emph{which} foot contacts when is usually still prescribed by hand, because optimising the
contact schedule turns a continuous problem into a combinatorial one.

\subsubsection{Stability from experience: learned control}
\label{sec:bg-rl}

The most recent answer replaces the analytic chain with a function fitted in simulation. A policy —
typically a small neural network — is optimised by reinforcement learning to maximise a reward over
millions of simulated episodes, and the planner, whole-body controller and joint tracker collapse
into a single evaluation. Heess et al.~\cite{heess2017emergence} showed that rich terrain and a
simple reward produce recognisable running gaits with no gait prior at all, and
PPO~\cite{schulman2017ppo} became the workhorse algorithm; fast simulators such as
MuJoCo~\cite{todorov2012mujoco} made the sample cost affordable.

Transfer to hardware is the hard part, and the toolkit that solved it is worth naming because this
thesis uses all of it. \emph{Domain randomisation}~\cite{tobin2017dr,tan2018sim2real} varies the
plant's parameters every episode, so the policy is optimised over a \emph{set} of robots and cannot
overfit any single wrong one. \emph{Learned actuator models}~\cite{hwangbo2019anymal} replace the
idealised motor with a network identified from hardware data — the step that first carried RL onto a
real quadruped convincingly. \emph{Teacher--student distillation}~\cite{lee2020terrain,miki2022perceptive}
trains with privileged simulator state and then distils into a policy that observes only what the
robot can actually sense. On bipeds, Siekmann et al.~\cite{siekmann2021} demonstrated the full range
of gaits on Cassie through periodic reward composition, Radosavovic et
al.~\cite{radosavovic2024humanoid} carried the approach to a full humanoid, and Crowley et
al.~\cite{crowley2024cassie} produced the current bipedal 100\,m record: 24.73\,s,
$\approx$4.1\,m/s average. Motion priors from reference data are a parallel
strand~\cite{peng2018deepmimic}, relevant here as the archetype of a \emph{shape} prior
(\cref{sec:bg-prior}).

\emph{What it buys.} No analytic contact model is required, which matters because contact is exactly
where analytic models are weakest and simulators are adequate. The policy is optimised over a
distribution of states and plants rather than around one operating point, which is the source of its
robustness claim (\cref{sec:bg-margin}). And inference is microseconds on cheap hardware, so the
entire stack fits on a single-board computer.

\emph{What it costs.} No stability proof, and no diagnosis when it fails. The reward function becomes
the specification, and an optimiser will satisfy the specification rather than the intent —
\cref{sec:rl-position} and \cref{tab:negative} catalogue what that looks like in practice. Sample
cost is $10^8$--$10^9$ environment steps, so each result is a multi-day cluster job. And the entire
result rests on the simulator: every modelling error is silently baked into the policy, which is the
thesis this document spends \cref{sec:plant-id} demonstrating.

Two observations close the survey. First, the families are complementary rather than competing, and

\subsection{The hopping lineage's assumptions, and why DASH-01 breaks all three}
\label{sec:bg-raibert}

The three-loop decomposition of \cref{sec:bg-hopping} is not free. It embeds three assumptions, and
they are worth naming individually, because DASH-01 violates all three and the manner of each
violation is a result of this thesis rather than a caveat to it.

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{A point foot is sufficient, because attitude authority comes from the hip and not
        from the foot.} This is the assumption most often quoted as a virtue of the approach, and
        it holds only while the hip route is actually open: hip torque against a pinned foot is a
        reaction torque, so the leg must have a usable moment arm, the stance must last long enough
        to apply it, and the friction cone must hold. When those fail, a point foot has
        \emph{no} remaining attitude authority at all, because a point contact has by definition
        zero centre-of-pressure travel. \Cref{sec:scripted-baseline} measures exactly this state on
        DASH-01: at milestone m3 the single toe sphere yields zero centre-of-pressure authority at
        \emph{any} ankle stiffness — the ankle there is not weak, it is disconnected — and
        \cref{sec:hw-force-map} shows the passive ankle that would have supplied the missing
        authority delivers 0.42 body weights of a 3.5\,BW requirement, clearing the running-stance
        requirement over 0.0\,\% of the region. The practical conclusion is that a point foot is an
        acceptable simplification only for a machine whose hip-and-stance route is strong, and that
        a foot with real fore--aft extent is a load-bearing design choice rather than a refinement.
  \item \textbf{The commanded foot placement is reachable.} \Cref{eq:neutral-point} returns a
        position, not a feasible one. On this robot the usable fore--aft workspace is
        $\pm 0.30$\,m of a 1.06\,m geometric span (\cref{sec:speed-ceiling}), and with the toe
        $\sim$0.92\,m below the centre of mass a pitch rate $\dot\theta$ sweeps the contact point
        forward at $L\dot\theta$ — measured to outrun the controller's corrective foot motion
        \textbf{2:1} until the workspace saturates, in under a second
        (\cref{sec:scripted-baseline}). The placement law does not fail because it is wrong; it
        fails because the plant diverges faster than the foot can travel, which is a statement
        about linkage proportions rather than about control.
  \item \textbf{The actuator executes what the placement law commands, within one stance.}
        Raibert's hydraulics did. DASH-01's drive is a 0.8\,Hz position loop,
        $\tau = 199$\,ms (\cref{sec:plant-id-drive}), against an inverted-pendulum divergence
        constant $\sqrt{h/g} = 311$\,ms. Latching the target once per swing rather than tracking it
        continuously — the structural choice \cref{sec:scripted-baseline} defends at length —
        recovers only $\sim$83\,\% of the commanded placement by touchdown.
\end{enumerate}

None of these three is repaired by replacing the classical controller with a learned one, which is
the substance of \cref{sec:scripted-baseline} and the reason this thesis reaches a mechanical
conclusion from a control experiment.

\subsection{The actuation axis, which decides which approach is available}
\label{sec:bg-actuation}

\Cref{tab:bg-approaches} ends each row with a hardware demand, and that column is not incidental. A
control approach is only available to a machine whose actuators can meet its requirement, so the
actuation choice is made \emph{before} the control choice whether or not the designer notices. Three
families dominate.

\textbf{Hydraulic.} Raibert's hoppers, BigDog~\cite{raibert2008bigdog} and the Boston Dynamics Atlas
are hydraulic: enormous force density, very high bandwidth, and a commanded force that arrives
essentially instantaneously. The cost is a pump, a reservoir, noise, leaks and a floor on system
size and budget that puts the family out of reach for most laboratories and all students.

\textbf{Series elastic (SEA).} Pratt and Williamson~\cite{pratt1995sea} proposed deliberately
inserting a spring between gearbox and load. Force then becomes a \emph{position} measurement — the
spring's deflection — which is cheap and accurate to sense, and the spring protects the gear teeth
from impact while storing running energy in the SLIP sense of \cref{sec:bg-slip}. ATRIAS, Cassie and
Digit are the canonical bipeds~\cite{hubicki2016atrias}. The cost is that the spring is a low-pass
filter: force bandwidth is capped by the spring--load resonance, and the compliance adds states that
must themselves be controlled.

\textbf{Quasi-direct drive (QDD), or proprioceptive actuation.} The MIT Cheetah
lineage~\cite{seok2015design,wensing2017proprioceptive,katz2019minicheetah} took the opposite route:
a large-diameter, low-$K_v$ motor behind a \emph{small} gear ratio, typically 4:1 to 10:1, so that
the transmission is nearly transparent in both directions. Three properties follow. Torque can be
inferred from motor current with no torque sensor at all, which is why the family is called
proprioceptive. Reflected inertia is low, so an impact backdrives the motor rather than shattering a
gear train. And force bandwidth is set by the current loop, which is kilohertz. The costs are
honest ones: torque density is lower than a high-ratio gearbox, so the machine is heavier for a given
torque, and since torque is current and current is heat, the binding limit becomes thermal rather
than mechanical — a constraint this thesis meets directly (\cref{sec:speed-ceiling-corrected}).

The consequence for DASH-01 is worth stating in the background chapter rather than discovering in the
results. The robot's actuators are QDD-class, chosen for exactly the reasons above and vindicated as
the Pareto-optimal choice in their class (\cref{sec:hw-motor-critique}) — but they are commanded
through the vendor's \emph{position} loop, which \cref{sec:plant-id-drive} measures at 0.8\,Hz. A
position loop of that bandwidth wrapped around a transparent transmission discards the single
property the transmission was chosen for. It also removes two entire rows of
\cref{tab:bg-approaches} from the menu: MPC and torque-mode RL both require that a commanded force
reach the ground, and neither is available through a 0.8\,Hz position servo. That is the mechanism
behind the recommendation in \cref{sec:future} that torque-mode control be treated as a day-one
architectural requirement rather than an optimisation.

\subsection{Control approaches: where this thesis sits}
\label{sec:bg-control}

Classical stacks decompose the problem: a gait or footstep planner, a whole-body or centroidal
controller, and joint-level tracking. They are transparent and analysable but demand accurate models
and high-bandwidth torque control. Learned controllers collapse the stack into a policy trained in
simulation, trading analysability for robustness to the messiness of contact — at the price of a
sim-to-real gap that must be closed with modelling fidelity and domain randomisation. This thesis
ends up using both: RL for the gait, and classical structure (reflexes, a clocked generator,
capture-point priors) exactly where feedback must not be blind (\cref{sec:rl-reflexes}). The scripted
baseline of \cref{sec:scripted-baseline} is the classical counterfactual, built on the same plant to
answer ``was the learning ever the bottleneck?''

\subsubsection{Two kinds of gait prior}
\label{sec:bg-prior}

``Learned controllers need no gait prior'' is a common and unhelpful summary, and this thesis is a
poor advertisement for it: the architecture that finally worked here
(\cref{sec:rl-cpg}) is a Fourier-series central pattern generator with hand-wired reflexes, which
is a great deal of prior. The useful distinction is not \emph{how much} prior but \emph{which kind},
and it is a distinction about the reachable set of gaits rather than about the amount of engineering
involved.

A \textbf{shape prior} fixes the trajectory and exposes its timing. Raibert's leg~\cite{raibert1986},
a fixed limit cycle in the classical CPG tradition~\cite{ijspeert2008}, a reference motion clip
tracked by imitation~\cite{peng2018deepmimic}, and any clocked foot-path generator all belong here:
the controller chooses phase, frequency, amplitude, when to reset on contact, but the \emph{shape}
of the motion is authored in advance. Its decisive property is that tuning does not enlarge what the
machine can do. However carefully the gains are set, the reachable set of gaits is a low-dimensional
family around the authored one, and if the authored one is wrong for the morphology, no amount of
tuning reaches a better one.

A \textbf{structural prior} constrains the \emph{class} of trajectories and leaves the member
unspecified. The Fourier action space of \cref{sec:rl-cpg} asserts only that the gait is periodic,
smooth, bounded, and mirrored left/right; which periodic function is a free parameter, and the
reachable set grows monotonically with the harmonic count $N$, approaching every periodic gait the
workspace admits. PMTG~\cite{pmtg2018} and CPG-RL~\cite{cpgrl2022} sit here too. The prior is doing
real work — it is the reason the problem is trainable at all, where the prior-free torque
parameterisation of \cref{sec:rl-torque} never converged — but it is doing work on the
\emph{search} rather than on the \emph{answer}.

The distinction is worth this much space because DASH-01 is precisely the case that separates them.
A shape prior is safe when the morphology has a natural template to copy: an animal with roughly
these proportions can be filmed, and the observed gait is a defensible starting shape. This robot
has no such template. Hardware calibration established that \textbf{the cam is a full crank},
sweeping $\sim$245$^\circ$ against a CAD-guessed $\pm 86^\circ$, with the 4-bar assembling at every
angle (\cref{sec:hw-experiments}); the transmission ratio it produces varies strongly with posture
(\cref{sec:hw-force-map}); and no animal offers a gait for that. The evidence that the wider search
space was genuinely used, rather than merely available, is the cadence result: reinforcement
learning repeatedly converged on a high-cadence solution, and the independent first-principles
feasibility sweep of \cref{sec:speed-ceiling} located the unconstrained optimum at the same
12.4\,steps/s from physics alone, with no learning in it. A learned gait found the plant's own
optimum on a morphology for which no template exists, and an independent analysis confirmed the
location.

Two qualifications keep this honest. First, \textbf{periodicity alone is demonstrably not
sufficient} on this plant. The literature constraint reported in \cref{sec:bg-rl-loco} — no
published system runs on per-cycle-only gait updates — was reproduced here by experiment: an
ablation that removed the per-step residual channel, leaving a pure oscillator, produced stability
without locomotion — 15\,m of a 72\,m course at 0.25\,m/s (\cref{sec:cpg-ab}). The working policy
class is periodic \emph{plus} an aperiodic intra-cycle correction; the structural prior is an
exploration scaffold, not the policy. Second, \textbf{the prior did not disappear, it moved}, and
where it moved is informative: the sagittal pitch reflex is hand-tuned rather than learned
(\cref{sec:rl-reflexes}), placed deliberately at the one channel where feedback must not be blind.
The honest description of the design axis is a dial running from ``which trajectory'' to ``which
class of trajectories, plus explicit structure only where the plant demands it'' — not a binary
between prior and no prior.

\subsubsection{What learning should buy in stability margin}
\label{sec:bg-margin}

The theoretical case for a learned controller on an unstable plant is a case about margin. A
hand-designed controller is a local object: gains are tuned around an operating point, the
structure encodes an assumed regime, and the region of attraction is whatever that construction
happens to yield. A policy trained over a distribution of states and plants is, in the limit, an
approximation to a globally optimal feedback law over that distribution — and, crucially, the
hand-designed controller lies \emph{inside} the policy class, so with an exact plant and unbounded
training the learned optimum can only match or exceed it.

That argument is correct and its premises are load-bearing, which is why
\cref{sec:scripted-baseline} finds the opposite on real numbers: a 21-gain scripted controller
beats the learned reference on the constrained plant and fails identically to it on the free one.
The premises that fail are the exact plant, the unbounded training, and — least obviously and most
importantly — the assumption that a margin exists to be won at all. The measured breakdown is given
in \cref{sec:margin-analysis}; the conclusion it supports is that the margin advantage of learning
scales with how much margin the plant has to give, which is a statement about machines rather than
about methods and is the reason this thesis ends in a mechanical recommendation.

\subsection{RL for locomotion: the lineage of this architecture}
\label{sec:bg-rl-loco}

Within the learned-control family of \cref{sec:bg-rl}, the relevant lineage for this work is narrower:
the family of \emph{periodically parameterised} action spaces, in which the policy does not emit
joint targets directly but shapes a periodic generator that does. Policies-Modulating-Trajectory-Generators
(PMTG)~\cite{pmtg2018} established the pattern, oscillator-based CPG-RL~\cite{cpgrl2022} instantiated
it with coupled phase oscillators in the tradition of~\cite{ijspeert2008}, and the clock and
phase-reward methods of Siekmann et al.~\cite{siekmann2021} carried Cassie to the 100\,m Guinness
record~\cite{crowley2024cassie}. The appeal is exactly the structural prior of \cref{sec:bg-prior}:
the network no longer has to discover that locomotion is periodic, only which periodic signal it
wants.

A literature review conducted mid-project (\cref{sec:rl-cpg}) returned one unambiguous constraint
that reshaped the architecture here: \textbf{no published system achieves running with
per-cycle-only gait-parameter updates and no intra-cycle feedback channel} — every successful system
keeps a per-step path from observation to action. The same review supplied the phase-gated contact
rewards and efficiency-term designs adopted in \cref{sec:rl-cpg}. On the sim-to-real side, the
standard toolkit surveyed in \cref{sec:bg-rl} — domain randomisation with curricula, observation
history in place of privileged state, explicit sensor models — is exercised in
\cref{sec:sim2real-lineage}, where an ablation shows which of its components actually carried the
cost.

\subsection{Existing platforms, and where DASH-01 sits}
\label{sec:bg-platforms}

The bipeds that produce the results cited above fall into three groups, separated more by actuation
and budget than by control philosophy.

\textbf{Purpose-built research bipeds with series elasticity.} ATRIAS~\cite{hubicki2016atrias} and
its commercial descendants Cassie and Digit are built to be spring-mass machines: leaf springs in
series with the drives, a high-voltage bus supporting the joint speeds that running demands, and a
morphology deliberately unlike a human's. They hold the bipedal speed
record~\cite{crowley2024cassie}. They are also the product of a decade of institutional effort and
priced accordingly.

\textbf{Quasi-direct-drive machines in the MIT Cheetah
lineage}~\cite{seok2015design,wensing2017proprioceptive,katz2019minicheetah}. Predominantly
quadrupeds, but architecturally the most influential group, because the QDD-plus-torque-mode pattern
they established has been adopted by essentially every recent legged platform — including the
current generation of commercial humanoids, which are QDD, torque-capable, RL-driven, and have
brought a capable biped within reach of a single laboratory's budget for the first time. This is the
architecture \cref{sec:future} concludes DASH-01 should have adopted end to end.

\textbf{Hobby-class humanoids}, built from position-controlled smart servos. They are cheap and
numerous, and they are restricted to the static-walking regime of \cref{sec:bg-zmp} for a reason
that is now familiar: a servo closing its own position loop cannot deliver a commanded ground force,
so the dynamic families are unavailable regardless of the controller written above them.

DASH-01 sits deliberately between the second and third groups: QDD-class actuators and a
running-oriented morphology, on a student budget and a single-board computer. Its design choices —
a parallel four-bar knee that moves the knee actuator's mass proximally, a passive ankle spring
instead of a seventh and eighth motor, six motors rather than ten — are all bets that mass and cost
can be traded against capability in the directions that matter for speed. The sensitivity analysis
of \cref{tab:mass-sensitivity} confirms the first bet was the right instinct, at $-0.93$\,(m/s)/kg
for distal mass against $-0.11$ for the torso; \cref{sec:hw-force-map} shows the second was not.
What distinguishes the platform from its neighbours in the literature is not any of these choices
individually, but that every one of them has been \emph{measured} rather than assumed — which is
what makes the design recommendations of \cref{sec:future} evidence rather than opinion.
