\section{Background and literature review}

\label{sec:bg}

This chapter provides a brief context for bipedal locomotion and, more specifically, for the Reinforcement Learning framework used in the literature to address this problem.

\subsection{Definitions}

\label{sec:bg-defs}

First, a few definitions are provided, as the following terms will recur throughout this thesis.

\textbf{Underactuation} is the presence of more degrees of freedom than independently actuated degrees of freedom.

\textbf{Unilateral contact} means that a foot can push against the ground but cannot pull it, and that the resulting contact force must lie within the \textbf{friction cone}.

The \textbf{sim-to-real gap} is the performance mismatch observed when transferring a policy from a simulated plant to the physical robot. This mismatch generally arises because the real plant lies outside the distribution of plants and disturbances encountered during training.

In the context of this thesis, \textbf{failure} refers to the termination of an episode following body contact with the ground or leaving the measured workspace.

\textbf{Stability margin}, in the context of Reinforcement Learning, is not necessarily expressed as a classical analytical stability margin. In this thesis, robustness is instead assessed through quantities such as the number of surviving episodes and the variance across random seeds.

\textbf{Plant}, borrowed from control theory, refers to the physical system to be controlled, in this case the robot.

\subsection{Abbreviations}

\textbf{RL} : Reinforcement Learning

\textbf{CoM} : Centre of Mass

\subsection{Bipedal locomotion approach}

\label{sec:bg-hard}

Bipedal locomotion can be approached in different ways, depending in particular on the speed of motion and on the assumptions made about contact and dynamics.

The simplest case is a robot with flat feet walking on flat terrain at sufficiently low speed that inertial effects can be neglected. This was the approach used for WABOT-1 in 1973 \cite{waseda2026wabot1}.

In this case, maintaining stability can be reduced to keeping the projection of the CoM inside the support polygon formed by the feet.

As the robot moves faster, inertial effects can no longer be neglected. The relevant quantity is then not simply the projection of the CoM, but the Zero Moment Point (ZMP), which must remain within the support polygon. This approach was used, for example, in ASIMO \cite{whatimakethis2020asimo}.

Running introduces a flight phase, which is incompatible with the previous approaches that require at least one foot to remain in contact with the ground at all times.

To enable running, Raibert \cite{raibert1986} proposed a foot-placement method based on the following relation:

\begin{equation}
  x_f = \tfrac{1}{2} \cdot \dot{x} \cdot T_s + k \cdot (\dot{x} - \dot{x}_d)
  \label{eq:neutral-point}
\end{equation}

where $x_f$ is the fore-aft foot placement, measured from the hip at touchdown, in metres; $\dot{x}$ is the current forward speed in metres per second; $\dot{x}_d$ is the commanded forward speed, in metres per second; $T_s$ is the stance duration, in seconds; and $k$ is a velocity feedback gain, also expressed in seconds.

The first term corresponds to the neutral point, which produces no net fore-aft impulse. The second term provides velocity feedback: when the robot is moving faster than commanded, the foot is placed further forward, producing a decelerating effect during stance.

This lineage runs through systems such as BigDog \cite{raibert2008bigdog} and later Atlas.

The underlying idea is that the gait can be stabilised, to a significant extent, by placing the feet at the appropriate location and time.

This also makes it possible to build running robots with point-contact feet, rather than requiring the large flat feet used by earlier approaches.

The approach was subsequently refined by solving the foot-placement and whole-body motion problem online, leading to what is commonly formulated as a Model Predictive Control approach \cite{kuindersma2016atlas}.

This approach was central to the development of the early dynamic legged robots developed by Boston Dynamics.

These approaches rely on a sufficiently accurate model of the plant.

In practice, however, the plant parameters are only partially known and may vary with configuration, load, contact conditions, and hardware.

Classical model-based controllers are therefore designed around a particular model and possess a finite robustness margin with respect to modelling errors and disturbances. Beyond this margin, the controller may no longer be able to stabilise the system.

Reinforcement Learning provides an alternative way of obtaining this robustness. Rather than explicitly deriving a controller from a single plant model, RL trains a policy over a distribution of simulated plants. The policy can therefore be exposed to variations in plant parameters during training, for example through domain randomisation, with the objective of improving its robustness to model mismatch.

The problem is further complicated by the unilateral and saturating nature of ground contact. A foot can only push against the ground, not pull it, and the tangential component of the contact force is bounded by the friction cone. Exceeding this bound results in slipping.

A second complication is the timescale of the dynamics. A legged robot can, to a first approximation, be considered as an inverted pendulum with characteristic length $h$.

In the absence of corrective input, the characteristic timescale of the inverted pendulum is on the order of

\begin{equation}
\tau \sim \sqrt{\frac{h}{g}},
\end{equation}

which corresponds to approximately $300\,\mathrm{ms}$ for a characteristic length of $1\,\mathrm{m}$.

During running, however, contact with the ground may last only a few tens of milliseconds. Within this timescale, the robot must measure the state of the system, estimate the relevant dynamics, compute a corrective command, communicate it to the actuator, and physically produce the required motion.

This places significant requirements on sensing, computation, communication latency, and actuator bandwidth.

The robot considered in this thesis has a measured actuator bandwidth corresponding to a $2\,\mathrm{Hz}$ pole in servo mode, and up to $20\,\mathrm{Hz}$ in MIT mode depending on the position-loop gains.

This limitation will become relevant again when discussing the control architecture and the sim-to-real problem later in this thesis.

\subsection{Reinforcement learning for locomotion}

\label{sec:bg-rl-loco}

\subsubsection{Actuator control architecture}

Several actuator-level control architectures have been used in the literature. The main ones considered here are direct torque control, direct position control, and impedance control.

Direct torque control allows the policy to command the actuator torque directly, giving it the highest level of control authority.

Direct position control allows the policy to command a position target, which is then tracked by a low-level controller.

Impedance control allows the policy to command a desired mechanical behaviour through parameters such as position, stiffness, damping, and, depending on the implementation, torque-related terms.

The latter provides a compromise between control authority and the requirements imposed on the hardware in terms of latency and bandwidth.

\textbf{Direct torque control} provides the policy with the highest level of flexibility, but also imposes demanding communication and control-loop timing requirements.

In simulation, the policy can directly command joint torques at a high frequency \cite{hwangbo2019anymal}.

On the physical robot, however, closing such a fast loop requires the policy, communication bus, actuator controller, and state estimator to operate with sufficiently low latency.

In the present hardware architecture, this approach was not practical through the CAN communication bus at the required frequency.

\textbf{Direct position control} substantially reduces these requirements by delegating the low-level actuator dynamics to the motor controller.

Position targets passed to the joint controller are used by several learned locomotion approaches \cite{tan2018sim2real,rudin2021}.

Two variants were investigated in this thesis: conventional smart-servo position control and the MIT control mode with fixed compliance parameters \cite{katz2019minicheetah}.

Both allow the RL policy to operate at a lower frequency while the actuator controller handles the faster inner control loop.

\textbf{Impedance control} provides an intermediate level of control authority \cite{bogdanovic2020impedance}.

Instead of directly specifying actuator torque, or being restricted to position targets, the policy can influence the desired mechanical behaviour through position, stiffness, damping, and/or torque-related parameters.

This avoids the need to close the torque loop at high speed over the communication bus while providing the policy with greater freedom than pure position control.

It also exposes more of the actuator dynamics to the policy and can reduce the sim-to-real gap compared with a smart-servo interface when the frequency and parameters of the inner control loop are not fully exposed.

\subsubsection{Gait prior}

For the purpose of comparing these approaches, the literature can be organised along a second dimension: the amount and location of prior structure imposed on the gait.

A strong prior can be introduced through a \textbf{reference trajectory}.

In this case, the policy is primarily trained to track an authored motion. This approach was, for example, explored on the Cassie robot \cite{xie2019}.

This strongly restricts the reachable set of motions and can substantially simplify learning.

However, the quality of the resulting behaviour is bounded by the choice of reference trajectory: there is no guarantee that the selected trajectory is optimal for the particular robot morphology and dynamics.

A less restrictive prior can instead be introduced through a \textbf{trajectory generator} whose parameters are modulated by the policy \cite{pmtg2018}.

This preserves the assumption that locomotion should have a structured, periodic form while allowing the learned controller to search for a gait adapted to the plant.

Trajectory generators can themselves provide different levels of freedom over the gait parameters.

At the stricter end of this generator-based formulation, the policy only modifies a small number of \textbf{high-level gait parameters}, such as phase, frequency, stride length, or amplitude \cite{cpgrl2022}.

This is similar to the reference-trajectory approach in the sense that the policy operates within a predefined family of trajectories. However, instead of learning the low-level joint commands required to reproduce a reference gait, the policy learns the high-level parameters that generate it.

This provides a relatively small search space but limits the set of gaits that can be represented.

A more moderate prior allows the policy to modify the \textbf{complete trajectory}. Rather than predicting every trajectory sample independently, the possible trajectories can be represented using a low-dimensional basis, such as Fourier series \cite{shafii2010tfs} or Bézier curves.

The policy then modifies the coefficients of this representation.

This retains useful structural assumptions such as periodicity and smoothness, while allowing the shape of the gait itself to become a learned variable.

It is also possible to use \textbf{neither a trajectory generator nor an explicit gait-shape prior}.

In this case, the policy learns the complete joint trajectory directly \cite{heess2017emergence}.

Periodic locomotion can still be encouraged through reward shaping, but periodicity then becomes an emergent property rather than an explicit constraint.

Such an approach provides a larger reachable set, but considerably increases the exploration and learning problem. There is also no guarantee that the learned solution will converge to a periodic gait.

Finally, the prior, when present, can be introduced at different levels: through the \textbf{reward} \cite{siekmann2021}, through a \textbf{reference motion} \cite{peng2018deepmimic}, or through an authored \textbf{trajectory generator} \cite{pmtg2018,cpgrl2022}.

These choices determine how much of the gait is fixed and how much remains available to the policy at run time, at the expense of increasing or reducing the complexity of the learning problem.

\subsubsection{Curriculum design}

The third major design dimension is the curriculum used to train the policy.

To help the policy converge, the training environment can be modified to make the task easier at the beginning and gradually increase its difficulty as the policy improves.

The literature has explored a variety of curriculum-design choices, including the order in which different tasks are presented, the rate at which difficulty is increased, and the specific parameters that are modified to create the curriculum.

\textbf{Terrain and command curricula.} A common scheme starts every agent on flat ground with a narrow command range.

As its performance exceeds a predefined threshold, the agent is promoted to rougher terrain or a wider velocity range, and can be demoted otherwise \cite{rudin2021}.

Difficulty therefore tracks each agent's competence rather than following a fixed schedule.

This approach is well suited to massively parallel training, where each agent can be promoted or demoted independently.

\textbf{Automatic domain randomisation.} Rather than fixing the domain-randomisation range before training, the range can be widened automatically once the policy exceeds a performance threshold at its current extremes \cite{akkaya2019rubiks}.

The randomisation range itself becomes the curriculum parameter, expanding only as fast as the policy can tolerate.

This approach requires a reliable in-training performance signal.

A noisy signal can cause the range to grow faster than the policy can adapt, destabilising training.

Whether the curriculum targets terrain, command range, or randomisation range, an adaptive schedule removes the need to manually tune a progression rate.

In its place, the design requires a performance threshold and a metric with which to evaluate it.

This introduces a problem similar to that encountered in reward design: if the metric does not accurately represent the intended notion of competence, the agent may optimise the metric rather than the desired behaviour.

\subsection{Platforms, and where DASH-01 sits}
\label{sec:bg-platforms}

The bipeds that hold speed records are purpose-built spring-mass machines with institutional budgets. Cassie, which held the 100\,m record before this project started, runs on series-elastic legs~\cite{crowley2024cassie,xie2019}.

A second family descends from the MIT Mini Cheetah~\cite{katz2019minicheetah}. It combines low-cost quasi-direct-drive actuators, light legs and a commodity computer, with the drives commanded in torque mode.

A third family is the hobby-class humanoid built from position-controlled smart servos. The servo interface hides the actuator dynamics from the controller, and these machines remain confined to quasi-static walking.

DASH-01 stands closest to the Mini Cheetah family in principle and in cost structure: quasi-direct-drive actuators, light legs and a commodity computer. It differs in the command interface, since its drives are commanded through their internal position loop rather than in torque. \Cref{sec:plant-id-drive} measures the cost of that difference.

\subsection{The gap}
\label{sec:bg-gap}

The learned-locomotion results above share a hardware assumption: the platform offers high-bandwidth torque or impedance control at the joint~\cite{hwangbo2019anymal,katz2019minicheetah,xie2019}.

How much of this methodology survives on a low-cost plant, commanded through a position-servo interface of limited bandwidth, is not documented.

This thesis explores that gap on DASH-01. \Cref{sec:robot} therefore starts by finishing and measuring the plant, so that the training and its results are judged against the machine as built rather than as designed.
