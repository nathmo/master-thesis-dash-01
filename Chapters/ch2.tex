\section{Background and State of the Art}

\subsection{What makes bipedal locomotion hard}
\label{sec:bg-hard}

A legged robot is not a robot arm with feet. An arm is bolted to the world, so the floor reacts
every joint torque the arm produces through its base. A legged robot touches the world only
through intermittent, unilateral contacts. Three consequences follow, and they organise this
chapter.

\textbf{It is underactuated.} A biped with six leg motors has twelve degrees of freedom. The extra
six belong to the body, which translates along three axes and rotates about three more with no
motor attached to any of them. Those six \emph{base} degrees of freedom can be influenced only
indirectly, through the ground. A flight phase has no ground, so the centre of mass follows a
ballistic arc that no actuation can alter. The robot can only choose the posture it will land in.
This thesis makes the point structurally. It releases the base degrees of freedom one at a time as
a training curriculum (\cref{sec:rl-cpg}), and the milestone at which the robot fails names the
degree of freedom it cannot control.

\textbf{The control inputs are unilateral and saturating.} A foot can push on the ground but not
pull. It can push only within a friction cone, or it slips. The point where that push applies is
the \emph{centre of pressure}, and it can move only within the contact patch. For a point foot the
patch is a point, so the centre of pressure cannot move at all. That leaves
\emph{where to put the next foot} as the principal input, and it is not continuous. It is chosen once per
step, and the leg's reachable workspace bounds it. A controller therefore holds a small number of
discrete, bounded opportunities to act rather than continuous authority.

\textbf{The timescales are short.} An inverted pendulum of leg length $h$ diverges with time
constant $\sqrt{h/g}$, which is about 310\,ms for this robot. Ground contacts in running last tens
of milliseconds. Any correction must therefore be planned, commanded and \emph{mechanically
executed} inside a window of a few tenths of a second. Actuator bandwidth is consequently a
first-class constraint, not an implementation detail. This project measured that constraint the
hard way: the drive's position loop is a 0.8\,Hz pole with $\tau = 199$\,ms, against the 310\,ms
divergence (\cref{sec:plant-id-drive}).

Running adds one thing to walking. In walking the centre of mass \emph{vaults} over a stiff
support leg and rises at mid-stance. In running the centre of mass \emph{bounces}: it falls at
mid-stance as the leg compresses, rises again as the leg rebounds, and passes through an aerial
phase with both feet off the ground. The distinction is not cosmetic. It changes which reduced
model applies, which is the subject of \cref{sec:bg-slip}. It is also why frameworks built for
walking do not extend to running when the commanded speed is simply raised.

The full dynamics are intractable to reason about directly, so the field works with \emph{reduced
models}: the smallest system that reproduces the behaviour of interest. Three such models appear
repeatedly in this thesis. The \textbf{inverted pendulum}, a point mass on a rigid massless leg,
models walking and gives the divergence time above. The \textbf{spring-loaded inverted pendulum}
(SLIP), a point mass on a springy leg, models running. The \textbf{capture
point}~\cite{pratt2006capture} is the location at which a foot must land to bring the inverted
pendulum exactly to rest, and is therefore the natural target for a foot-placement controller. It
underlies both the hand-wired pitch reflex of \cref{sec:rl-reflexes} and the scripted baseline of
\cref{sec:scripted-baseline}. These toy models stay load-bearing on a real robot, and not as a
convenient simplification: the free-topple time of the plain inverted pendulum turns out to
\emph{be} the failure signature this thesis spends a chapter diagnosing (\cref{tab:freefall}).

\subsection{A short history: six answers to one question}
\label{sec:bg-history}

The history of walking robots is more legible when it is read as a sequence of answers to a single
question: \textbf{where does the stability come from?} Every family below answers that question
differently, and every answer buys something and costs something. The six families are not a
progression from worse to better. All six are in active use, and the choice among them is governed
less by fashion than by what the hardware can physically do, which is the subject of
\cref{sec:bg-actuation}.

\subsubsection{Stability from geometry: static walking and the zero-moment point}
\label{sec:bg-zmp}

The first walking robots avoided the dynamics rather than solving them. WABOT-1 was built at Waseda
University in 1973 and is generally credited as the first full-scale anthropomorphic walker. It
moved slowly enough that its own momentum could be neglected~\cite{lim2007waseda}. It was, in
effect, a statue that occasionally shifted its weight. Stability meant keeping the vertical
projection of the centre of mass inside the \emph{support polygon}, the convex hull of the points
touching the ground. A robot that satisfies that condition at every instant cannot tip, by a purely
geometric argument that needs no model of the dynamics.

The dynamic generalisation is the \textbf{zero-moment point} (ZMP). Vukobratovi\'c and Juricic
introduced it in 1969~\cite{vukobratovic1969} and later surveyed it
themselves~\cite{vukobratovic2004}. The ZMP is the point on the ground about which the horizontal
components of the ground reaction moment vanish. Equivalently, it is the point at which gravity and
all inertial forces can be replaced by a single vertical force. While the ZMP stays strictly inside
the support polygon, the foot does not rotate about its edge and the contact behaves as though the
robot were bolted to the floor. One scalar condition thus converts the balance problem into a
geometric constraint that a planner can check.

The framework carried the field for three decades. Honda's P2 and its descendant
ASIMO~\cite{hirai1998honda} made it famous. Kajita's 3D linear inverted pendulum
model~\cite{kajita2001lipm} and ZMP preview control~\cite{kajita2003preview} made it cheap. Both
reduce the robot to a point mass constrained to a plane of constant height, then solve for a
centre-of-mass trajectory whose ZMP tracks a planned footstep sequence. That problem is small
enough to solve online on 1990s computers.

\emph{What it buys.} Transparency and a checkable guarantee. The condition is a single inequality
and needs no contact model beyond the geometry of the foot. The foot is never permitted to rotate,
so the approach tolerates a fairly inaccurate dynamic model. It is safe, and for a long time it was
the only approach that worked.

\emph{What it costs.} The guarantee is bought by never leaving the regime in which it holds. The
support polygon \emph{is} the foot, so the robot needs large flat feet and flat ground. Holding the
centre of mass at constant height demands a permanently bent-knee posture, which is why ASIMO-class
walkers show a cost of transport near 1.6 against roughly 0.2 for a walking human. Toe-off and
heel-strike are the foot rotations that make human walking efficient, and the framework forbids
both by construction, because both place the ZMP on the boundary. Most decisively for this thesis,
ZMP is a \emph{walking} framework. Running contains an interval with no support polygon at all, so
the central condition is not merely violated but undefined.

\subsubsection{Stability from the mechanism: passive dynamics}
\label{sec:bg-passive}

The opposite extreme arrived with McGeer's passive dynamic walkers~\cite{mcgeer1990}. These are a
pair of legs with no motors, no sensors and no computer, and they walk stably down a shallow ramp
on gravity alone. The gait is a \emph{limit cycle}: a closed orbit in state space that the robot
returns to on its own after a small disturbance. That orbit is a property of the linkage's geometry
and mass distribution, not of any controller. Collins, Ruina, Tedrake and Wisse~\cite{collins2005}
later added the minimum actuation needed to walk on the level, an ankle push-off replacing the
slope. They measured a cost of transport of about 0.20, comparable to a human and an order of
magnitude better than the ZMP walkers of the same era.

\emph{What it buys.} Efficiency that no other approach has matched, and a conceptual result that
outlives the hardware: \textbf{morphology is control}. A well-proportioned leg performs most of the
stabilisation for free. The actuators then fight nothing, because the commanded motion is the
motion the mechanism already wanted.

\emph{What it costs.} Authority. The basin of attraction is tiny, and a centimetre of unexpected
terrain ends the gait. Speed and heading are essentially not commandable, and each walker is tuned
to a single operating point. The result is stability without controllability.

The lesson that transfers is the one this thesis re-derives from the opposite direction. A
mechanism that carries the gait needs only a weak controller. A mechanism that fights the gait
cannot be rescued by any controller. \Cref{sec:hw-force-map} and \cref{sec:speed-ceiling} make that
statement quantitative for DASH-01.

\subsubsection{Stability from elasticity: spring-mass templates}
\label{sec:bg-slip}

Biomechanics supplied the model that walking frameworks lack. Blickhan~\cite{blickhan1989} showed
that one model describes running, hopping and trotting across animals spanning orders of magnitude
in body mass. That model is the \emph{spring-loaded inverted pendulum}: a point mass on a massless
linear spring. The leg compresses on touchdown, stores the impact energy elastically, and returns
it at push-off. Full and Koditschek~\cite{full1999templates} named such a model a \emph{template},
meaning the simplest system that exhibits the behaviour, with the physical animal or robot as its
\emph{anchor}. They argued that good legged design consists of building an anchor that behaves like
its template.

The engineering consequence is direct. A running robot wants a real spring in its leg. A large part
of the controller's job is then to hold the mechanism near a spring-mass limit cycle, rather than to
compute a trajectory from scratch. Hurst's ATRIAS~\cite{hubicki2016atrias} is the most literal
instantiation: a biped built to \emph{be} a SLIP, with fibreglass leaf springs in series with its
drives. Cassie and Digit are its commercial descendants.

\emph{What it buys.} Correct physics for running, rather than a walking framework pushed past its
validity. The spring recovers impact energy. The spring also rejects high-frequency disturbances at a speed much faster than any feedback loop can react.

\emph{What it costs.} The spring rate is a \emph{design commitment}, chosen once, in hardware. It
sets the leg's natural frequency and therefore the narrow band of speeds at which the robot is
efficient, and software cannot retune it. Series elasticity also inserts a low-pass filter between
motor and ground, which caps force-control bandwidth. Both costs appear in this thesis in their
sharpest form. DASH-01's passive ankle spring is exactly such a commitment, and getting its
stiffness wrong is the central mechanical finding of the work
(\cref{sec:ankle-build,sec:hw-force-map}).

\subsubsection{Stability from foot placement: the hopping lineage}
\label{sec:bg-hopping}

Raibert's hopping robots~\cite{raibert1986} answered the question with the insight that reframed the
field. A running robot does not have to be balanced \emph{continuously}. It has to be balanced
\emph{once per step}, at the instant it commits to where the next foot will go. Control then
decomposes into three nearly independent loops. Thrust energy injected at bottom of stance sets the
hopping height. Foot placement relative to a neutral point sets the forward speed,
\begin{equation}
  x_f \;=\; \tfrac{1}{2}\,\dot x\,T_s \;+\; k\,(\dot x - \dot x_d),
  \label{eq:neutral-point}
\end{equation}
and hip torque applied \emph{during stance only} corrects body attitude. Stance is when friction
pins the foot, so the leg can serve as a reaction strut. The lineage runs from the 1984 one-legged hopper
through BigDog~\cite{raibert2008bigdog} to the Boston Dynamics Atlas. It remains the skeleton of
most classical running controllers, including the scripted baseline of
\cref{sec:scripted-baseline}, in which \cref{eq:neutral-point} appears directly.

\emph{What it buys.} Running, which is what ZMP cannot do. It needs no support polygon, so point
feet are permissible. It needs no accurate dynamic model, because the placement law carries
essentially one empirical gain. The three-loop decomposition is also simple enough to implement on
1980s hardware.

\emph{What it costs.} Three assumptions, of which DASH-01 violates
all three. And no proof: the hoppers demonstrably worked, but the approach offers no certificate
that they will.

\subsubsection{Stability from a provable limit cycle: hybrid zero dynamics}
\label{sec:bg-hzd}

Passive dynamics gives a limit cycle that cannot be commanded. Raibert gives commandable behaviour
that cannot be proved. Hybrid zero dynamics (HZD) is the synthesis. The controller does not track a
trajectory indexed by \emph{time}. It enforces \emph{virtual constraints} instead: relations among
the joint angles parameterised by the robot's own progress through the step. Enforcing those
constraints collapses the closed-loop dynamics onto a low-dimensional surface, the \emph{zero
dynamics}, whose remaining motion has a single degree of freedom. A Poincar\'e map can then certify
the orbital stability of the resulting gait. Westervelt, Grizzle and
Koditschek~\cite{westervelt2003hzd} established the framework, the accompanying
monograph~\cite{westervelt2007book} systematised it, and RABBIT~\cite{chevallereau2003rabbit}
demonstrated it on a physical underactuated biped with point feet.

The mechanism can be stated as a choice about what advances the phase, and three options span the
field. A \emph{clock} advances it at a rate fixed independently of the robot, so a step that runs
slow leaves the reference ahead of the body and the error compounds. A \emph{coupled oscillator}
advances it by its own dynamics while sensory feedback modulates that rate, so a swing leg that has
not yet reached its target holds the oscillator back until it does. That is the entrainment
mechanism of the central pattern generator literature~\cite{ijspeert2008}. A \emph{virtual
constraint} removes the oscillator and reads the phase from the configuration itself as
$\theta(q)$, usually the absolute angle of the stance leg, which sweeps once per step whatever the
step duration. HZD is the stiff limit of the second option: the phase keeps no dynamics of its own,
so the reference can neither lead nor lag the body. That is also why the zero dynamics is
two-dimensional, because the phase contributes no state.

\emph{What it buys.} The only genuine stability proof in this chapter, for an underactuated robot
with point feet, which is precisely the case ZMP cannot address. It also buys an idea that has
outlived its framework: indexing a gait by \emph{phase} rather than by time. That idea reappears in
essentially every modern learned locomotion architecture. This thesis's own generator sits in the
middle of that axis, with a coupling that is learned rather than authored: the gait phase $\varphi$
integrates a frequency the policy re-selects from the observation at every control step, rather
than a rate fixed in advance or a function of configuration (\cref{sec:rl-cpg}).

\emph{What it costs.} The proof is a proof about the \emph{model}. The gait itself comes from an
offline trajectory optimisation over that model, so the accuracy requirements are severe and the
guarantee degrades silently when the plant is wrong. This thesis met that failure mode in a
different guise (\cref{tab:plant-id}). Each gait is a separate optimisation, so speed changes,
turning and terrain must be handled by scheduling among precomputed cycles rather than emerging
from the controller. The stiff phase coupling also removes the oscillator's fallback. A central
pattern generator keeps producing a rhythm when feedback is absent or corrupted, whereas a phase
read as $\theta(q)$ is only as good as the estimate of $q$, and outside the modelled regime the
phase variable itself stops meaning anything.

\subsubsection{Stability from re-solving the problem: optimisation-based control}
\label{sec:bg-mpc}

By the late 2000s computers were fast enough to solve the control problem online, repeatedly.
Wieber~\cite{wieber2006mpc} recast ZMP walking as \emph{model-predictive control}. At each cycle
the controller solves a constrained optimisation over a short future horizon, applies only the
first input, discards the rest, and re-solves at the next cycle. The constraint set is written down
explicitly, so friction cones, torque bounds and kinematic limits become constraints rather than
hoped-for margins. Kuindersma et al.~\cite{kuindersma2016atlas} built the whole-body version that
ran Atlas at the DARPA Robotics Challenge. Di Carlo et al.~\cite{dicarlo2018mpc} produced the
convex formulation on MIT Cheetah 3 that now underlies most classical legged stacks.

\emph{What it buys.} Constraints as first-class objects. Genuine feedback at the level of the
trajectory rather than only at the joints. And the commandability that passive dynamics and HZD
lack: one controller walks at different speeds, turns, and recovers from pushes.

\emph{What it costs.} Three things. An accurate model, because the optimiser trusts it over the
whole horizon and the errors compound. A solver fast enough to close a loop, which constrains the
formulation to be convex or nearly so. And, the requirement that decides this thesis,
\textbf{high-bandwidth torque control}, because the output is a force command that must reach the
ground essentially unfiltered. Which foot contacts when is usually still prescribed by hand,
because optimising the contact schedule turns a continuous problem into a combinatorial one.

\subsubsection{Stability from experience: learned control}
\label{sec:bg-rl}

The most recent answer replaces the analytic chain with a function fitted in simulation. A policy,
typically a small neural network, is optimised by reinforcement learning to maximise a reward over
millions of simulated episodes. Planner, whole-body controller and joint tracker collapse into a
single evaluation. Heess et al.~\cite{heess2017emergence} showed that rich terrain and a simple
reward produce recognisable running gaits with no gait prior at all.
PPO~\cite{schulman2017ppo} became the workhorse algorithm, and fast simulators such as
MuJoCo~\cite{todorov2012mujoco} made the sample cost affordable.

Transfer to hardware is the hard part. The toolkit that solved it is worth naming, because this
thesis uses all of it. \emph{Domain randomisation}~\cite{tobin2017dr,tan2018sim2real} varies the
plant parameters every episode, so the policy is optimised over a \emph{set} of robots and cannot
overfit any single wrong one. \emph{Learned actuator models}~\cite{hwangbo2019anymal} replace the
idealised motor with a network identified from hardware data, and that step first carried RL onto a
real quadruped convincingly. \emph{Teacher--student
distillation}~\cite{lee2020terrain,miki2022perceptive} trains with privileged simulator state, then
distils into a policy that observes only what the robot can actually sense. On bipeds, Siekmann et
al.~\cite{siekmann2021} demonstrated the full range of gaits on Cassie through periodic reward
composition. Radosavovic et al.~\cite{radosavovic2024humanoid} carried the approach to a full
humanoid. Crowley et al.~\cite{crowley2024cassie} produced the current bipedal 100\,m record:
24.73\,s, or about 4.1\,m/s average. Motion priors from reference data are a parallel
strand~\cite{peng2018deepmimic}, relevant here as the archetype of a \emph{shape} prior
(\cref{sec:bg-prior}).

\emph{What it buys.} No analytic contact model is required. That matters, because contact is
exactly where analytic models are weakest and simulators are adequate. The policy is optimised over
a distribution of states and plants rather than around one operating point, which is the source of
its robustness claim (\cref{sec:bg-margin}). Inference takes microseconds on cheap hardware, so the
entire stack fits on a single-board computer.

\emph{What it costs.} No stability proof, and no diagnosis when it fails. The reward function
becomes the specification, and an optimiser satisfies the specification rather than the intent.
\Cref{sec:rl-position} and \cref{tab:negative} catalogue what that looks like in practice. The
sample cost is $10^8$ to $10^9$ environment steps, so each result is a multi-day cluster job. And
the whole result rests on the simulator, because every modelling error is silently baked into the
policy. \Cref{sec:plant-id} is this document's demonstration of that last point.

Two observations close the survey. First, the families are complementary rather than competing, and
most modern systems combine them. A typical modern system is a learned policy over a phase variable

\emph{transfer of the stability burden from the controller to the robot and to the model}. The
burden moves first from a geometric condition checked at runtime to a mechanism that is stable on
its own. It then moves to a simulator faithful enough that a policy trained in it survives reality. That
trend is what makes measurement, rather than control design, the critical path of a project like
this one.

\subsection{Actuation decides which control approach is available}
\label{sec:bg-actuation}

\Cref{tab:bg-approaches} ends each row with a hardware demand, and that column is not incidental. A
control approach is available only to a robot whose actuators can meet its requirement. The
actuation choice is therefore made \emph{before} the control choice, whether or not the designer
notices. Three families dominate.

\textbf{Hydraulic.} Raibert's hoppers, BigDog~\cite{raibert2008bigdog} and the Boston Dynamics
Atlas are hydraulic. The family offers high force density, high bandwidth, and a commanded
force that arrives essentially instantaneously. The cost is a pump, a reservoir, noise, leaks and a
floor on system size and budget that puts the family out of reach for most laboratories and all
students.

\textbf{Series elastic (SEA).} Pratt and Williamson~\cite{pratt1995sea} proposed inserting a spring
between gearbox and load deliberately. Force then becomes a \emph{position} measurement, namely the
spring's deflection, which is cheap and accurate to sense. The spring also protects the gear teeth
from impact and stores running energy in the SLIP sense of \cref{sec:bg-slip}. ATRIAS, Cassie and
Digit are the canonical bipeds~\cite{hubicki2016atrias}. The cost is that the spring is a low-pass
filter. The spring--load resonance caps force bandwidth, and the compliance adds states that must
themselves be controlled.

\textbf{Quasi-direct drive (QDD), or proprioceptive actuation.} The MIT Cheetah
lineage~\cite{seok2015design,wensing2017proprioceptive,katz2019minicheetah} took the opposite
route: a large-diameter, low-$K_v$ motor behind a \emph{small} gear ratio, typically 4:1 to 10:1,
so that the transmission is nearly transparent in both directions. Three properties follow. Torque
can be inferred from motor current with no torque sensor, which is why the family is called
proprioceptive. Reflected inertia is low, so an impact backdrives the motor instead of shattering a
gear train. And the current loop sets force bandwidth, which puts it in the kilohertz range. The
costs are honest ones. Torque density is lower than a high-ratio gearbox gives, so the robot is
heavier for a given torque. And torque is current, current is heat, so the binding limit becomes
thermal rather than mechanical. This thesis meets that limit directly
(\cref{sec:speed-ceiling-corrected}).

The consequence for DASH-01 belongs in the background chapter rather than in the results. The
robot's actuators are QDD-class, chosen for exactly the reasons above, and
\cref{sec:hw-motor-critique} vindicates them as the Pareto-optimal choice in their class. They are
nevertheless commanded through the vendor's \emph{position} loop, which \cref{sec:plant-id-drive}
measures at 0.8\,Hz. A position loop of that bandwidth wrapped around a transparent transmission
discards the single property the transmission was chosen for. It also removes two entire rows of
\cref{tab:bg-approaches} from the menu. MPC and torque-mode RL both require that a commanded force
reach the ground, and a 0.8\,Hz position servo delivers neither. That is the mechanism behind the
recommendation in \cref{sec:future}: treat torque-mode control as a day-one architectural
requirement rather than as an optimisation.

\subsection{Control approaches: where this thesis sits}
\label{sec:bg-control}

Classical stacks decompose the problem into a gait or footstep planner, a whole-body or centroidal
controller, and joint-level tracking. They are transparent and analysable, but they demand accurate
models and high-bandwidth torque control. Learned controllers collapse the same stack into a policy
trained in simulation. They trade analysability for robustness to the messiness of contact, and
they pay for it with a sim-to-real gap that modelling fidelity and domain randomisation must close.
This thesis uses both. RL supplies the gait, and classical structure (reflexes, a clocked
generator, capture-point priors) sits exactly where feedback must not be blind
(\cref{sec:rl-reflexes}). The scripted baseline of \cref{sec:scripted-baseline} is the classical
counterfactual, built on the same plant to answer one question: was the learning ever the
bottleneck?

\subsubsection{Two kinds of gait prior, on two axes}
\label{sec:bg-prior}

``Learned controllers need no gait prior'' is a common and unhelpful summary, and this thesis is a
poor advertisement for it. The architecture that finally worked here (\cref{sec:rl-cpg}) is a
Fourier-series central pattern generator with hand-wired reflexes, which is a great deal of prior.
The useful distinction is not \emph{how much} prior but \emph{which kind}, and on \emph{which axis}.

A \textbf{shape prior} fixes the trajectory and exposes its timing. Raibert's
leg~\cite{raibert1986}, a fixed limit cycle in the classical CPG tradition~\cite{ijspeert2008}, a
reference motion clip tracked by imitation~\cite{peng2018deepmimic} and any clocked foot-path
generator all belong here. The controller chooses phase, frequency, amplitude and the reset on
contact, but the \emph{shape} of the motion is authored in advance. The decisive property of a
shape prior is that tuning does not enlarge what the robot can do. However carefully the parameters
are set, the reachable set of gaits stays a low-dimensional family around the authored gait. If the
authored gait is wrong for the morphology, no amount of tuning reaches a better one.

A \textbf{structural prior} constrains the \emph{class} of trajectories and leaves the member
unspecified. The Fourier action space of \cref{sec:rl-cpg} asserts that the sagittal motion of a
leg is periodic, band-limited and bounded. Which periodic function it is remains a free parameter,
because the policy emits the coefficients themselves. The reachable set is every waveform
expressible in $N$ harmonics, and it grows with $N$ toward any periodic gait the workspace admits.
That growth is a property of the parameterisation rather than a measured result: $N = 3$ throughout
this work, and no sweep over $N$ was run. The prior still does real work, since it is the reason
the problem is trainable at all, where the prior-free torque parameterisation of
\cref{sec:rl-torque} never converged. But it works on the \emph{search} rather than on the
\emph{answer}.

\textbf{The class is well populated, and the basis is not the contribution.} A band-limited
periodic parameterisation of a gait predates this work by more than a decade, and the citation
trail is worth stating plainly before any claim is made on top of it. Truncated Fourier series
gait generators ran on RoboCup humanoids from 2009, with the coefficients fixed offline by
particle swarm optimisation~\cite{shafii2010tfs}. Yang et al.~\cite{yang2010tfs} combined the
same formulation with reinforcement learning, which adjusted the reference online while the
series supplied the shape. Rhythmic dynamic movement primitives~\cite{ijspeert2013dmp} carry a
Fourier-type basis over phase and are the standard statement of ``smooth cycle, shape left
free'', as distinct from the fixed limit cycle of~\cite{ijspeert2008}. Fourier Latent
Dynamics~\cite{fld2024} parameterises periodic and quasi-periodic motion in a continuously
parameterised Fourier latent and uses it as a structured representation for motion learning on
the MIT Humanoid. Wang et al.~\cite{wang2023fourier} plan a Fourier-series periodic gait offline
and hand the policy only the phase, and Ates and Morg\"ul~\cite{ates2025freqgait} learn a
frequency-domain gait generator from motion capture and wrap it in PPO.

The nearest neighbour is Castillo et al.~\cite{castillo2020hzdrl}, who put the \emph{coefficients}
of the trajectory basis into the action space directly. Their policy maps a reduced state to the
B\'ezier coefficients of the hybrid-zero-dynamics virtual
constraints~\cite{westervelt2003hzd,westervelt2007book}, plus the tracking gains, and the
reachable-set argument given above applies to their parameterisation unchanged. The basis differs,
B\'ezier against Fourier, which matters only in that a truncated Fourier series closes the cycle by
construction where a polynomial over phase closes it by boundary condition. \textbf{Emitting basis
coefficients as the action is therefore theirs, not this thesis's.} What is added here is narrower:
the mid-cycle rewrite is explicitly priced rather than free (\cref{sec:rl-cpg}), the gait channel
and the fast-feedback channel are separated and billed differently, and the choice of basis is
settled by a measured A/B against the oscillator family on identical plants and rewards
(\cref{sec:cpg-ab}) rather than by assertion. The taxonomy that follows is likewise a way of
reading published systems, not a new object.

\textbf{The distinction is per-axis, not per-system.} A legged controller carries two independent
decisions, and a system can take opposite positions on them. The \emph{intra-limb} axis is the
trajectory one leg follows through its own cycle. The \emph{inter-limb} axis is the phase
relationship between legs, which is what the word ``gait'' usually names. Filing a whole system
under a single label hides that split.

CPG-RL~\cite{cpgrl2022} is the clearest case. It places one amplitude-controlled phase oscillator
on each leg of a quadruped, and the policy emits an intrinsic amplitude $\mu_i$, an intrinsic
frequency $\omega_i$ and an orientation rate $\psi_i$ per leg, which is twelve numbers at 100\,Hz.
The foot target is reconstructed from the oscillator state in closed form,
\begin{equation}
  x_i = -d_{\text{step}}\,(r_i - 1)\cos\theta_i\cos\phi_i,
  \qquad
  z_i = -h + \begin{cases} g_c \sin\theta_i & \text{if } \sin\theta_i > 0,\\[2pt]
                           g_p \sin\theta_i & \text{otherwise,}\end{cases}
  \label{eq:cpgrl-foot}
\end{equation}
with the step length $d_{\text{step}}$ and the stance penetration $g_p$ fixed. On the intra-limb
axis that is a shape prior in the strict sense given above. One authored curve, an ellipse
flattened during stance, is scaled by $\mu_i$, retimed by $\omega_i$ and rotated in the ground
plane by $\phi_i$. No action the policy can emit changes the curve, and there is no coefficient and
no harmonic count to raise. On the inter-limb axis the same paper does the opposite. The coupling
weights are set to zero, $w_{ij} = 0$, deliberately, so that the four oscillators are independent
and the policy has to discover the phase relationship. Trot, walk and the transitions between them
are outcomes there rather than inputs.

DASH-01 takes the opposite position on both axes, which makes the two systems complements rather
than neighbours.

\textbf{What the mirror buys, and what it costs.} The left--right mirror of \cref{sec:rl-cpg} is a
shape prior on the inter-limb axis, by the test stated above: no coefficient setting produces an
asymmetric gait, so tuning cannot enlarge the reachable set in that direction. It was chosen for
two reasons that hold for this project. The task is a straight-line dash, in which an alternating
symmetric gait is the answer anyway. And the mirror halves the emitted gait specification from 28
coefficients to 14 while guaranteeing alternation by construction, which is worth real wall-clock
time at the throughput available here, about 2900 environment-steps per second on one node
(\cref{sec:rl-results}).

The cost was paid later. A mirror-symmetric gait can only travel in a straight line, so steering
had to be added afterwards as two explicit asymmetry channels rather than emerging on its own, and
the drivable-demonstrator lineage of \cref{sec:sim2real-lineage} could not warm-start from the
sprint milestones, because both the observation and the action widths changed. CPG-RL shows that
the alternative is available. Emitting the two legs independently, at 28 coefficients instead of
14, would leave DASH-01 free on both axes and let the policy discover antiphase for itself. The
price is a larger action space and an exploration problem that has to rediscover a relationship the
mirror supplies for nothing. That trade was not affordable on this compute budget, and it is the
first thing to revisit given a larger one.

\textbf{Why the intra-limb axis was the one worth freeing.} DASH-01 separates the two kinds of
prior precisely because it has no template to copy. A shape prior is safe when the morphology has
one. An animal with roughly these proportions can be filmed, and the observed gait is a defensible
starting shape. Hardware calibration established that \textbf{the cam is a full crank}: it sweeps
about $245^\circ$ against a CAD-guessed $\pm 86^\circ$, and the 4-bar assembles at every angle
(\cref{sec:hw-experiments}). The transmission ratio the 4-bar produces varies strongly with posture
(\cref{sec:hw-force-map}), and no animal offers a gait for that. The cadence result is the evidence
that the wider search space was genuinely used rather than merely available. Reinforcement learning
repeatedly converged on a high-cadence solution, and the independent first-principles feasibility
sweep of \cref{sec:speed-ceiling} located the unconstrained optimum at the same 12.4\,steps/s from
physics alone, with no learning in it. A learned gait found the plant's own optimum on a morphology
for which no template exists, and an independent analysis confirmed the location. No shape-prior
arm was ever trained on this robot, so that is evidence the freed axis was used, rather than a
measurement of what an authored foot path would have cost.

\textbf{Gait-shape optimality is a studied question, in a different literature.} The claim above is
not that the optimal shape of a gait is an open problem in general. It is answered, and answered
well, in trajectory optimisation. Srinivasan and Ruina~\cite{srinivasan2006} optimise a minimal
biped over an infinite family of gaits under an energetic cost and recover inverted-pendulum
walking at low speed, a bouncing run at high speed, and a pendular run in between, with no gait
authored in advance. Rosa et al.~\cite{rosa2023optimalgaits} generate continuous families of
energetically optimal gaits by continuation from passive dynamic walking gaits~\cite{mcgeer1990},
and the passive-walker lineage itself~\cite{collins2005} is an argument that the shape can be a
property of the mechanism rather than of the controller. What that literature answers is optimality
for a reduced-order model under an analytic cost. The gap this thesis works in is narrower and
should be stated as such: no reduced-order model of this transmission exists, the cost is a task
reward rather than cost of transport, and the plant is measured rather than assumed
(\cref{sec:hw-force-map}). The Fourier prior is therefore adopted because the optimal shape here
cannot be computed in advance, not because nobody has computed one anywhere.

Two qualifications keep this honest. First, \textbf{periodicity alone is demonstrably not
sufficient} on this plant. \Cref{sec:bg-rl-loco} reports the literature constraint that no
published system runs on per-cycle-only gait updates, and the generator A/B of \cref{sec:cpg-ab}
reproduced it here. The arm that removed the per-step residual channel from the \emph{oscillator}
generator produced stability without locomotion: it survived most of the episode but travelled
15\,m of a 72\,m course at 0.25\,m/s. No equivalent arm was run on the Fourier generator, so the
result constrains the family rather than this exact controller. The constraint is on update rate
rather than on residuals as such. CPG-RL carries no residual channel at all and satisfies it by
re-selecting the oscillator setpoints at 100\,Hz, while PMTG keeps a residual, which on an authored
trajectory generator is also the only channel that can leave the authored family. The working
policy class is periodic \emph{plus} an aperiodic intra-cycle path of some kind. The structural
prior is an exploration scaffold, not the policy.

Second, \textbf{the prior did not disappear, it moved}, and where it moved is informative. The
sagittal pitch reflex is hand-tuned rather than learned (\cref{sec:rl-reflexes}), and it sits
deliberately at the one channel where feedback must not be blind. Each axis is therefore a dial
rather than a binary between prior and no prior, and each runs from ``which trajectory'' to ``which
class of trajectories, plus explicit structure only where the plant demands it''.

\subsubsection{What learning should buy in stability margin}
\label{sec:bg-margin}

The theoretical case for a learned controller on an unstable plant is a case about margin. A
hand-designed controller is a local object. Its gains are tuned around an operating point, its
structure encodes an assumed regime, and its region of attraction is whatever that construction
happens to yield. A policy trained over a distribution of states and plants approximates, in the
limit, a globally optimal feedback law over that distribution. The hand-designed
controller lies \emph{inside} the policy class. With an exact plant and unbounded training, the
learned optimum can therefore only match or exceed it.

The argument is correct, and its premises are load-bearing. That is why
\cref{sec:scripted-baseline} finds the opposite on real numbers: a 21-gain scripted controller
beats the learned reference on the constrained plant, and fails identically to it on the free one.
Three premises fail. The plant is not exact. The training is not unbounded. And a margin does not
always exist to be won. \Cref{sec:margin-analysis} gives the measured breakdown. The conclusion it
supports is that the margin advantage of learning scales
with how much margin the plant has to give. That is a statement about robots rather than about
methods, and it is the reason this thesis ends in a mechanical recommendation.

\subsection{RL for locomotion: the lineage of this architecture}
\label{sec:bg-rl-loco}

Within the learned-control family of \cref{sec:bg-rl}, the lineage relevant to this work is
narrower. It is the family of \emph{periodically parameterised} action spaces, in which the policy
does not emit joint targets directly but shapes a periodic generator that does. Policies Modulating
Trajectory Generators (PMTG)~\cite{pmtg2018} established the pattern. Oscillator-based
CPG-RL~\cite{cpgrl2022} instantiated it with coupled phase oscillators in the tradition
of~\cite{ijspeert2008}. The clock and phase-reward methods of Siekmann et al.~\cite{siekmann2021}
carried Cassie to the 100\,m Guinness record~\cite{crowley2024cassie}. The appeal is exactly the
structural prior of \cref{sec:bg-prior}: the network no longer has to discover that locomotion is
periodic, only which periodic signal it wants.

\textbf{The periodicity prior is close to universal, and systems differ in where they put it.} It
is common to summarise learned locomotion as prior-free, and the summary does not survive contact
with the systems that work. There are four places to put the periodicity, and published work uses
all of them. It can go in the \emph{reward}, as in the phase-gated periodic reward composition of
Siekmann et al.~\cite{siekmann2021}, where the action space stays joint-level and the clock
supplies the structure. It can go in a \emph{reference}, as in imitation of a motion
clip~\cite{peng2018deepmimic} or a mocap-derived spectral prior~\cite{ates2025freqgait}. It can go
in an authored \emph{generator} that the policy modulates, as in PMTG~\cite{pmtg2018} and
CPG-RL~\cite{cpgrl2022}. Or it can go in the \emph{action space} itself, as in the basis
coefficients of Castillo et al.~\cite{castillo2020hzdrl} and of \cref{sec:rl-cpg}. Only the last
of these makes the shape of one cycle a decision variable at run time, and only the first leaves
the action space untouched. The useful comparison across this lineage is therefore placement
rather than presence, which is what \cref{tab:prior-axes} sorts on. DASH-01 sits in the
action-space column, and \cref{sec:rl-torque} records what happened on this plant when the prior
was placed nowhere at all.

A literature review conducted mid-project (\cref{sec:rl-cpg}) returned one unambiguous constraint,
and that constraint reshaped the architecture here. \textbf{No published system achieves running
with per-cycle-only gait-parameter updates and no intra-cycle feedback channel.} Every successful
system keeps a per-step path from observation to action. The same review supplied the phase-gated
contact rewards and the efficiency terms adopted in \cref{sec:rl-cpg}. On the sim-to-real side,
\cref{sec:sim2real-lineage} exercises the standard toolkit surveyed in \cref{sec:bg-rl}: domain
randomisation with curricula, observation history in place of privileged state, and explicit sensor
models. An ablation there shows which components actually carried the cost.

\subsection{Existing platforms, and where DASH-01 sits}
\label{sec:bg-platforms}

The bipeds that produce the results cited above fall into three groups, separated more by actuation
and budget than by control philosophy.

\textbf{Purpose-built research bipeds with series elasticity.} ATRIAS~\cite{hubicki2016atrias} and
its commercial descendants Cassie and Digit are built to be spring-mass systems. They carry leaf
springs in series with the drives, a high-voltage bus that supports the joint speeds running
demands, and a morphology deliberately unlike a human's. They hold the bipedal speed
record~\cite{crowley2024cassie}. They are also the product of a decade of institutional effort, and
are priced accordingly.

\textbf{Quasi-direct-drive robots in the MIT Cheetah
lineage}~\cite{seok2015design,wensing2017proprioceptive,katz2019minicheetah}. These are
predominantly quadrupeds, but the group is architecturally the most influential of the three.
Essentially every recent legged platform has adopted the pattern it established, namely QDD
actuation commanded in torque mode. That includes the current generation of commercial humanoids,
which are QDD, torque-capable and RL-driven, and which have brought a capable biped within reach of
a single laboratory's budget for the first time. \Cref{sec:future} concludes that DASH-01 should
have adopted this architecture end to end.

\textbf{Hobby-class humanoids}, built from position-controlled smart servos. They are cheap and
numerous, and they are restricted to the static-walking regime of \cref{sec:bg-zmp} for a reason
that is now familiar. A servo closing its own position loop cannot deliver a commanded ground
force, so the dynamic families stay unavailable whatever controller is written above them.

DASH-01 sits deliberately between the second and third groups: QDD-class actuators and a
running-oriented morphology, on a student budget and a single-board computer. Three design choices
define it. A parallel 4-bar knee moves the knee actuator's mass proximally. A passive ankle spring
replaces a seventh and eighth motor. Six motors do the work of ten. Each choice is a bet that mass
and cost can be traded against capability in the directions that matter for speed. The sensitivity
analysis of \cref{tab:mass-sensitivity} confirms the first bet as the right instinct, at
$-0.93$\,(m/s)/kg for distal mass against $-0.11$ for the torso. \Cref{sec:hw-force-map} shows the
second bet was not. What distinguishes this platform from its neighbours in the literature is not
any single choice. It is that every choice has been \emph{measured} rather than assumed, which is
what makes the design recommendations of \cref{sec:future} evidence rather than opinion.
