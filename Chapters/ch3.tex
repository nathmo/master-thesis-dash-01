\section{Development Methodology}

\subsection{Overall methodology}
\label{sec:overall-method}

The project inherited a partially designed mechanical platform and proceeded in the following
order, each stage gating the next:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{Modelling and hardware selection.} Re-assembly of the CAD, export to a simulable
        model (\cref{sec:modelling}), and selection of the non-mechanical hardware: compute,
        sensing, battery (\cref{sec:hw-selection}).
  \item \textbf{Model validation.} Workspace calibration on the physical robot, static
        validation, and — much later, with better instrumentation — full dynamic identification
        (\cref{sec:plant-id}). The lesson of the project in one sentence: this stage was done too
        late and too lightly at first, and every subsequent correction moved the plant in the
        pessimistic direction.
  \item \textbf{Capability analysis.} A first-principles gait-feasibility sweep on the validated
        model (\cref{sec:speed-ceiling}), so that learned results have a ceiling to be judged
        against.
  \item \textbf{Toy problem.} A single-motor inverted pendulum carrying the complete
        train--export--deploy loop (\cref{sec:toy-problem}) before committing the 12-DOF robot
        to the same pipeline.
  \item \textbf{RL policy development.} Three action-space generations, a reflex architecture, a
        base-DOF-release curriculum, and a generator A/B — trained on the EPFL Izar cluster
        (\cref{sec:rl}).
  \item \textbf{Deployment.} The 200\,Hz control stack, calibration and safety machinery, and the
        flight recorder on the robot itself (\cref{sec:deploy}), plus the measurement campaign
        that fed the corrections of \cref{sec:plant-id} back into every earlier stage.
\end{enumerate}

Two cross-cutting disciplines emerged as more important than any single stage: \emph{adversarial
review of anything that produces a number} (rewards, simulators, estimators and evaluators all
produced confident wrong answers before being caught — \cref{sec:sim-hygiene,sec:plant-id-sysid}),
and \emph{measurement before assumption} (three project-level decisions — the torque-control
abandonment, the 50\,Hz control rate, the ankle actuation question — were each made on assumed
numbers and each reversed or reframed by later measurement).

\subsection{Hardware selection}
\label{sec:hw-selection}

\subsubsection{Motor}
\label{sec:hw-motor}

\paragraph{The selection instrument.} Actuator datasheets are not comparable as published, so
the choice was made against a purpose-built component database and explorer rather than against a
spreadsheet of vendor headline figures.\footnote{\url{https://github.com/nathmo/actuatorReview}}
It holds 140 commercial integrated actuators, 121 motors, 121 gearboxes and 9 standalone drivers
as four joined tables, with every unit decomposed into its motor, gearbox and driver layer so that
a candidate can be \emph{composed} rather than only looked up. The modelling follows Lee et al.,
\emph{How to Model Brushless Electric Motors for the Design of Lightweight Robotic Systems}
(arXiv:2310.00080).

Two ideas from that work do the actual work of making a selection possible.

\textbf{The motor constant is the only portable figure of merit.} $K_m = K_t/\sqrt{R} =
\tau/\sqrt{P_{\text{copper}}}$ is invariant both to operating current and to rewinding (rewind
$n$ turns and $K_t \propto n$ while $R \propto n^2$), so unlike $K_t$ or $K_v$ it can be compared
across vendors, and it sets continuous torque through the thermal limit as $\tau_{\text{cont}} =
K_m\sqrt{\Delta T/R_{th}}$. Ranking candidates by $K_v$ — the intuitive move, since $K_v$ is what
sets top speed — compares winding choices rather than motors.

\textbf{Every electrical constant is meaningless without its reference frame.} A $K_t$, $K_v$ or
$R$ can be quoted phase, line-to-line, q-axis, bus or RMS, in wye or delta, and vendors each pick
a different one; the same physical motor differs by up to $\sqrt{3/2}$ or a factor of 3 depending
on the convention. The database therefore stores a raw value plus an explicit frame tag and
normalises on the fly, leaving under-determined conversions flagged rather than guessed. This is
not a pedantic point: \cref{sec:hw-motor-critique} shows it lands directly on this project's own
$K_t$ measurement.

$80\,\mathrm{rpm/V} \times 48\,\mathrm{V} / 39 = 10.3$\,rad/s at the joint; the AKE90-8 is KV35,

capability envelope: the capability analysis of \cref{sec:speed-ceiling} finds 87\,\% of feasible
gaits limited at the cam actuator's peak-power corner, which makes \emph{no-load speed}, not peak
torque, the axis a successor should buy. The low reduction ratios keep reflected inertia low
enough that the legs can be backdriven by hand — the property the teach-and-replay gait recording
of \cref{sec:hw-experiments} depends on.

The pattern in the table is the design lesson, not the winner. Peak mechanical power per kilogram
saturates around 450--670\,W/kg regardless of vendor or price; what varies is \emph{where on the
torque--speed line} a product places that power, and the reduction ratio is the knob. Strain-wave
and high-ratio planetary units buy torque density and positioning accuracy at a catastrophic cost
in speed — the eRob110i has a comparable peak torque to the AKE90-8, costs three times as much,
weighs 1.8 times as much, and delivers one twelfth the mass-specific power, because 50:1 puts its
no-load speed at 6.3\,rad/s. For an industrial arm that is the right trade. For a running leg it
is fatal, and it is the trade that most of the actuator market is built around.

\paragraph{The choice was correct, and correct for the stated reason.} This matters because it
would have been easy to buy the same part for the wrong reason. A selection made on
\emph{torque} density would have chosen the MyActuator RMD-X15 (129\,Nm/kg peak against the
AKE90-8's 121) and lost 49\,\% of joint speed; one made on \emph{rated} torque density would have
chosen from the database's own headline ranking — MAB MA-p-100-30 at 45.5\,Nm/kg continuous, or
the MyActuator EPS-RH-25-100 at 44.6 — and lost 54\,\% and 86\,\% of joint speed respectively.
That ranking is a trap for this application, and it is the first thing the tool shows you. The one metric that selects
the AKE90-8 is the product $\tau\omega$, and \cref{sec:speed-ceiling} independently confirms
$\tau\omega$ is what binds.

\paragraph{The coupled torque--speed curve, now that $R$ is known.} An earlier draft of this
section recorded that the simulation applies independent torque and speed caps but \emph{no
coupled torque--speed curve}, on the grounds that the phase resistance was unavailable. That was
wrong: the resistance is published, and the database ingested it — $R_{ll} = 0.164\,\Omega$ for
the AKE90-8 and $0.60\,\Omega$ for the AK60-39, in the same line-to-line frame as the quoted
$K_t$, which is the pairing that makes $K_m = K_t/\sqrt{R}$ exact without knowing the winding.
CubeMars' own published $K_m$ of 0.674 reproduces $K_t/\sqrt{R_{ll}}$ to five digits, which
cross-validates both numbers.

With $R$ in hand the envelope can be stated rather than bounded. Back-EMF plus $IR$ exhausts the
48\,V bus at $K_b\omega_m = 48 - (72 \times 0.164) = 36.2$\,V, i.e.\ a corner at
$36.2/0.272 = 133$\,rad/s at the motor, \textbf{16.6\,rad/s at the joint}. Below that corner the
actuator is current-limited and the torque cap is flat; above it torque falls linearly to zero at
22\,rad/s. Every operating point the capability analysis actually visits (the worst case is
64\,N$\cdot$m at 13.7\,rad/s) lies on the flat branch with roughly a factor of two in torque to
spare, so the constant force range in simulation is not merely defensible — it is correct, and
the omission previously flagged as ``optimistic in the fast-running regime'' is smaller than
feared. What remains genuinely unmodelled is the fall-off between 16.6 and 22\,rad/s, which is
now a five-line addition to the model rather than an unmeasurable unknown.

\subsubsection{What the actuator selection got wrong}
\label{sec:hw-motor-critique}

Three criticisms survive the vindication above, in increasing order of consequence.

\textbf{The abduction actuator was chosen on the wrong axis, harmlessly.} The AK60-39 ranks 10th
of 18 in its own class (45--130\,N$\cdot$m, under 1.1\,kg) on mass-specific power, at 246\,W/kg
against 472 for the CubeMars AK10-9. It was bought for torque density — 96\,Nm/kg peak, which is
genuinely near the top of that class — and abduction is not a propulsive axis, so the low speed
costs nothing measurable. The real cost is elsewhere and was not anticipated: 39:1 gives it
0.046\,kg$\cdot$m$^2$ of reflected armature inertia, more than double the sagittal pair's, and a
published 1.4\,N$\cdot$m of back-drive torque. The teach-and-replay workflow of
\cref{sec:hw-experiments} depends on backdriving the legs by hand, and the abduction axis is the
one that resists. A 16:1 part (the AKH70-16, 78\,N$\cdot$m, 0.88\,kg, EUR\,525) would have kept
the torque and cut the ratio-squared inertia amplification by $(39/16)^2 \approx 6$ for EUR\,132
more per side. This is a small, real, and cheap-to-fix error.

\textbf{The measured 85\,\% ``gearbox efficiency'' may not be gearbox efficiency.} The database's
central methodological warning applies with force to this project's own measurement. The
datasheet $K_t$ is tagged line-to-line; the drive reports a current over CAN whose reference
frame is documented nowhere and was never established. If the drive reports q-axis current, the
correct comparator is $K_t^q = \sqrt{3/2}\,K_t^{ll} = 0.333$, and the measured effective constant
is not 85\,\% of datasheet but 69\,\% — a number no planetary gearbox explains. If it reports bus
current the discrepancy moves the other way. A single-stage 8:1 planetary should run at 94--97\,\%,
so 85\,\% was always slightly suspicious and was accepted because it sat comfortably inside a
plausible range. The two hypotheses are not separable from the data already recorded, and the
experiment that separates them is trivial: command a known current into a stalled motor and
measure the DC bus current with a clamp meter. Until that is done, the peak torques of
\cref{tab:motors} carry an unquantified frame risk on top of the geometric uncertainty already
acknowledged in \cref{sec:plant-id}. \textbf{The correction, if it is one, is pessimistic.}

\textbf{The driver was the mistake, and it was a separable purchase.} The single largest
sim-to-real gap in this project is the drive's 0.8\,Hz proportional-only position loop with
unwritable gains (\cref{sec:plant-id-drive}). It is worth being precise about whose fault that is.
The database records the AKE90-8 as a bare quasi-direct-drive module — no encoder, no driver
board, sold separately — which means the drive electronics were an independent decision, not an
inseparable consequence of choosing the best motor in the field. Its driver table lists at least
two alternatives that were available at the time and would have removed the problem entirely: the
mjbots moteus-n1 (100\,A peak, 10--54\,V, 14.6\,g, EUR\,137, open-source firmware, integrated
absolute encoder) and the MAB MD80 v3.0 (80\,A peak, 48\,V or 60\,V variants, 15\,g, EUR\,220,
FDCAN at 8\,Mbit, with position, velocity, impedance and torque modes and writable gains). Either
would have cost \emph{less} than the bundled CubeMars electronics, exposed a torque mode with a
known loop, and — in the 60\,V MD80 case — raised the bus to 60\,V for a 25\,\% gain on the
no-load speed that \cref{sec:speed-ceiling} identifies as the binding axis.

The counter-argument is real and should not be waved away. A bare module plus a third-party driver
requires mounting and calibrating an encoder, commissioning firmware, and tuning a commutation
loop per joint — six times — before a single line of control code runs, on a one-person thesis
with a fixed end date. The integrated option was plug-and-play with a documented CAN protocol and
bought perhaps three weeks of schedule. That is a defensible trade against a nine-month project.

What is \emph{not} defensible is that the trade was made without asking the question. No vendor
publishes a servo-loop bandwidth, so it could not have been looked up — but it could have been
measured on a single unit in an afternoon with the swept-sine rig that eventually did measure it a
year later, and the cost of being wrong was not three weeks, it was every policy trained in the
project. The database captures peak torque, rated torque, speed, mass, price, IP rating and
communications, and \textbf{it has no column for control-loop bandwidth or gain writability},
because at the time of building it nobody knew to ask. That omission is the most useful thing this
project learned about buying actuators, and it is recorded here as the schema's first missing
field rather than as a datasheet the vendor should have written.

\paragraph{A flagged inconsistency for the corrected sweep.} The capability analysis of
\cref{sec:speed-ceiling} caps the cam actuator at $P \approx \tau_0\omega_0/4 \approx 935$\,W.
That expression is the maximum of a linear torque--speed line running from stall torque to no-load
speed, which is the envelope of a voltage-limited brushed motor. It is not the envelope of a
current-limited FOC drive, which is flat at $\tau_{\text{pk}}$ up to the 16.6\,rad/s corner
derived above and therefore peaks near $170 \times 16.6 \approx 2.8$\,kW; CubeMars' own
peak-power curve for the part is annotated 1500\,W. The burst figures of
\cref{tab:speed-ceiling} may therefore be \emph{pessimistic} by a factor between 1.6 and 3 —
the only correction in this entire chapter that would move in the optimistic direction. Two
cautions keep this from being good news. The thermally sustained figure is set by the
55\,N$\cdot$m continuous rating and does not move at all, and it is the sustained figure that
decides whether a record is reachable (\cref{sec:speed-ceiling-corrected}); and the 0.8\,Hz drive
bandwidth caps achievable cadence far below any of these power limits. The discrepancy is
recorded here rather than resolved because resolving it means re-running the sweep with a proper
envelope, which is listed among the outstanding items in \cref{sec:speed-ceiling-corrected}.

\paragraph{Cost, delivery and assembly.} For completeness, the non-performance constraints that
bounded the search. \emph{Cost}: six actuators at EUR\,424/EUR\,393 is EUR\,2482, roughly
half the hardware budget; the strain-wave alternatives in \cref{tab:actuator-field} would have
been EUR\,7000--12000 for strictly worse running performance, so price and performance pointed
the same way for once. \emph{Delivery}: CubeMars ships to Switzerland in two to three weeks
against quote-only lead times of two to three months for Maxon's HEJ series, and a slipped
actuator delivery is a slipped project — the database records list price and stock channel for
exactly this reason. \emph{Assembly}: the integrated units bolt to a face and expose a four-wire
CAN and power harness, against a bare module needing an encoder mount, a driver enclosure and
thermal path per joint; with 12 joints' worth of harness already in the base this was a real
consideration and it pulled the same way as the schedule argument above. \emph{Effort}: the
database itself cost about a week to build and populate across eight vendors, which is only
justifiable because it is reusable — it is published as a standalone tool, and its verdict on
this robot's actuators is reproducible by anyone who disagrees with it.

\subsubsection{Battery pack}
\label{sec:hw-battery}

The motor selection fixes the electrical requirement: six drives on a nominal 48\,V bus, with
short, violent current peaks — a sprint robot's duty cycle, not a marathon robot's. (Usain Bolt
needs under ten seconds for 100\,m; this robot would be delighted with thirty.) The pack is
therefore optimised for \emph{power} density rather than energy density.

\paragraph{The selection instrument.} As with the actuators, the choice was made against a
purpose-built database and explorer rather than a shortlist.\footnote{\url{https://github.com/nathmo/batteryReview}}
It holds roughly 430 distinct cell models, 54 BMS modules and 110 commercial packs, each row
carrying a source URL, and it synthesises packs from \texttt{cell $+$ BMS $+$ (nS, mP)} so that
``what I could build'' plots on the same axes as ``what I can buy''. Two conventions in it matter
for what follows.

\textbf{Current is a curve, not a number.} The direct analogue of the actuator database's
rated/peak/momentary torque tiers. Every current rating carries a duration stamp and falls into
one of three tiers: \emph{continuous} (thermal steady state), \emph{pulse at $t$} (thermal
transient, interpolated as $I(t) = \sqrt{I_{\text{cont}}^2 + (I_{\text{pulse}}^2 -
I_{\text{cont}}^2)\,t_{\text{pulse}}/t}$), and \emph{fault} (the protection trip, which is a
disconnection event and never usable power). A rating quoted without its duration cannot be
placed in any tier and is not a specification.

\textbf{Estimated values are marked as such.} Roughly half the cell rows carry at least one field
inferred rather than read off a datasheet, written \texttt{ESTIMATED<n>} so it still parses and
can be filtered out. Every figure quoted below comes from a datasheet-sourced row.

\paragraph{What the database says about the pack that was built.} The pack is \textbf{13S2P} of
\textbf{EVE INR21700-50PL} (48.1\,V nominal, 10\,Ah, 468\,Wh, 1.87\,kg of cells), with a BMS rated
200\,A continuous and a software peak cap at 250\,A.
% TODO(nathann): confirm BMS make/model if you want it named.
The database corrects the specification this was sized against, and the correction is not
cosmetic. The 50PL is a 5.0\,Ah cell at \textbf{50\,A continuous} and \textbf{180\,A for 2\,s};
the 125\,A figure this project sized the pack on is a marketed peak with \emph{no published
duration}, which is precisely the class of number the tier convention exists to reject. The pack's
real ratings are therefore those of \cref{tab:pack-tiers}.

Two consequences follow. First, the BMS is \emph{not} the binding element at continuous, as was
assumed — the cells are, at half the BMS's rating, so the pack is over-specified on the BMS and
under-specified on the cells. Second, the 250\,A software cap is a 2-second-tier number enforced
with no duration logic: a policy drawing 250\,A for thirty seconds runs the cells at 25\,C against
a 10\,C continuous rating, and nothing in the stack notices. That is a real safety gap and the fix
is cheap — an $I^2t$ accumulator in the control daemon rather than a scalar clamp.

Whether 4.8\,kW continuous suffices is the question the sizing should have asked. Six actuators at
the capability analysis's worst observed operating point draw a few kilowatts electrical, so the
pack is adequate — but with a margin near 1.2--1.6$\times$, not the 3$\times$ the 250\,A figure
implied.

\paragraph{Chemistry: right answer, wrong reasons.} The original rationale was that LTO and
high-rate LiPo are nominally more power-dense but neither could be sourced as bare cells from a
reputable supplier — LTO surfacing only on AliExpress, LiPo unavailable packaged at 48\,V — which
left high-rate 21700 NMC as the practical space. Having actually swept the supplier landscape,
both halves of that reasoning turn out to be wrong, and they fail in opposite directions.

\textbf{LTO: rejected for a bad reason, and the rejection was correct anyway.} EU-warehouse LTO
specialists do exist and were found (SeLian Energy and BatteryFinds both stock Yinlong cells in
Germany and Poland with published prices, and ELERIX and GWL sell into the EU from Czechia), so
the provenance objection does not hold. What kills LTO is the number nobody checked: it is
\emph{not} more power-dense on the axis that matters. The best LTO cell in the database is the
Toshiba 20\,Ah SCiB at 932\,W/kg and 93\,Wh/kg; the chosen NMC cell delivers 2500\,W/kg and
250\,Wh/kg. LTO's reputation for power comes from its C-rate, and C-rate is a ratio — its 2.3\,V
cell voltage and heavy steel construction throw the advantage away in absolute terms. A 48\,V LTO
pack needs 21S at 2.3\,V, and at 1.24\,kg per Yinlong cell that is a \textbf{26\,kg} battery on a
15\,kg robot. The 21S floor is what makes this inescapable: the lightest LTO cell in the database
is a 1.5\,Ah 18650 at 45\,g, and 21S of those carries only 76\,Wh, so reaching 480\,Wh at 48\,V
still costs 6.6\,kg. No LTO configuration in the database clears the requirement in under 6\,kg.
The chemistry was excluded on sourcing when it should have been excluded on mass, and
that matters because the sourcing argument would have collapsed under thirty seconds of scrutiny
while the mass argument is unanswerable.

\textbf{LiPo: rejected for a bad reason, and this one changes the answer.} High-rate LiPo is
sourceable as bare 1S cells, from \textbf{Swaytronic in Niederlenz} — a Swiss domestic supplier,
no import, no customs, no VAT handling, two-day delivery, which makes it the \emph{easiest}
supplier in the entire survey rather than the hardest. Its 1S cells are rated 25\,C continuous and
50\,C burst, and because they are 1S the ``no packaged 48\,V module'' objection dissolves: series
count is a free parameter. \Cref{tab:pack-alt} puts the two builds side by side.

The comparison is unflattering on the criterion the section itself declares. The stated design

the one that should have driven the choice, and on that tier the LiPo pack delivers 2.7 times the

\cref{tab:mass-sensitivity} prices it at $-0.11$\,(m/s)/kg, or about $-0.07$\,m/s — against an
actuator budget of EUR\,2482 the EUR\,463 is real but not decisive. On the project's own stated
optimisation criterion, this was the wrong pick.

Three honest counterweights, none of which was actually the reason at the time:
\emph{cycle life} — high-rate LiPo runs a few hundred cycles against 500--1000 for a 21700, and
this robot is a lab platform that will see thousands of trials; \emph{assembly} — 26 cylindrical
cells spot-weld into a rigid, self-supporting brick that carries load in the base, whereas 13
pouch cells need a compression fixture, a case, and tabs joined without cooking them, which is
more work and a worse failure mode; and \emph{lab safety} — high-rate LiPo attracts storage and
charging restrictions that a cylindrical NMC pack behind a certified BMS does not, and a battery
one cannot leave charging overnight in the lab has a schedule cost that no datasheet shows. Taken
together these are a genuine defence of the built pack. They are not the defence that was
recorded, and the difference between ``we chose NMC because LiPo was unsourceable'' and ``we chose
NMC because pouch cells are a fire-safety and assembly liability we did not want on a shared
bench'' is the difference between an error and a trade-off.

\paragraph{Build versus buy.} Building was necessary, and the database is what establishes it
rather than assumption. Of 110 commercial packs surveyed, the 48\,V space is almost entirely
stationary storage — 16S LFP rack and wall units from 5 to 32\,kWh weighing 29 to 370\,kg, useless
here — and e-bike packs. The closest commercial match to the requirement is a Swaytronic 48\,V
13S3P 10.35\,Ah at 497\,Wh, 2.2\,kg and EUR\,485, which is essentially the pack this project
built, at twice the cost and with no assembly labour. It fails on exactly one axis: as an e-bike
pack it is rated in the 40\,A class, roughly 2.5$\times$ short of the requirement. \textbf{Nothing
in the commercial 48\,V market is built for a sprint duty cycle}, which is the finding that
justifies building a pack at all, and it took a 110-row survey to state it rather than assume it.

\paragraph{Cost, delivery and assembly.} \emph{Cost}: EUR\,96 of cells plus roughly EUR\,90--165
for a 13S 200\,A smart BMS (JBD and JKBMS both list suitable units) plus nickel strip and holders
— call it EUR\,250 against EUR\,485 for the nearest commercial pack, which is a saving but not the
reason. \emph{Delivery}: the surveyed supplier space is stratified by border, not by price — a
Swiss supplier ships in two days, EU sellers in under a week with Swiss import VAT at the border,
China-direct via EU warehouses in one to three days but with loose cells frequently barred from
air freight, and US shops, which have the best transparency and MOQ of anyone, cannot legally air
freight loose lithium to Europe at all and were excluded on that basis alone. \emph{MOQ}: single
cells are available for 18650/21700 NMC, whereas LTO ships in six-cell matched sets and large-format
LFP in four-packs — the minimum order quantity, not the price, is what actually gates a prototyper
out of a chemistry. \emph{Assembly}: 26 spot welds of nickel strip is an afternoon with a lab
welder and yields a structural element; this is the constraint that most quietly favoured
cylindrical cells and it was never written down as a criterion.

\paragraph{Voltage sag is probably the largest unmeasured quantity on the robot.} One consequence
of the \emph{drive} selection only became visible once the CAN traffic was sniffed
(\cref{sec:deploy-can}): the motor controllers publish position, speed, current, temperature and
an error flag, but \textbf{no bus voltage}. Pack sag under a sprint is therefore not observable
from the drives at all, and would need external sensing on the Pi or the BMS. Until that exists,
voltage droop is modelled in simulation as a randomised within-episode torque derating
(\cref{sec:sim2real-lineage}) rather than as a measured quantity.

The database's internal-resistance model shows this is not a second-order effect, and connects it
to the actuator argument above. A pack's sag ceiling is $(V_{\text{nom}} - V_{\text{cutoff}})/R$,
and a 13S2P arrangement multiplies cell resistance by $13/2 = 6.5$. The 50PL row is one of those
that carries \emph{no} datasheet internal resistance — a gap in the data, flagged rather than
filled — but a high-drain 21700 sits around 12--15\,m$\Omega$, which puts the pack near
80--100\,m$\Omega$. At the 100\,A continuous tier that is 8--10\,V of sag; at the 184\,A
ten-second tier it is 15--18\,V, taking a nominally 48\,V bus down toward 30\,V.

If that estimate is even approximately right it is the most important unmeasured number on the
robot, because \cref{sec:speed-ceiling} establishes that no-load speed is the binding axis and
no-load speed is \emph{linear in bus voltage}. A 30\,\% sag is a 30\,\% loss of joint speed
arriving exactly when the policy is asking for speed, on a robot that cannot see it happen. It
also sharpens the LiPo comparison of \cref{tab:pack-alt} in the same direction: a 13S1P pack has
$13\times$ cell resistance against $6.5\times$, but from cells whose resistance is roughly an
order of magnitude lower, so its sag would be a fraction of this.

The figures in this paragraph are an inference from a typical cell resistance, not a measurement,
and they are presented as such. The point is not the number — it is that the cheapest remaining
experiment in the project (a voltmeter across the pack during a sprint, or one datasheet lookup
for the 50PL's DCIR) addresses a plant parameter with more leverage on top speed than most of what
\cref{sec:plant-id} did measure. That it went unmeasured is the same failure mode this chapter
documents everywhere else: the quantity nobody instrumented is the quantity that moved.

Two properties matter more for control than the noise figures do.

\textbf{Latency.} The path from a physical event to a published value is 10--20\,ms: DLPF group delay (5--10\,ms, datasheet, not verified on this part), output-rate quantisation (0--4.3\,ms), the I2C read (1.7\,ms) and the poll period (0--5\,ms). At a 5\,ms control period that is two to four whole control steps, which is a larger effect than the sensor noise and is modelled as such in simulation.

\textbf{The attitude filter dominates, not the sensor.} A 6-axis Madgwick filter with $\beta = 0.06$\,rad/s runs at the poll rate. Its accelerometer correction is rate-limited to roughly $3.4^\circ$/s regardless of the size of the error, so the published attitude is overwhelmingly gyro-driven and the accelerometer acts as a slow trim. Roll and pitch are gravity-referenced and absolute; yaw is pure gyro integration and drifts, so the gyro zero must be captured on a stationary robot each session. If a policy consumes fused attitude rather than raw rates, the simulation has to model this filter and not merely the sensor.

The magnetometer is deliberately not fused: it sits centimetres from two brushless motors and the battery, and its reading moves when they do.

\paragraph{A methodological trap worth recording.} Noise figures taken while the robot swayed on its test rig measure the sway, and nothing in the resulting number says so. That mistake produced a yaw-axis noise figure roughly $8\times$ too high, and a filter sweep in which a \emph{narrower} low-pass appeared to measure \emph{more} noise. Both results looked like plausible sensor data. The benchmark tool now gates every noise figure on a peak-to-peak motion check, and every published figure carries a provenance label distinguishing measured-at-rest, motion-independent, datasheet-unverified and uncharacterised. The uncharacterised list is published rather than guessed at: temperature dependence of the gyro bias, vibration rectification under footfall impact, accelerometer cross-axis sensitivity, and clipping behaviour at $\pm4$\,g during real impacts.

\paragraph{Getting the rate the sensor was configured for.} The IMU loop initially reached only 190--194\,Hz of a nominal 200. The missing ticks were not the IMU's: the air, pressure, light and ADC channels were being read inline on the same thread, and those drivers spend nearly all their time asleep waiting for a conversion. The humidity sensor alone sleeps 15\,ms, three whole control ticks. Moving the slow chips to a second thread, with a lock serialising only the bus transfers, brought the IMU to 199.5\,Hz and reduced the CAN daemon's late ticks from 11.5\,\% to 9.8\,\% as a side effect. The remaining slips are operating-system scheduling.
\subsection{Robot modelling}
\label{sec:modelling}

The robot was re-assembled in Autodesk Inventor (chosen for fluency), each limb exported as STEP
with its origin centred on its joint, then assembled in Fusion~360 and exported to URDF with a
plugin. The path was not smooth, and the detours are worth recording because they are the normal
experience of CAD-to-simulation work:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{Inertia matrices from the mechanical engineer's SolidWorks model arrived in the
        wrong orientation}, and after multiple attempts the SolidWorks URDF export never yielded
        a usable file. The working pipeline validates every CAD-supplied inertia tensor against
        its reference frame explicitly rather than trusting the export.
  \item \textbf{URDF cannot express this robot at all} (\cref{sec:simulation}): the parallel knee
        closes a kinematic loop, so the URDF is at best an open-tree approximation with a
        floating pushrod, and MJCF with an explicit \texttt{<equality><connect>} is the actual
        model. Both are exported and kept; only the MJCF is simulated.
  \item \textbf{Validation is a tool, not an inspection.} A dedicated tool discovers plausible
        inertia matrices from the physical robot (moving the legs in the air with the base fixed,
        inside the calibrated safe workspace) and compares them against the CAD values. The full
        dynamic identification this grew into is reported in \cref{sec:plant-id-sysid}; its
        bottom line for the model is that the CAD centres of mass \emph{survive} scrutiny (a free
        18-parameter fit explains at most 11--19\,\% of the observed gravity residual, so the CAD
        values were kept), while the link inertia \emph{tensors} remain CAD placeholders scaled
        by measured mass ratios — which is why the domain-randomisation range on inertia stays at
        $\pm$25\,\% while the mass ranges narrowed.
  \item \textbf{The kinematic model is consistent with the measured robot}: the MJCF closes the
        4-bar at 100\,\% of the backdriven-workspace poses recorded on the real machine
        (1383/1383 left, 1406/1406 right, residual $10^{-14}$) — a one-sided test (it would only
        catch a model band \emph{smaller} than reality), but a reassuring one.
  \item \textbf{The exported model is not left/right symmetric, and symmetrising it was tried and
        reverted.} An audit found 17 of 30 mirror-compared quantities off — a $1.2^\circ$
        rotation in one hip frame, sub-millimetre CoM offsets — which the near-singular 4-bar
        amplifies into $6.5^\circ$ of hip-roll difference in the standing keyframe. A tool that
        mirrors the model exactly (worst error $1.2^\circ \to 2\times10^{-5}$) was built, and
        \emph{not applied}: the crooked stance survives symmetrisation (the gravity settle
        bifurcates into a $\sim$7$^\circ$ lean from a symmetric start — the asymmetry only chose
        \emph{which way}), an unexplained regression in the pitch-reflex kick-arrest failed the
        smoke gate, and the deployment lineage had already chosen to \emph{randomise} the
        asymmetry rather than deny it (\cref{sec:sim2real-lineage}). The tool is kept; the
        premise — that the asymmetry was the bug — was measured to be wrong.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Simulation}
\label{sec:simulation}

Simulation is done in MuJoCo. The choice was forced more by the robot's topology than by preference.

\subsubsection{URDF cannot describe this robot}

The parallel knee closes a kinematic loop: the cam motor drives a pushrod whose distal end ties to the shin below the knee, so hip, cam, pushrod, shin and thigh form a planar 4-bar. URDF is a \emph{tree} format and has no way to express that closure. The exported URDF is therefore not a description of the robot but of a robot with a floating pushrod, and any simulator that consumes it directly will produce plausible and wrong dynamics.

MuJoCo's MJCF closes the loop with an explicit \texttt{<equality><connect>} constraint. Both an open-tree URDF and a closed MJCF are exported and kept; only the MJCF is used for physics. The practical consequence for control is significant and is discussed further in \cref{sec:rl-cpg}: cam and thigh are \emph{not} independent, the loop is assemblable over only a diagonal band of the nominal joint box, and commanding a target outside that band is not merely hard to track but physically impossible.

\subsubsection{Compute backend}

Physics runs on CPU throughout; there is no GPU physics backend in this stack. Training nonetheless runs measurably faster with the policy network on a GPU: 2900 environment-steps per second against 1530 on the same cluster node, a factor of 1.9. The cause is not matrix throughput, since the network is a two-layer MLP whose forward pass costs 117\,$\mu$s even on the robot's Raspberry Pi. It is core contention: PyTorch on CPU steals cores from the parallel MuJoCo workers, and moving the network off the CPU returns those cores to physics.

\subsubsection{How do you know a simulation result is real?}
\label{sec:sim-hygiene}

The same discipline that the reward function required (see \cref{sec:rl-position}, where the policy exploited every loophole it was offered) turns out to be necessary for the \emph{physics} as well. A simulation bug does not usually announce itself: it produces a clean, converged, entirely plausible learning curve for a robot that does not exist. Four examples, all found while setting up a single ankle study, each of which would have silently produced a wrong answer:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{Joint limits are not rigid by default.} MuJoCo's default limit softness let body weight push $3.4^\circ$ past the stop of a supposedly rigid strut. That is a rubber bar, not a rigid one, and it would have quietly given the rigid arm of the study a compliant ankle. Stiffening the constraint reduced the penetration to $0.08^\circ$.
  \item \textbf{A constraint that ships inactive is inactive.} The rigid arm re-settled its stance with a \emph{free} ankle, because the locking equalities are only switched on at episode reset. It was being initialised in a pose its episodes never visit, 268\,mm lower than intended.
  \item \textbf{A safety criterion measured in the wrong frame.} The workspace termination measures the toe in the \emph{base} frame, so it cannot distinguish a collapsed ankle from a folded 4-bar. One arm stood outside the threshold from its own reset pose, and every episode was terminated 0.1\,s in. The criterion had to be re-referenced per arm to its own settled stance.
  \item \textbf{A stale keyframe.} The shipped stance is an equilibrium of one particular ankle law. Arms with a softer ankle start off equilibrium and lurch at every reset.
\end{itemize}

A fifth class is environmental rather than physical: the local development machine ran MuJoCo 3.7.0 and the cluster 3.3.7, and a weld constraint that held on the newer solver \emph{gave way} on the older one, drifting 0.86\,rad over a 60-step rollout. The ``rigid'' arm was silently compliant on the cluster only. Anything solver-marginal has to be tuned against the older version.

The countermeasures are structural rather than heroic: a smoke-test suite that builds and briefly runs all 123 training presets before any long launch, self-tests inside the analysis tools that check analytic Jacobians against finite differences and loop closure against the model's own keyframe, and assertions that fail loudly when a plant does not match what a preset claims it is.

\subsubsection{Mass}

The weighed masses of \cref{tab:masses} were baked into the simulation model, replacing the CAD placeholders, with the inertia tensors scaled by the per-body mass ratio and the stance keyframe re-settled under the new load.

The decision worth recording is that this was \emph{not} made an experimental variable. An earlier arrangement kept both a CAD plant and a measured plant as selectable model files, and the effect was that every preset which had not explicitly opted in was still training on the 12.83\,kg robot, which was all of them but two. Splitting the plant in two had made the wrong one the default. There is one robot; it weighs 15.14\,kg; the model file says so.

The accepted cost is that warm-starting any new run from a pre-correction checkpoint is now a cross-plant transfer: those weights and their observation normalisation statistics were fitted to a robot 18\,\% lighter with a shin 2.6 times too light.

\subsubsection{Peak torque is what the gearbox delivers, not what the datasheet quotes}

The actuator force ranges in the model carried the datasheet peak torque, which is the torque at an ideal torque constant. The gearbox does not deliver it.

$K_t$ was measured by clamping a known 1.552\,kg mass to the shin and repeating a quasi-static sweep with and without it. The method matters: fitting the two runs \emph{together} with an indicator column makes the mass term's coefficient exactly $1/K_t$, because a known mass on a known kinematic lever arm involves no link centre of mass anywhere. That breaks a degeneracy which had defeated the identification until then, since $K_t$ and the gravity-model gain are otherwise perfectly confounded and only their product is observable.

The effective joint $K_t$ came out at 82--89\,\% of datasheet, consistent with a gearbox at roughly 85\,\% efficiency. Peak torque scales with $K_t$ at the same peak current, so the model was updated: 170\,$\rightarrow$\,144.5\,N$\cdot$m for the cam and thigh actuators (measured), and 72\,$\rightarrow$\,61.2\,N$\cdot$m for the hip-roll actuator. The second figure is the same efficiency \emph{transferred}, not measured, because no datasheet $K_t$ is published for that part to fit against; it is probably optimistic, since a 39:1 reduction needs more gear stages than an 8:1.

The remaining uncertainty is geometric rather than physical. The mass was clamped by hand, $K_t$ trades against its position at about 1.9\,\%/cm, and the perpendicular standoff of the clamp is known only to 5--10\,cm. Across that box $K_t$ spans 82--98\,\% of datasheet. Settling it needs the mass on the shin centreline at a measured distance, not more analysis of the existing data.

The simulation models these actuators as position servos with $k_p = 200$, $k_v = 5$, which is critically damped at roughly 13\,Hz. The real drive rolls off at 0.8\,Hz. That is a bandwidth mismatch of more than an order of magnitude, against a learnable gait frequency range whose top is 4\,Hz. At 2.5\,Hz the hardware executes 30\,\% of the commanded amplitude, $94^\circ$ late. \textbf{Every policy in \cref{tab:rl-results} assumes authority the hardware cannot deliver.}

Three consequences follow.

First, the un-executed part of a command \emph{is} the tracking error, and it tends to the full commanded amplitude as frequency rises. Any high-frequency content therefore caps the usable excitation amplitude at around $15^\circ$ whatever the workspace allows, which is why an early identification sweep tripped the safety limit six seconds in on the joint with the most room. Excitation amplitude now solves against travel, no-load speed and tracking error together.

Second, \textbf{inertia cannot be excited in position mode at all}. Obtaining real joint accelerations needs either negligible amplitude at high frequency or current-mode excitation. The quasi-static runs are the valuable data and always were.

Third, and most consequential for the project, the fix is architectural rather than a gain change: the board's cascaded position loop is proportional-only and nothing in the runtime can write its gains. The route the measurement points to is current-mode commands with the position PD closed on the Pi at 200\,Hz, and a torque-source actuator with a proper torque--speed envelope in simulation instead of a fictional stiff servo. \cref{sec:rl-torque} discusses why this partially reverses a decision taken at the start of the project.

Adapting an existing policy to the measured drive cannot be done by switching it on. Applying 0.8\,Hz in one step to a converged walking policy collapsed it from an episode length of 1448 and a return of $+511$ to 251 and $-78$, with nothing else changed. It is now ramped in as a curriculum, logarithmically in frequency, because the equivalent filter coefficient is strongly nonlinear in bandwidth and a linear-in-Hz ramp would do almost nothing for most of its length and then fall off a cliff.

\subsubsection{System identification: including what it could not resolve}
\label{sec:plant-id-sysid}

Beyond the individual parameters above, a full dynamic-parameter identification was attempted, and its limits are as informative as its results.

\paragraph{Method.} Inverse dynamics through the closed loop was rejected: the 4-bar is near-singular over part of the operating region and the resulting inverse blows up. Identification is instead a per-joint linear regression of measured current against a gravity-torque model, viscous and Coulomb friction, an inertia term and an intercept, with the loop closure differentiated out through a constrained Jacobian.

\paragraph{Four estimator bugs, each of which produced a confident wrong answer.} A missing sign convention on the current column made every $K_t$ come out negative. The joint mapping never mirrored the right leg, which put the reconstructed configuration outside the assemblable band and left a loop residual of 0.78\,m rather than $10^{-8}$; this reproduced identically on three separate recordings, so it was clearly not a bad capture. Pooling rows from joints that a given excitation run did not move, together with a missing intercept, each independently flipped the sign of $K_t$, because the drive's DC current offset landed in the gravity column. And the loop Jacobian was over-regularised: the problem is well conditioned in this pose region, and the damping alone inflated the fitted gravity gain by 20\,\%. With the first two fixed, the two legs agree to 5--6\,\% instead of disagreeing in sign, which is the cross-validation that says the fixes are right.

\paragraph{The rejected result.} With $K_t$ pinned by the known-mass test, the model still under-predicts gravity torque by a factor of 1.3 to 1.75. The obvious explanation is that the CAD centre-of-mass offsets are wrong. That hypothesis was tested and \textbf{rejected}: gravity torque is linear in each body's centre of mass, so it is a single linear solve, and with all 18 parameters free, no regularisation and no plausibility constraint, moving mass explains at most 11--19\,\% of the residual. Ten of the eighteen directions are pure null space, which is where physically absurd fitted offsets come from. The CAD centres of mass therefore stand. Per-body mass, load-dependent stiction and base tilt were tested and rejected on the same grounds. Friction of any origin was ruled out by a decisive test: friction opposes motion and therefore reverses sign with direction of travel, whereas fitting the gravity gain separately on up-strokes and down-strokes puts both on the same side of unity for all six joints. The residual is conservative, and no friction model can produce it.

What is left lies outside the span of every rigid-body inertial parameter, which points at the kinematic model rather than the mass distribution. That has not been resolved.

\paragraph{Why this is worth reporting.} An identification that returns a number is easy to over-trust. This one returns a measured, repeatable, per-joint gravity gain that can be applied as a correction or a randomisation range without being understood, and an explicit statement that the mass distribution is not the explanation. Knowing which parameters the experiment \emph{could not} resolve, and why, is what stops the next person fitting the same null space again.

Three results, all of which contradict assumptions made earlier in the project.

\textbf{The drives broadcast their state at exactly 200.0\,Hz, unprompted.} All six motors on both buses, period 5.00\,ms, p99 5.03\,ms. It is free-running and continues with the control software stopped, so it is not poll-triggered. The sensing ceiling is therefore exactly 200\,Hz and matches the control stack. A 50\,Hz loop would discard three of every four sensor frames.

\textbf{200\,Hz is clean and 50\,Hz is reproducibly worse.} The 50\,Hz row above is not frame loss, since the interface counters show nothing dropped; it is a scheduling artefact of the longer sleep combined with a drain three times more expensive because four times as many frames have accumulated. The case for dropping to 50\,Hz had been compute headroom, and compute is not the constraint: policy inference is 117\,$\mu$s mean and 144\,$\mu$s at the 99th percentile, 2.3\,\% of a 5\,ms budget.

\textbf{The CAN bus was never near its limit.} Six motors at command plus reply is about 26\,\% of one 1\,Mbit bus at 200\,Hz, across two buses.

The conclusion is to deploy at 200\,Hz, and there is no 50\,Hz fallback worth having. Note carefully that none of this helps with the real bottleneck: running the outer loop at 200\,Hz cannot compensate for an actuator that executes a quarter of the command $94^\circ$ late (\cref{sec:plant-id-drive}). What the measurement does establish is that the Pi has ample budget to close the position loop itself, which is the proposed fix.

\subsubsection{The CAN protocol, as measured}
\label{sec:deploy-can}

The protocol was sniffed on both live buses rather than read from documentation, and the difference mattered. Exactly two frame types exist per motor. Outbound is the command frame. Inbound is a single feedback frame at command byte \texttt{0x29}, at exactly 200\,Hz, using all eight bytes: position, speed, current, temperature and an error flag.

Absent are the VESC-style extended status frames that would report input voltage and duty cycle. The hypothesis that these drives are a VESC derivative with the extra status packets available is measurably false: the command byte is \texttt{0x29} rather than VESC's 9, so the vendor renumbered rather than inheriting. This closes the electrical route to $K_t$, which is why the known-mass test of \cref{sec:plant-id} was necessary, and it means pack sag cannot be observed from the drives.

Worth recording as a process note: nothing in the codebase decoded the command byte, because every consumer masked it off and keyed on the node id. A comment asserting the wrong command number sat in the repository unnoticed for months, in dead code. Assumptions that are never exercised are never falsified.

\subsection{Achievable gait: actuator and mass-distribution limits}
\label{sec:speed-ceiling}

The maximum running speed achievable by the robot was estimated independently of reinforcement learning using a first-principles analysis of the mechanical system. While RL demonstrates a lower bound on performance, this analysis provides an upper bound imposed by the robot's kinematics, dynamics, and actuator capabilities.

\subsubsection{Method}

The analysis was performed directly on the MuJoCo model (\texttt{training/model/dash01.xml}), ensuring consistency with the model used for policy training. Candidate gaits were parameterized by duty factor $D$, contact length $L_c$, ride height $h$, and cadence.

For each candidate gait, the corresponding stride length, flight time, swing duration, and ground reaction forces were computed using a simplified running model. Joint torques and velocities were then obtained through the leg Jacobian throughout the gait cycle.

A gait was considered feasible only if all joints remained within the admissible 4-bar linkage assembly region while respecting actuator torque, speed, and power limits. The parameter space was exhaustively explored to determine the maximum feasible running speed.

The implementation was validated against MuJoCo. Static equilibrium predictions agreed within 1\%, while the analytical Jacobian matched \texttt{mj\_jacGeom} with an error below $10^{-10}$.

At the practical optimum ($\leq6$ steps/s), the predicted gait has a duty factor of approximately 0.45, a stride length of 1.1--1.3\,m, a contact length of 0.25--0.30\,m, and peak vertical ground reaction forces of approximately 3.5 body weights (440\,N), with ground contact durations between 36 and 81\,ms.

Consequently, motor temperature must be experimentally validated before sustained hardware operation. On the corrected plant of \cref{sec:speed-ceiling-corrected} the margin disappears entirely.

The unconstrained optimum occurs at 12.4 steps/s, corresponding to the same high-cadence solution repeatedly discovered by reinforcement learning. This agreement confirms that the high-cadence behavior originates from the system dynamics rather than from a simulator artifact.

\subsubsection{Limiting factors}

Maximum speed is primarily limited by swing-leg actuator power rather than by kinematic reach.

The dominant constraint is the cam actuator during swing, which requires approximately 878\,W (64\,N$\cdot$m at 13.7\,rad/s), close to its theoretical maximum output of 935\,W. Consequently, increasing actuator no-load speed would improve achievable running speed more effectively than increasing peak torque.

Stance loading represents the second limiting factor. Peak ground reaction forces require approximately 167\,N$\cdot$m of the available 170\,N$\cdot$m, leaving minimal torque margin.

Kinematic reach is not the limiting factor. Although the leg can theoretically achieve a fore-aft workspace of 1.06\,m, only 0.20--0.35\,m is practically usable. Near the edge of the assembly region, the transmission ratio of the 4-bar mechanism deteriorates, causing joint velocities to increase rapidly and reducing achievable running speed.

Introducing a flight phase substantially improves performance. Reducing the duty factor from 0.85 to 0.45 increases the predicted speed from 4.6 to 6.9\,m/s while requiring only a 1.2\,cm vertical center-of-mass excursion. Lower duty factors require ground reaction forces exceeding 4.5 body weights and are therefore infeasible.

\subsubsection{Ankle stiffness}

A static load analysis independently reproduced the ankle stiffness threshold previously observed during reinforcement learning.

The passive ankle spring ($28.65\,\mathrm{N\,m/rad}$) supports only approximately half body weight before collapsing due to positive mechanical feedback, despite the actuators operating well below their torque limits. Increasing stiffness progressively improves load capacity, with approximately $350\,\mathrm{N\,m/rad}$ required to support two body weights and maintain stable stance.

The agreement between the analytical load model and the reinforcement learning experiments provides independent validation of this stiffness threshold. A separate static end-effector force map (\cref{sec:hw-force-map}) reproduces the same conclusion from a third direction.

\subsubsection{The corrected ceiling}
\label{sec:speed-ceiling-corrected}

The figures above were computed at 12.83\,kg with a 170\,N$\cdot$m peak torque. \cref{sec:plant-id} shows both to be wrong, and wrong in the direction that hurts most:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
  \item Peak torque falls from 170 to 144.5\,N$\cdot$m. Since 87\,\% of feasible gaits are limited at the cam actuator's peak-power corner, this is a direct hit on the binding constraint.
  \item Distal leg mass rises by roughly 0.70\,kg across both legs (the shin body from 222 to 573\,g), at a sensitivity of $-0.93$\,(m/s)/kg from \cref{tab:mass-sensitivity}.
\end{itemize}

Propagating both through the sensitivity figures gives approximately \textbf{2.7\,m/s credible burst and 1.3\,m/s thermally sustained}, against 3.7 and 1.8 on the CAD plant. This changes the headline comparison of the whole project. The learned policy's 2.6\,m/s is no longer comfortably inside the envelope; it sits essentially \emph{at} it, and roughly twice the sustained figure. For context, the bipedal 100\,m record is approximately 4\,m/s average, and a 100\,m dash is about 25\,s of continuous running against a continuous torque rating, so it is the \emph{sustained} number that decides whether a record is reachable.

The honest conclusion is that this chassis is a walking and jogging demonstrator with an unusually good measurement pedigree, not a record contender. The design implications for a successor fall directly out of the analysis above: bus voltage first, since speed scales linearly with no-load speed and not at all with peak torque, and distal mass second.

These corrected figures are a propagation of the sensitivity coefficients, not a re-run of the full feasibility sweep. The sweep should be repeated properly on the corrected plant before submission; the tooling to do so now exists in validated form.

\subsubsection{Limitations}

The analysis provides an optimistic upper bound on performance. It neglects torso attitude stabilization, lateral dynamics, impact losses, control bandwidth limitations, and the coupled actuator torque-speed envelope. The last of these is no longer a small omission: \cref{sec:plant-id-drive} measures the drive's position loop at 0.8\,Hz, so the assumption that a commanded joint trajectory is actually executed fails well below the cadences this analysis assumes.

Inertia tensors also remain approximate. The segment \emph{masses} are now weighed (\cref{tab:masses}) and the model's tensors were rescaled by the per-body mass ratio, but the tensor \emph{shapes} are still CAD, and the identification of \cref{sec:plant-id-sysid} was unable to resolve the mass distribution any further. Since \cref{tab:mass-sensitivity} shows the result is most sensitive to exactly that quantity, this is the largest remaining uncertainty in the analysis.

\subsection{Toy problem: one joint, end to end}
\label{sec:toy-problem}

Before committing a 12-DOF robot to an unproven pipeline, the complete chain — simulate, train,
export, deploy on embedded Linux, close a loop over CAN — was exercised once at minimum stakes: a
single-motor inverted pendulum.

The environment is a MuJoCo pendulum wrapped in a Gymnasium interface whose observation is
deliberately \emph{the hardware telemetry and nothing else}: $[\text{position (turns)},
\text{velocity (turns/s)}, \text{torque (N$\cdot$m)}]$, exactly as the drive reports it. The
policy outputs a torque command clipped to $\pm$1\,N$\cdot$m; the success criterion is reaching
the upright band within 5\,s and holding $\pm$0.1\,turn and $\pm$0.1\,turn/s to the end of a
20\,s episode. Training is SB3 PPO with a curriculum; the export bakes the observation
normalisation statistics \emph{into} the ONNX graph (a detail that returns as a hard requirement
in \cref{sec:rl-validation}); and the policy runs on a Raspberry Pi with \texttt{onnxruntime},
closing the loop on a moteus drive over CAN at 50\,Hz with a watchdog. The sim-to-real transfer
succeeded, and the exercise bought exactly what it was designed to buy:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
  \item the \textbf{train$\to$ONNX$\to$embedded-inference path} existed and was debugged a year
        before the biped needed it (transport initialisation on the CAN adapter was already
        non-trivial and cost real iterations at this stage — cheap here, expensive later);
  \item the discipline of \textbf{building the observation from raw hardware telemetry} rather
        than simulator conveniences, which later reappears as the ``no privileged observations''
        rule of \cref{sec:sim2real-lineage};
  \item early evidence on the \textbf{compute question} of \cref{sec:compute-choice} — the
        BeagleBone-class board originally envisaged was rejected partly on the experience gained
        here.
\end{itemize}

The rig also survives as a candidate student lab exercise (TP), since it demonstrates the entire
RL-for-hardware loop with one motor and one afternoon of training.

\subsection{RL policy design and validation}
\label{sec:rl}

The control policy went through three distinct action-space designs over the project. This section documents all three, the reflex structure that ended up
wrapped around the final one, and the results obtained so far. Throughout, the learning algorithm
itself was held fixed : PPO from Stable-Baselines3, an actor--critic MLP, trained on CPU-parallel MuJoCo environments.
So the comparisons are about the \emph{action representation}, not about the optimiser.

\subsubsection{Attempt 1: direct joint torque}
\label{sec:rl-torque}

The first framework commanded joint torque directly: the policy output was mapped to
motor setpoints through a fixed torque scale on the actuators. This work was attemped with a simplifed bipedal robot as the one developed in this thesis was still in development.
This is the textbook formulation and the one most legged-locomotion papers use in simulation.

But, it was abandoned for two reasons:

\textbf{It did not converge.} The training history from that period is an unbroken sequence of
reward-shaping attempts : raising the fall penalty because the robot learned to belly-flop,
adjusting the alive bonus because it learned to dawdle, re-tuning the feet-air-time thresholds
because it learned to vibrate in place. After a few itteration of this whicked whack-a-mole exploit patching, It was realised that with a
raw torque action space the policy has to synthesise, from scratch and at every control step, both
the gravity compensation that keeps the robot standing \emph{and} the periodic structure that makes
it walk; the reward landscape in between is dominated by ways of falling over that tricked the reward into thinking it was doing well.

\textbf{More decisively, it was judged undeployable.} The CubeMars drives expose a servo mode with an
internal position/velocity loop running on the drive itself; commanding torque means bypassing that
loop and streaming current setpoints over CAN from Python on a Raspberry Pi. The budget was estimated
rather than measured, and the estimate was pessimistic on three counts: that the achievable loop rate
would be low and jittery, that the CAN bus would not carry the traffic needed to go faster, and that
a missed deadline in torque mode leaves the drive holding the last commanded torque blindly, which on
a loaded leg integrates into a runaway rather than a pause. Only the third of those is still true.
On top of it, the output-side torque constant of the actuator was not known, so the Newton-metres
being commanded would have been approximate in any case.

The two arguments pointed the same way: the policy class that most needs high control bandwidth and
an accurate actuator model appeared to be exactly the one for which we had neither. Torque control
was dropped, and position control adopted in its place.

\paragraph{The measurement campaign partially reverses this decision.} It is worth being explicit
that this is one of the clearest results of the project, because it is a case of instrumentation
overturning a design decision that had already been acted on. Every quantitative premise of the
bandwidth argument was measured a year later (\cref{sec:plant-id-timing}), and both of the first two
were wrong, in the same direction:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
  \item The Raspberry Pi holds a full 200\,Hz control loop --- drain both CAN buses, run the policy
  forward, write six commands --- at a jitter of 0.141\,ms standard deviation and \textbf{half its
  time budget spare}, with the web interface competing for CPU. 400\,Hz also holds. Policy inference
  is 117\,$\mu$s, 2.3\,\% of a 5\,ms budget. Far from being marginal, 50\,Hz is reproducibly
  \emph{worse} than 200\,Hz.
  \item The CAN bus is at 26\,\% of one 1\,Mbit line, and the drives already broadcast their state at
  exactly 200.0\,Hz whether asked to or not.
\end{itemize}

So the compute and the bus, the two things assumed to be the constraint, have ample headroom. The
actual bottleneck turned out to be \emph{inside the servo mode that was kept instead}: the drive's
own position loop rolls off at 0.8\,Hz (\cref{sec:plant-id-drive}), which is the single largest
sim-to-real gap in the project.

The current direction is therefore \texttt{SET\_CURRENT} commands with the position PD closed on the
Pi at 200\,Hz, and a torque-source actuator with a real torque--speed envelope in simulation. That is
the Cheetah-lineage standard, the drives support it, and the timing measurement shows the Pi has the
budget to do it. It is not a full return to Attempt 1: the policy would still emit position targets,
and the PD loop closing them would simply move from the drive to the Pi, where its bandwidth is known
and its gains are writable. What is reversed is the premise, not the action space.

\subsubsection{Attempt 2: joint position targets (PD)}
\label{sec:rl-position}

The second design made the action six \emph{position} targets, one per actuated joint, at
50\,Hz, with the knee and ankle following passively through the parallel linkage and the
ankle spring. In simulation these are MuJoCo \texttt{<position>} actuators with gains and force
ranges taken from the datasheets of the cubemars actuator (\texttt{kp}~120, \texttt{kv}~4, 72\,N$\cdot$m at the
hip-roll; \texttt{kp}~200, \texttt{kv}~5, 170\,N$\cdot$m at cam and thigh). On hardware
this maps one-to-one onto what the drives actually accept.

\paragraph{What worked.} Standing and balancing converged after a fair ammount of effort and reward shapping. After switching to a point-foot contact
model only a 2.5\,cm sphere at each toe collides, so the policy cannot rest a
shin on the ground and damage the real robot, the policy held full-length episodes on two toe
points. This also surfaced a floor-penetration exploit: with only the toe sphere
colliding, the non-colliding foot and shin meshes could pass \emph{through} the floor, and the
first apparently-perfect balancing policy was largely exploiting that by slipping trough a stable equilibrium by lowering its CoM below the floor. It was fixed by terminating
on both an upper-hemisphere toe contact (the sphere sank) and any foot/shin vertex below the floor
plane.

\paragraph{What failed: skating.} Walking did not. After 32~million steps the policy reached
a perfectly healthy-looking reward curve and was \emph{sliding}, not stepping. Both feet grounded on 57--66\,\%
of steps, duty factor 0.74--0.83, median ``swing'' duration of one control step (contact
chatter at 5--10 touchdowns per second), in-contact toe slip of 0.11--0.24\,m/s
accounting for essentially all of its translation and a mean forward velocity of
$-0.09$\,m/s under a $+0.5$\,m/s command.

Three separate causes were found and fixed: there was no price on foot motion while in contact (a
slip penalty was added, with a grounded predicate that survives micro-hop and ghost-drag dodges);
the air-time term could only ever punish stepping, since credit was paid at touchdown and never
lifting scored zero (it was made one-sided and capped, and paired with a per-foot stance-time cap
and a swing-clearance term to give PPO an immediate gradient toward lifting a foot); and the
contact physics silently made skating free, because \texttt{condim=3} disables torsional and
rolling friction, turning the toe spheres into ball casters. Two reward-plumbing bugs compounded
it: observation normalisation was also normalising the \emph{reward}, dividing the carefully
balanced raw scale by a return standard deviation of $\approx 110$ so that a 200-point fall
penalty reached the optimiser as $-1.8$; and the entropy coefficient on a clipped Gaussian policy
was effectively free entropy, so the action standard deviation ratcheted up until training
behaviour and deterministic evaluation were different controllers.

The lasting methodological lesson is the first one: \textbf{the system will happily exploit any loophole in the reward function}.

\paragraph{Why we moved on.} Even after the fixes, the fundamental problem remained: a per-step
position policy must rediscover the periodicity of a gait from scratch at every control step.
Nothing in the representation says that locomotion is cyclic. Compounding this, I updated the the six motor masses (7.1\,kg, more than half
the robot) and re-settling the stance keyframe under gravity moved the plant enough that the
earlier standing policy no longer transferred, and the walking policy that had been slowly
improving stopped improving at all. Rather than re-tune the same representation against a heavier
plant, the representation was changed.

\subsubsection{Attempt 3: a central pattern generator tuned by the policy}
\label{sec:rl-cpg}

The third design puts the periodicity into the action space itself. Instead of emitting joint
targets, the policy emits the \emph{coefficients of a Fourier series} describing one gait cycle,
and the environment reconstructs the joint targets from those coefficients at the current gait
phase. This is a central pattern generator whose shape is learned rather than hand-authored: the
network no longer has to invent periodicity, only to choose which periodic signal it wants.
The work of the biorob was discovered after this idea emerged.

\paragraph{Formulation.} For the two sagittal joints of the left leg (cam and thigh, the pair that
drives the parallel 4-bar and therefore propulsion), the deviation from the nominal stance posture
at gait phase $\varphi$ is
\begin{equation}
  \Delta q_j(\varphi) \;=\; A_j \left[ w_0 a_0 + \sum_{k=1}^{N} w_k \bigl( a_k \cos k\varphi + b_k \sin k\varphi \bigr) \right],
  \qquad w_k \propto \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{k}},
  \label{eq:fourier}
\end{equation}
with $N = 3$ harmonics, so seven coefficients per joint. The harmonic weights $w_k$ are normalised
to sum to one, which bounds the waveform, and the $1/\sqrt{k}$ schedule was chosen deliberately
over the more obvious $1/k$: a $1/k$ schedule biases the series so strongly toward its fundamental
that every gait it can express is near-sinusoidal, i.e. walking, whereas the push-off of a running
stride is impulsive and needs high-harmonic authority. The result is clipped to $\pm A_j$ of the
nominal posture a \emph{relative} bound, because the nominal is always a validated, assemblable
posture and the coupled 4-bar is only assemblable over a diagonal band of the joint box.

The phase $\varphi$ advances at a frequency that is itself part of the action, so the policy
chooses its own cadence. The right leg is not emitted at all: it is constructed as the mirror of
the left, half a cycle out of phase, $\Delta q_{R,j}(\varphi) = -\Delta q_{L,j}(\varphi + \pi)$.
This halves the action dimension, guarantees an alternating gait by construction, and matches the
convention already used by the hand-authored gait demo. It has one consequence worth flagging,
because it cost us time later: a mirror-symmetric gait can only travel in a straight line, so
steering had to be added afterwards as two explicit asymmetry channels (differential stride
amplitude and differential stance width) rather than emerging on its own.

\paragraph{First version: per-cycle updates, and why it was not enough.} The initial implementation
made one environment step equal one full gait cycle: the policy chose a gait, the environment ran
it to completion (roughly 30 decisions per episode instead of 1000), and the macro
reward was the mean of the per-step rewards. This was immediately better than the per-step PD
policy --- a hand-crafted elliptical foot path pushed through the same reconstruction walked at
0.357\,m/s open-loop, versus the PD policy's 0.01\,m/s, and
the first trained policy walked properly at $\approx$ 0.5\,m/s with no skating
signature at all. The structural prior worked exactly as intended.

But it was slow, and it fell over as soon as the pitch degree of freedom was released. A literature
review at that point returned an unambiguous verdict: \textbf{no published system achieves running
with per-cycle-only parameter updates and no intra-cycle feedback.} Every successful periodic
parameterisation keeps a per-step channel --- PMTG modulates the trajectory generator and adds
per-step residual corrections; CPG-RL re-selects the oscillator parameters at 100\,Hz. The
same review found a genuine semi-Markov bug in our formulation: taking the \emph{mean} of the
per-step rewards over a macro-step, with a fixed discount per macro-step, makes the total return
scale with cadence and makes the effective horizon vary between 4.8\,s and
28\,s depending on the gait frequency the policy happened to choose. It also flagged two
things the observation was missing --- base velocity (the quantity the reward maximises was not
observable at all) and distance-to-the-line (so the value function was partially observed and
braking was unplannable).

\paragraph{Final version: per-step re-parameterisation plus residuals.} The current stack keeps the
Fourier gait but re-emits the entire specification at \emph{every} control step, and adds a
per-step correction channel on top. The 24-dimensional action is:

\begin{table}[H]
\centering
\begin{tabular}{clp{9.2cm}}
\toprule
\textbf{dims} & \textbf{channel} & \textbf{role} \\
\midrule
14 & Fourier coefficients & cam + thigh gait shape, \cref{eq:fourier}; mirrored to the right leg \\
1  & gait frequency & cadence, 0.5--4.0\,Hz; the phase integrates it \\
3  & abduction reflex gains & $k_p$, $k_d$, bias of the lateral balance reflex \\
6  & per-step residuals & direct corrections on the six PD targets, $\pm$0.08\,rad \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\caption{The per-step action. The first 18 dimensions are the gait \emph{specification}; the last
six are the fast-feedback channel.}
\label{tab:action}
\end{table}

Rewriting the gait specification mid-cycle is legal but priced: a penalty proportional to
$\sin^2(\varphi/2)$ bills changes to the specification, so a rewrite is free exactly at the cycle
boundary and most expensive mid-stride. The residuals are deliberately \emph{never} billed by it,
because they exist to be per-step --- a one-off stumble correction should not have to pay for
rewriting the whole gait. This gives a clean separation: the Fourier channel is the gait, the
residual channel is the correction, and a separate magnitude penalty keeps the residual from
quietly becoming the controller.

The observation is 55 values stacked over 5 frames (275 total): motor position, velocity and
torque; IMU gravity direction and gyro; base velocity; $\sin\varphi$ and $\cos\varphi$; a task
channel carrying the run/stop flag and the distance remaining; and the previous action.

\paragraph{Reward.} The sprint objective is a single dash --- stand at the line, run, stop. The
core insight, worth stating because it is not obvious, is that \textbf{a per-step speed reward
cannot price time}: integrating $w\,v_x$ over a fixed course gives $w \cdot \text{distance}$
regardless of pace, identical for a fast run and a slow one. What prices time is a \emph{constant
per-step clock cost}, which integrates to $-w_t T$; and correspondingly the alive bonus must be
exactly zero, because paying per second of existence rewards dawdling. The break-even pace is the
ratio of the two, 0.25\,m/s.

Around that core sit the anti-skate terms inherited from the PD era, a Siekmann-style phase-gated
contact schedule (each foot pays for ground contact during its expected swing window, using the
action's own gait clock; as the stance ratio ramps from 0.65 down to 0.42 the two swing
windows begin to overlap, so a flight phase is explicitly demanded --- this is the one term that
asks for running rather than fast walking), and efficiency terms on torque, motor velocity and
positive mechanical power, ramped in over 60~million steps so they cannot smother gait
emergence early.

Four reward exploits were found by adversarial review of the reward code and fixed before the
long runs: a stop-phase income that was net-positive per step and therefore farmable forever by
twitching the hold timer; a one-sided velocity clip that made shuttling back and forth in front of
the finish line strictly better than crossing it; and a cold-start suicide, where the sum of
always-on penalties for a standing robot ($\approx -2.2$ per step) made diving at $-100$
value-optimal for any policy that could not yet locomote --- fixed by flooring the per-step total,
not just the individual terms.

\paragraph{Curriculum over base degrees of freedom.} Rather than ask a fresh policy to solve
balance and locomotion simultaneously, the base is progressively released. The free joint was
replaced in the model by six explicit scalar joints ($x$, $y$, $z$, roll, pitch, yaw) plus six
equality constraints that can be activated individually, so any subset of the base degrees of
freedom can be rigidly railed at reset. Milestone \texttt{m1} frees only forward translation (with
height railed at a randomised ride height drawn from a measured lookup table), \texttt{m2} adds
height, \texttt{m3} pitch, \texttt{m4} lateral translation, \texttt{m5} roll, \texttt{m6} is the
real plant. Rigid equality constraints were chosen over clamping the state after each step
precisely because they produce the correct reaction wrench \emph{during} the step, so the leg
torques stay faithful. Critically, the observation and action dimensions do not change across
milestones, so every stage warm-starts from the previous one's checkpoint.

\subsubsection{Reflexes: what is learned and what is hard-wired}
\label{sec:rl-reflexes}

Not everything in the controller is a Fourier series, and the split is deliberate. A Fourier series
over gait phase is by construction \emph{feedforward}: it is a function of where we are in the
cycle, not of what the robot is doing. Balance is not a periodic problem, it is a feedback problem,
and encoding it as a phase-indexed trajectory would make it blind. Three feedback channels
therefore sit outside the CPG.

\paragraph{Lateral (abduction) reflex --- learned gains, fixed structure.} The hip-roll joints are
not driven by the gait generator at all. They are driven by a linear reflex on base roll,
\begin{equation}
  u \;=\; k_p\,\phi_{\text{roll}} \;+\; k_d\,\dot{\phi}_{\text{roll}} \;+\; b,
  \qquad q_{\text{hip roll},L} = q^{\text{nom}}_L + u, \quad q_{\text{hip roll},R} = q^{\text{nom}}_R - u,
\end{equation}
evaluated at every control step. Because the left and right roll axes are mirrored, opposite signs
in joint space move both feet the same physical way, which is the lateral centre-of-mass shift we
want. The three gains $(k_p, k_d, b)$ are \emph{emitted by the policy}, so the controller is
learned --- but only three numbers of it are, rather than a free trajectory. This was the design
decision that made the original per-cycle formulation tractable at all (a feedforward CPG for
propulsion plus a feedback reflex for balance, instead of a per-step policy with free abduction),
and it was kept in the per-step version because it works. Nothing assumes which sign stabilises;
the policy discovers that. The reflex is necessarily inert while roll is railed (milestones
\texttt{m1}--\texttt{m3}, where the gains are unidentifiable) and only becomes live at
\texttt{m5} --- expected behaviour, not a bug, and worth knowing before reading those training
curves.

\paragraph{Sagittal (pitch) reflex --- hand-tuned, not learned.} A fixed PD on base pitch and pitch
rate drives a \emph{symmetric} fore/aft thigh offset,
\begin{equation}
  u_p = -\operatorname{clip}\bigl(k_p^{\theta} g_x + k_d^{\theta} \dot{\theta} + b^{\theta},\; \pm u_{\max}\bigr),
  \qquad q_{\text{thigh},L} \mathrel{+}= u_p, \quad q_{\text{thigh},R} \mathrel{-}= u_p,
\end{equation}
where $g_x \approx \sin\theta$ is the fore/aft component of the measured gravity direction. Again
the thigh axes are mirrored, so the opposite signs move both feet the \emph{same} physical
direction: when the robot pitches nose-down, both feet shift forward, under the falling centre of
mass. This is capture-point control in its crudest form, and the gain scales follow that reading
($k_p \approx h_{\text{CoM}}/\ell \approx 1$, $k_d \approx \sqrt{h/g} \approx 0.3$).

The reason this one is \emph{not} learned is worth spelling out, because it is the opposite choice
from the abduction reflex. It was introduced when the pitch-free milestone was collapsing in
1.5\,s, and a learned gain has no useful gradient in that regime: the policy has to
survive long enough to experience the correction before it can learn to tune it, and it was not
surviving. A hand-tuned prior breaks that circularity. The gains were set by a grid search on the
pitch-free plant: $k_d \geq 0.3$ oscillates through the one-step action delay and the target
smoothing filter, and an authority clip above 0.35\,rad starts fighting the gait itself,
which fixes $k_p = 2.0$, $k_d = 0.2$, clip 0.25\,rad. Measured on a pitch-kick arrest
test, the reflex reduces the final pitch excursion by a factor of 2.6--6.7 in both
directions, and lifts early-retraining survival from about 18 to about 80 control
steps.

That number is the point: the reflex is a \textbf{stabilising prior, not a controller}. The plant
with height free cannot stand at zero action at all --- holding a ride height requires an active
gait, so it collapses in $\approx$ 1.3\,s no matter what the pitch does. The reflex does
not fix that; it buys the policy enough surviving episodes to find a pitch-neutral gait itself. An
early acceptance gate of ``zero action stands for 20 seconds'' was simply wrong for this plant and
was replaced by the kick-arrest test.

\paragraph{Residuals --- the fast channel.} The six per-step residuals are the third feedback path,
and the one the literature is most emphatic about: they are what lets the controller respond to
something unmodelled within a single control step without rewriting the gait. They are kept small
(0.08\,rad, raised to 0.20\,rad once the control rate moved to 200\,Hz)
and are magnitude-penalised, so the Fourier prior stays dominant.

All three are implemented in pure NumPy, in the same module that reconstructs the gait, with no
dependency on the simulator. That is not incidental --- it is what allows the identical reflex code
to run on the Raspberry Pi against a real IMU, rather than being re-implemented and re-tuned at
deployment time.

\paragraph{A negative result: the emulated ankle reflex.} One more reflex was built and rejected.
Since the ankles are passive springs with no actuator, we implemented a fixed PD that applies a
pitch-restoring \emph{torque} at the ankle joints, gated on that foot being grounded --- an
emulated actuated ankle, and a shippable controller if a real ankle motor were ever added. At
40\,N$\cdot$m of authority it \emph{destabilised} the running gait outright (episodes
collapsed to 28 steps): a phase-blind torque fights the gait it is trying to stabilise. At a
gentle 12\,N$\cdot$m it neither destabilised nor solved anything, giving a small,
non-solving lift. The negative result was still informative --- it was evidence that ankle
authority was the right lever, which is what motivated the passive-stiffness experiment described
next.

trained well. Releasing pitch at \texttt{m3} produced the hardest failure of the project: episodes

of work went into it, and it is worth recording what was ruled out, because the null results are
the useful part.

Two methodological lessons came out of this table. First, \textbf{any training aid that holds the
robot up will be leaned on}, and the in-training curve will look wonderful while the unaided policy
gets worse; only an evaluation with the aid forced to zero tells the truth. We were misled by this
three times before adopting the assist-off evaluation as protocol. Second, when every software
lever saturates at the same value, the constraint is not in the software.

\paragraph{The diagnostic that cracked it.} Rather than try a ninth lever, we instrumented the
failure: a tool that rolls out a policy and plots, per foot, the fore/aft offset between the toe at
touchdown and the whole-robot centre of mass. The result was unambiguous. The base pitch ramps
monotonically forward into a pure topple, and meanwhile the feet land 8--9\,cm
\emph{behind} the centre of mass --- only 11\,\% of touchdowns landed ahead of it, and
they fell progressively further behind as the body ran away forward. The policy had a perfectly
serviceable forward-running gait and had never learned the \emph{capture step}: plant the foot
ahead of the centre of mass to catch a forward fall. It was, quite literally, running out from
under itself.

\paragraph{The fix was a plant change.} The candidate lever this pointed to was the passive ankle.
Stiffening the ankle spring by roughly a factor of twelve (28.65--350\,N$\cdot$m/rad)
gives the passive foot the stance pitch-restoring authority the plant lacked. The threshold is
clean: $k \leq 200$ barely helps, $k \geq 350$ breaks out of the plateau and climbs steeply.
Post-fix, the same diagnostic reports 67\,\% of touchdowns landing ahead of the centre of
mass and the base pitch pinned near zero.

One implementation detail is essential and cost us a wasted run. The standing ankle sits at
$\approx \pm 0.25$\,rad while the spring reference is at $\pm 0.7$\,rad, so the
spring carries $\approx 12.8$\,N$\cdot$m of \emph{preload} at stance. Naively raising the
stiffness balloons that preload, the foot slams to its spring reference and the robot flips over.
The reference has to be shifted simultaneously so that $k(q_{\text{stand}} - q_{\text{ref}})$ is
preserved: only the restoring \emph{gain} rises, the posture is unchanged.

This result also \emph{overturned} our own earlier recommendation. After the emulated-ankle
experiments we had concluded that the robot needed a policy-controlled actuated ankle --- a motor,
two more action dimensions, and a full retrain of the milestone ladder. The stiffness experiment
shows a passive spring is sufficient, which is dramatically cheaper. And, as noted in
\cref{sec:speed-ceiling}, the static load analysis independently arrives at the same
350\,N$\cdot$m/rad threshold.

That left one question open --- whether the real ankle could be built that stiff --- and the answer,
when it was measured, invalidated the result.

\paragraph{The measured spring, and what it does to this conclusion.}
\label{sec:rl-ankle-overturned}

The fabricated ankle is $k = 41.4$\,N$\cdot$m/rad with \textbf{no preload}. Neither number matches the
model: $k = 350$ will not be built, and the 12.8\,N$\cdot$m of stance preload that the simulation
carried does not exist on the robot.

The second discrepancy turns out to matter more than the first, which is the genuinely useful finding
here. The preload, not the stiffness, was doing almost all of the work. At the same $k$, the modelled
spring sags 19\,mm at stance with its preload and \textbf{145\,mm without it}. That reframes the whole
ankle question: the parameter the study spent its compute sweeping was not the parameter that
mattered.

Statics disposes of the real spring before any learning is involved. At 0.5 body weights per foot ---
double support, the easiest possible load case, against a 3.5\,BW running requirement --- the
no-preload spring deflects $26.6^\circ$, drops the robot 145\,mm, and puts the heel 11.9\,mm into the
floor at the reset pose. Contact forces are well behaved throughout, so this is genuine load-capacity
collapse rather than a solver artefact.

\textbf{Everything in \cref{tab:rl-results} was therefore obtained on an ankle that does not exist.}
The balance result of this section is real as a statement about what the \emph{plant} needed; it is
not a statement about what this robot does.

The live candidate is now neither the fabricated spring nor a stiffer one: it is a tension-only
strut, taut at the flat-foot angle and buckling in compression. It stands like a welded ankle
($0.08^\circ$ of deflection, 0.8\,mm of sag) and, by deleting the spring assembly, removes 249\,g from
each shin. \cref{tab:mass-sensitivity} independently favours that: at $-0.93$\,(m/s)/kg on distal
mass, 249\,g per leg is worth roughly $+0.5$\,m/s of top speed, which is the largest single
improvement available anywhere on the robot. A design decision reached for one reason turns out to be
supported by an unrelated measurement, which is the closest this project comes to a free result.

\subsubsection{The cadence problem and a cautionary bug}
\label{sec:rl-cadence}

With balance solved, the remaining pathology was cadence: the policies stepped far too fast, on the
order of 11\,Hz, pattering rather than striding. Several external limits were tried ---
hard velocity caps, acceleration caps, a cadence penalty --- and all of them collapsed the
warm-started policy instead of slowing it.

Instrumenting the trained policy's own action channels found why. When the control rate was raised
to 200\,Hz, the learnable gait-frequency range had been widened to
0.5--50\,Hz on the reasoning that it would then be ``non-binding''. But the map from
the raw action to frequency is linear over that range, so a \emph{neutral} network output
($\approx 0$) means 25\,Hz. The policy simply left that output near zero and inherited a
24\,Hz gait clock --- the commanded thigh trajectory reversing sign 35 times a second at
$\pm 0.45$\,rad, a signal that would destroy the real actuators. Every external limit had
been fighting a broken internal default. Remapping the range to 0.5--4.0\,Hz, so that
a neutral action means $\approx$ 2\,Hz, produced the best controller of the entire project
(\cref{tab:rl-results}).

The general lesson is worth carrying: \textbf{a parameter range widened to be ``non-binding'' can
silently become the operating point}, because a network's default output is not a neutral gait ---
it is whatever the middle of the range happens to decode to.

A residual pathology survives the fix. After the remap, the gait clock slowed to $\approx$
1\,Hz but the actual \emph{footfall} rate stayed near 6\,Hz: the policy steps
through the fast residual channel rather than through the clock. A ring-down test showed the passive
ankle mode sits at 1.0\,Hz, not 6.3\,Hz, so this is a forced oscillation, not a
resonance --- the policy pumping its legs to balance a top-heavy robot at 200\,Hz. Neither
a residual-rate penalty, a torque-budget curriculum, reducing the pitch-reflex derivative gain, low-pass
filtering the reflex input, nor heavy ankle damping removed it, and warm-started runs are all
trapped in the fast-stepping basin. The honest conclusion is that it needs a from-scratch retrain
with cadence pressure present from step zero, or a plant change (a lower centre of mass); on
hardware, the real mechanical damping of the ankle would absorb some of it.

\paragraph{An A/B on the generator itself.} Because the Fourier series is one of several ways to
parameterise a periodic gait, it was compared directly against a phase-amplitude coupled-oscillator
generator of the kind used in the biologically-inspired locomotion literature, with the foot
trajectory mapped through a measured inverse-kinematics table. Sixteen runs, one plant, one reward,
one schedule, only the generator swapped. The verdict is that \textbf{there is no clear winner}: the
oscillator is more sample-efficient from a cold start on the hardest milestone, the Fourier
generator is more reliable across random seeds, and the single best controller measured in the whole
experiment was an oscillator run --- the only arm of the sixteen that ever executed the stop phase,
at 2.9\,m/s with a left/right asymmetry of 0.08.

One ablation in that experiment is worth reporting because it refutes a hypothesis stated earlier in
this chapter. Removing the per-step residual channel, leaving a pure oscillator, produced
\emph{stability without locomotion}: the robot survived most of the episode but travelled 15\,m of 72
at 0.25\,m/s, with the worst cadence in the entire set. The residual channel was the suspected fast
open path responsible for the 6\,Hz footfall of \cref{sec:rl-cadence}; removing it made the chatter
worse, not better, so that explanation is wrong.

\paragraph{Cost.} Training ran on the EPFL Izar cluster. Throughput is $\approx 2900$
environment-steps per second on a V100 node, which is $\approx 1.9\times$ the CPU-only rate on the
same node --- and, worth recording, not because the network is large. Physics is always on the CPU
(there is no GPU physics backend in this stack); putting the tiny policy network on the GPU simply
stops PyTorch from stealing cores from the parallel MuJoCo workers. At that rate a 600\,M-step
milestone is a multi-day job, which is why the warm-start chain across milestones matters as much
as it does.

\subsubsection{Validation status and known gaps}
\label{sec:rl-validation}

Stating this plainly matters more than the results above, because the gap between them is the work
that remains.

\textbf{Sim-to-sim} is exercised continuously by the milestone ladder: each stage is a different
plant (different constrained dynamics) and every stage loads the previous stage's weights, so a
policy that only worked by exploiting a particular rail fails visibly at the next rung. The
ankle-stiffness and centre-of-mass experiments are the same mechanism used deliberately as a
plant-sensitivity probe, and the drive-bandwidth correction of \cref{sec:plant-id-drive} is the most
severe instance of it: a converged walker collapsed from an episode length of 1448 to 251 with only
the ankle and the drive changed.

\textbf{No trained policy has run on the robot.} That is the precise statement, and it is narrower
than ``nothing has run on hardware''. A great deal has: the hand-authored fixed gait plays on the
real motors, the web interface drives all six joints under safety limits, the workspace calibration
and the system-identification excitation runs are all recordings taken from the machine, and every
measurement in \cref{sec:plant-id} came off it. What has never happened is a learned controller
closing a loop on the physical robot.

The sprint policies in \cref{tab:rl-results} are not deployable as they stand, independently of the
plant correction. They observe two privileged quantities that do not exist on the robot: the base
linear velocity, which would require a state estimator that has not been built, and the distance
remaining to the finish line. A separate policy lineage was therefore started for a drivable
demonstrator, which removes both --- velocity has to be inferred from a 200\,ms strided observation
history instead --- and adds per-episode plant randomisation (mass, inertia, friction, servo gains,
joint damping, ankle spring stiffness, gravity tilt, actuation delay), a sensor-noise model including
the accelerometer leakage that makes a real IMU's ``down'' swing by several degrees during push-off,
and the steering channels the mirror-symmetric gait lacks. That lineage cannot warm-start from the
sprint milestones, since the observation and action dimensions both changed, so it is a separate
training cost. Its results, and the diagnoses that produced them, are reported in
\cref{sec:sim2real-lineage}.

Domain randomisation ranges are now set from measurement rather than from taste where the measurement
exists. Ground friction is sampled over 0.35--0.60 against a measured $\mu = 0.84$ (a $40^\circ$ slip
angle), deliberately well below the real value, because the failure mode being guarded against is
planting a foot that then slides. Joint homing error is sampled at $5^\circ$ against a calibration
accurate to 1--2$^\circ$, as a safety envelope. Where no measurement exists the range stays wide and
is flagged as such: link inertia tensors are randomised at $\pm 25\,\%$ precisely because
\cref{sec:plant-id-sysid} could not resolve the mass distribution.

The remaining known gaps, recorded deliberately rather than discovered later: there is no policy
export path and no inference loop on the Pi, which is the critical path for everything else; and the
actuator model still gives each motor an independent torque limit and speed limit rather than a
coupled torque--speed envelope, which makes the simulation optimistic exactly in the high-speed
regime the project cares about. First hardware bring-up remains planned with the base fixed and the
legs in the air, which validates wiring, direction conventions, joint limits and gait shape, but with
no ground contact tells us nothing about balance or transfer.

The five subsections that follow report what has happened since this status was written: a generator
A/B (\cref{sec:cpg-ab}), the ankle resolved as a build decision (\cref{sec:ankle-build}), the
drivable lineage's descent onto the measured plant (\cref{sec:sim2real-lineage}), a classical
baseline built to test whether learning was ever the bottleneck (\cref{sec:scripted-baseline}), and
the rebuilt robot (\cref{sec:rebuilt-robot}).

\subsubsection{A second generator: explicit-oscillator CPG versus the Fourier series}
\label{sec:cpg-ab}

The Fourier parameterisation of \cref{sec:rl-cpg} is one member of a family; the other canonical
member is the explicit limit-cycle oscillator of the Ijspeert school, as used by CPG-RL. Since
the literature gave no basis to prefer one, it was measured: sixteen cluster jobs, one plant, one
reward, one schedule, \emph{only the generator swapped}. Building the oscillator arm forced a
foot-space formulation with a measured inverse-kinematics lookup table, and the LUT construction
itself surfaced plant facts worth recording: from the stance pose \emph{both} cam and thigh move
the toe mostly fore--aft and either sign of cam \emph{shortens} the near-straight leg (the
intuitive ``thigh swings, cam lifts'' mapping is simply wrong and produces no step); the 4-bar
has a folded solution branch that must be filtered; the IK inversion must propagate continuity
between cells or it flips branches mid-swing; and reachable foot lift at mid-stride is only
$\sim$8\,cm, because the leg is near-straight there.

Two results stand out beyond the tie. First, the \textbf{pure oscillator with the residual
channel removed produces stability without locomotion}: it survives 9700 of 12\,000 steps but
travels 15\,m of 72 at 0.25\,m/s with the worst foot-scuffing cadence of all sixteen arms. This
\emph{refutes} the standing hypothesis that the per-step residual channel was the open fast path
causing the 6\,Hz footfall chatter of \cref{sec:rl-cadence} — removing it made chatter worse, not
better — and it is the empirical form of the literature's verdict that a per-step channel is
mandatory. Second, when the full base-DOF ladder was subsequently run on the oscillator
generator, it \textbf{cleared m1--m4 (100\,m dash at m2, 2.95\,m/s at m3) and walled hard at m5
(roll)} — episode length 337 against the Fourier ladder's 3213 at matched budget, with the
plateau confirmed at 2.5$\times$ budget. On the roll axis the Fourier generator succeeds where
the oscillator does not; both share the identical 3-parameter roll reflex, so the difference is
in the generator itself and points future investigation at lateral authority. The pragmatic
conclusion adopted: keep the Fourier generator for the deployment lineage, use the oscillator
result as the tiebreak evidence that the action-space \emph{family} is right.

Getting this A/B to run also produced a curriculum lesson expensive enough to state as a rule.
One milestone in the ladder took six restarts, five of them self-inflicted, and the terminal bug
was a competence gate that held \emph{both} the difficulty ramp and the efficiency-penalty ramp:
for a policy that never reached the gate, the efficiency terms were pinned at exactly zero for
the entire run — a run that looked like ``the robot cannot learn this'' was actually ``the reward
is not the one we designed''. What settled it was not another hypothesis but a field-by-field
configuration diff against the last run that worked. \textbf{Never gate the ramp that is supposed
to rescue competence on competence itself; and diff configurations before theorising.}

\subsubsection{The ankle, revisited as a build decision}
\label{sec:ankle-build}

The 350\,N$\cdot$m/rad ankle-stiffness result of \cref{sec:rl-ankle-overturned} answered a
simulation question; the hardware then asked a different one. The spring actually fabricated
measures $k = 41.4$\,N$\cdot$m/rad with \emph{no preload} (\cref{sec:plant-id-ankle}), and a
spring wound to 350 will not be manufactured. The question became: what should be \emph{built} —
the real soft spring, or no spring at all (a rigid strut), which would also delete the 249\,g
spring assembly from each shin, on the axis the sensitivity analysis prices at
$-0.93$\,(m/s)/kg?

\paragraph{The soft no-preload spring is eliminated by statics alone, before any learning.} At
0.5 body weights per foot — double support with the base held, the easiest load case there is,
against a 3.5\,BW running requirement — the as-built spring deflects the ankle $26.6^\circ$ past
flat, drops the robot 145\,mm, and rotates the foot so far that the heel carries 208\,N with the
toe driven 11.9\,mm \emph{into} the floor at the reset pose. No controller fixes this; a policy
chooses where to put a foot, it cannot make a spring hold seven times its load. The instructive
split is preload versus stiffness: the same $k=41.4$ sags 19\,mm \emph{with} the original preload
versus 145\,mm without — \textbf{the preload, not the stiffness, was doing 7.6$\times$ of the
work} in the shipped ankle.

\paragraph{The strut wins the learning screen.} In the RL comparison (cold m3, corrected plant),
the compression-capable strut arm reaches episode length 2307 in \emph{one third} the steps of
the fully welded ankle (29\,M vs 73\,M) and walks 3$\times$ more symmetrically; the welded arm,
given time, completes the 57\,m dash at 2.55\,m/s in 5 of 6 episodes, while a tension-only strut
(no compression capacity) manages 0.6\,m and stands on one leg. The compression capacity is the
entire story — the arms are statically identical otherwise.

\paragraph{And the as-built part fails by arithmetic.} The fabricated strut is a 500\,mm
$\times$ 3\,mm solid PETG rod: slenderness $L/r = 667$, Euler buckling load
\textbf{0.24--0.33\,N} — thirty-five times below the 10\,N the simulation arm assumed, and ten
times less moment at the ankle than the foot's own gravity torque, so it cannot even hold the
foot flat in swing; its tension capacity (283\,N) is also below the 360\,N walking peak. A 3\,mm
pultruded carbon rod ($P_{cr}$ 14.1\,N, 4241\,N tension, 5.7\,g) or a 6/4\,mm carbon tube
(181\,N) reproduces the simulated part. \textbf{The ankle question closed as a build
instruction: make it a stiff link, in carbon, and delete the spring mass.}

The instruction was executed (\cref{sec:rebuilt-robot}): the ankle now is a rigid carbon tube
weighing 40\,g, removing a net 209\,g per shin (573\,$\to$\,364\,g; the robot totals 14.72\,kg) on
the axis worth $-0.93$\,(m/s)/kg — 209\,g off each shin buys more predicted speed than 1.8\,kg off
the base would.

\subsubsection{The drivable-demonstrator lineage: closing on the measured plant}
\label{sec:sim2real-lineage}

The deployable lineage announced in \cref{sec:rl-validation} has since accumulated results, and
they are the closest thing this project has to a sim-to-real verdict short of untethered
hardware. The lineage removes both privileged observations (velocity must be inferred from a
200\,ms strided history), adds steering, and layers on the full sensor/plant randomisation
package. Its history is a sequence of measured diagnoses:

\paragraph{Steering was broken twice over, and both causes were in the plant model.} The first
policy could not turn right at all. An open-loop authority test (hand-built gait, roll and pitch
railed — isolating plant from policy) found (a) the \emph{model} is not left/right symmetric: two
linkage anchor coordinates differ by $\sim$1\,mm in the wrong axes, a CAD-export artefact worth
$+0.42$\,rad/s of intrinsic yaw; and (b) the two steering channels were scaled inversely to their
measured authority — differential stance width is $\sim$6.6$\times$ stronger per unit than
differential stride, and the weak channel's whole authority (0.26\,rad/s) was smaller than the
intrinsic bias. The fix randomises the anchor asymmetry ($\pm$1.5\,mm) rather than symmetrising
the file — the real robot will be asymmetric too, and on a perfectly symmetric plant the policy
has no reason to learn the correction that transfers. Post-fix, yaw-response slope went from 0.19
to 1.22 with both turn directions reachable.

\paragraph{Domain randomisation was the thing breaking the policy — measured, not assumed.} On a
fixed 20-seeded-episode evaluation, removing one adversity at a time from a trained policy:
pushes changed nothing (1.0$\times$ mean time between falls), sensor noise 1.5$\times$, trips
1.8$\times$ — and \textbf{plant randomisation 19.8$\times$} (95.4\,s vs 4.8\,s MTBF; a clean
plant gives 196\,s and zero falls). The policy walked fine; full-width plant DR from step zero
was what kept it down, while every other axis already had a curriculum. DR width now rides a
gated, retreating ramp like everything else. Three evaluation-hygiene rules were paid for here:
training reward across runs with different curricula is not comparable (always evaluate on a
fixed task); six evaluation episodes gave a 5$\times$ spread on fall rate for the \emph{same}
policy (use $\geq$20 seeded episodes); and a curriculum that can only advance will ride a policy
down — competence must gate the \emph{advance}, not just the opening (one run peaked at
113\,M steps and declined monotonically for 215\,M more while the clock-driven ramps kept
tightening).

\paragraph{The measured plant, applied all at once, kills a working policy — applied as a
curriculum, it does not.} With every measured correction active (weighed masses, 144.5\,N$\cdot$m
torque, strut ankle, 0.8\,Hz + 25\,ms drive, friction deliberately below the measured
$\mu = 0.84$), the previous best walker collapses from episode length 1448 / reward $+511$ to 251
/ $-78$ with nothing else changed. Ramping the drive bandwidth logarithmically in Hz (the filter
coefficient is violently nonlinear in bandwidth) over 120\,M steps, the same policy completes the
descent to the real 0.8\,Hz drive and stabilises at \textbf{episode length 4938 (peak 6833) and
reward $+3271$} — three times its parent's score, \emph{on the real drive}. That checkpoint is
the warm-start parent for everything downstream. The residual gap to a deployable walker is
honest but bounded: on the hardest configuration the current generation survives a median 9.4\,s
with 3 of 12 seeded episodes reaching the 60\,s cap, walking at $\sim$0.14\,m/s with a limp — and
the command-range fix this motivated (the command box asked for 0.6\,m/s from a plant that tops
out at 0.14; forward commands above 0.3\,m/s are fatal while \emph{backward} is the safest
direction) is in the currently training generation.

\paragraph{The lineage also re-proved the ladder.} This is the one lineage in the project that
trains with all six base degrees of freedom free from step zero — and the one that does not
converge (three plateaus across $\sim$1.4\,G steps). Meanwhile the only controller to reach
2.55\,m/s on the \emph{measured} plant did so at milestone m3, roll and yaw railed, in 73\,M
steps. Everything that has ever worked on this robot used the base-DOF ladder; the audit of the
archive (97 runs at m3) makes that an empirical claim, not a preference. Two architectural
responses are now in training: restarting the lineage \emph{through} the ladder on the rebuilt
plant, and an asymmetric actor--critic — the critic keeps the privileged observation, and a
separately supervised velocity-estimator head (trained on labels, detached from the policy
gradient so PPO cannot bend it toward whatever the actor wishes were true) replaces the
privileged velocity for the actor, motivated by the measurement that only $R^2 \approx 0.81$ of
base velocity is recoverable from the observation history.

\paragraph{Two bugs from this lineage belong in every RL practitioner's checklist.} A
warm-started run inherited the \emph{source checkpoint's} RNG seed — so every warm-started ``seed
replicate'' in the project was a bit-identical duplicate (verified: two seeds, 100 rollouts,
identical curves) until the seed was re-asserted after loading. And the evaluator rebuilt the
environment from the config's \emph{starting} values rather than the curriculum's final ones,
reading a 340\,M-step policy at 32$\times$ below its true performance — the fourth
evaluation-restore bug of the project, and the reason the rule ``\texttt{curriculum.json} is
ground truth for anything a curriculum moved'' now exists. Related: outcomes on the hard plant
are \emph{bimodal} (fall at $\sim$2\,s or survive 60\,s), so unpaired small-sample comparisons
are noise — one early comparison ``found'' a 27$\times$ sensor-noise effect that does not exist.
Common random numbers and survivor counts, minimum twelve episodes, are the reporting standard
for everything above.

\subsubsection{A scripted baseline: how far does no learning get?}
\label{sec:scripted-baseline}

Late in the project a classical controller was written for the same plant: a clock-driven foot
trajectory through the measured foot IK, once-per-swing Raibert/capture-point foot placement,
low-gain pitch and roll trims, and feedforward inversion of the 0.8\,Hz drive — 21 hand-set
gains, zero training, injected through the environment's residual channel so that every plant
feature (drive model, delay, randomisation, contact, termination) is byte-identical to the RL
runs. Its purpose was to answer a question the RL curves cannot: when training stalls, is the
\emph{learning} the bottleneck, or the machine?

The controller's structure is itself an argument about the plant. The foot-placement target is
latched \emph{once per swing} rather than tracked continuously, because the drive is a 0.8\,Hz
pole ($\tau = 199$\,ms) against an inverted-pendulum divergence constant of
$\sqrt{h/g} = 311$\,ms: a continuous pitch loop would need crossover around 1\,Hz \emph{through}
a 0.8\,Hz actuator pole, spending $\sim$60$^\circ$ of phase before acting, whereas latching at
swing entry gives the drive a whole swing ($\approx$1.8$\tau$), delivering $\sim$83\,\% of the
commanded placement by touchdown. The same arithmetic sets the tuned cadence near 1\,Hz (at
2\,Hz the drive returns 0.38 of the commanded amplitude) and motivates the one element RL
structurally lacks: explicit feedforward inversion of the drive pole,
$q_{\text{cmd}} = q + \tau\,\dot q$.

The answer is sharp in both directions. On the height-free, attitude-railed plant (m2), the
scripted controller runs \textbf{60\,s on all 5 seeds} — where the RL reference achieves
20.3\,$\pm$\,21.6\,s — winning less on the mean than on the variance, since the RL outcome is
bimodal. On the pitch-free plant (m3) it survives \textbf{1.0\,s, 0 of 5}, against the RL
reference's 1.15\,s: \emph{the same wall, to the decimal}.

\paragraph{The wall is free fall, proved by a formula with no controller in it.} Modelling the
robot as a rigid inverted pendulum about the toe, the passive topple time from initial lean
$\theta_0$ is $t = \sqrt{h/g}\,\cosh^{-1}(60^\circ/\theta_0)$. Sweeping gravity in simulation:

Three mechanisms compose the wall. The stance keyframe starts every episode with the toes
9.1\,cm ahead of the centre of mass — a $5.67^\circ$ backward lean at $t=0$ (the model's own
comment claims ``feet under the CoM''; measurement says otherwise), and correcting it buys
exactly the free-fall time of the residual lean, no more. The single toe-sphere contact gives
\emph{zero} centre-of-pressure authority at any ankle stiffness — the ankle at m3 is not weak, it
is disconnected. And foot placement is geometrically outrun: with the toe $\sim$0.92\,m below the
CoM, a pitch rate $\dot\theta$ sweeps the contact point forward at $L\dot\theta$, measured to
beat the controller's corrective foot motion 2:1 until the $\pm$0.30\,m fore--aft workspace
saturates in under a second. Escaping m3 therefore needs \emph{either} a corrected keyframe
\emph{plus} a foot with real fore--aft extent and an ankle strong enough to use it, or
step-on-demand triggering in place of a free-running gait clock. This is a plant conclusion, not
a controller one — which is exactly what the identical RL and scripted failures imply.

Two further findings transfer beyond the baseline. The stand keyframe's control vector is not a
static equilibrium — the position servo needs $\sim$6\,cm more commanded foot extension to hold
15\,kg, which RL policies discover silently and hand-built controllers must be told. And
\emph{feedforward beats feedback through this drive}: a constant extension offset stands for
20\,s where adding ride-height feedback drops it to 7.8\,s, because a continuous loop closed
through a 0.8\,Hz pole against a plant diverging in 0.31\,s destabilises. Finally, a
methodological rhyme: the baseline's automatic gain tuner, given the objective
$t_{\text{alive}} + 3\,\mathrm{dist}$, immediately exploited it exactly as a policy would —
driving 1.6\,m/s against a 0.3\,m/s command to farm the distance term on the pitch-railed plant.
\textbf{Reward exploitation is a property of optimisation against an objective, not of neural
networks.}

\paragraph{Why the learned controller did not win, and what that says about stability margin.}
\label{sec:margin-analysis}
\Cref{sec:bg-margin} states the theoretical expectation: the scripted controller lies inside the
policy class, so with an exact plant and unbounded training the learned optimum can only match or
exceed it. The measurement disagrees at both milestones, and --- this is the part worth reporting
--- \emph{for different reasons at each}. Collapsing the two into a single ``RL underperformed''
loses the whole finding.

\emph{At m2, a margin existed and the learning did not find it.} The scripted controller survives
60\,s on 5 of 5 seeds against the learned reference's 20.3\,$\pm$\,21.6\,s, so this is not a plant
limit; the plant is stabilisable and one of the two controllers stabilises it. Three mechanisms
account for the gap, and each is a statement about the optimisation rather than about the policy
class:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{The feedforward inverse is representable but not discoverable.} The scripted
        controller inverts the drive pole explicitly, $q_{\text{cmd}} = q + \tau\dot q$. Nothing
        prevents a network from expressing that map --- it is linear in quantities the observation
        history contains --- but expressing it and \emph{finding} it are different problems. The
        gradient signal for phase lead is indirect: a policy is rewarded for surviving, not for
        leading the plant by 199\,ms, and the credit for a correctly anticipated command arrives
        several control steps after the action that earned it. Universality of the policy class
        does not confer the credit assignment needed to exploit it.
  \item \textbf{Margin is not in the objective, and the variance is the evidence.} The return is
        survival time and distance; the distance from the state to the failure boundary is priced
        nowhere. An optimiser given that objective has no reason not to park close to the boundary
        and collect the reward, and the signature of a policy that has done so is bimodality
        rather than degradation --- it works or it falls. That is exactly the measured shape:
        20.3\,$\pm$\,21.6\,s is a standard deviation larger than its own mean, against 5 of 5 at
        the episode cap. \textbf{The seed variance is the margin measurement.} A controller with
        margin does not produce a bimodal seed distribution, and a learning curve reporting the
        mean will not show this.
  \item \textbf{``Unbounded training'' is doing real work in the theoretical claim.} A milestone
        here is 600\,M environment-steps and a multi-day cluster job
        (\cref{sec:rl-results}); the project as a whole spent roughly 3\,billion. That is a large
        budget and it is not the asymptotic one the argument assumes.
\end{itemize}

\emph{At m3, no margin existed for either controller.} Survival equals the analytic free-fall time
of an \emph{uncontrolled} inverted pendulum to within 3--9\,\% across a 5$\times$ change in the
plant's time constant (\cref{tab:freefall}), from a formula with no controller term in it. The
mechanisms above are the reason: with zero centre-of-pressure authority and foot placement outrun
2:1, the set of stabilising action sequences from the initial condition is essentially empty, and
every controller drawn from an empty feasible set performs identically. Reinforcement learning
optimises over the space of \emph{controllers}; it has no purchase on the space of \emph{physics}.
The theoretical margin advantage is not wrong at m3 --- it is vacuous, because the quantity it
promises to enlarge is already zero.

Two further observations tie this to the rest of the chapter. First, the mechanism one would
normally use to convert the theoretical advantage into a real one --- randomising what is not known,
so that the policy is optimal over a set of plants rather than one --- was measured on this robot to
\emph{cost} more than it bought: domain randomisation was the thing breaking the drivable policy
(\cref{sec:sim2real-lineage}), and the measured plant applied all at once killed a working policy
that survived the same correction applied as a curriculum. The robustness bought by randomisation is
paid for out of performance margin, and on a plant whose margin is already near zero there is
nothing to pay with. On a machine with margin to spare the trade is invisible; here it is the
dominant term. Second, margin is a frequency-domain quantity, and neither method can synthesise
phase the actuator does not have: a 199\,ms drive pole against a 311\,ms divergence constant bounds
what \emph{any} controller can achieve, learned or hand-built, which is why the drive loop appears
in \cref{sec:speed-ceiling-corrected} and \cref{sec:future} as a mechanical priority rather than a
tuning one.

The claim this supports is narrower and more useful than either ``RL beats classical'' or the
reverse: \textbf{the stability-margin advantage of learning scales with how much margin the plant
has to give.} On a plant with margin, learning must still be paid --- in reward design that prices
the boundary, and in the training budget to reach the asymptote --- before it collects. On a plant
without margin, the comparison measures the machine, and the correct response is to change the
machine.

\subsubsection{Postscript: the rebuilt robot, and a cap that was never there}
\label{sec:rebuilt-robot}

On 2026-08-10 the hardware caught up with the analysis, and the training plant was re-issued to
match — four changes, three of them the physical robot converging on the model's recommendations
and one a modelling gap that had been open the entire project.

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{The actuators' speed was never capped in simulation.} Peak torque had always been
        limited (at the measured 61.2 / 144.5\,N$\cdot$m), but joint \emph{velocity} was
        unconstrained — a position servo could crank a joint arbitrarily fast for free. The
        record shows what that bought: the celebrated k350 pitch-balance winner
        (\cref{sec:rl-pitch-wall}) ``balanced'' partly by pattering one foot at 11\,Hz with peak
        thigh speed 23\,rad/s, \emph{past the motor's 22\,rad/s no-load ceiling}. The cap is now
        per joint at the measured no-load output speeds (10.30\,rad/s hip roll, 22.01\,rad/s
        cam/thigh) — per joint because the two actuator families differ by 2.1$\times$, so any
        scalar cap is wrong for one of them. One more entry in the pattern of
        \cref{sec:sim-hygiene}: an unconstrained axis is an exploit waiting to be found.
  \item \textbf{The ankle is now the rigid carbon tube} of \cref{sec:ankle-build} — stiff,
        symmetric in push and pull, no buckling limit — replacing both the soft spring and the
        PETG rod that modelled as tension-only.
  \item \textbf{The mass moved the right way}: net $-209$\,g per shin, total 14.72\,kg.
  \item \textbf{The drive was retuned from 0.8 to $\approx$3\,Hz}, measured by repeating the
        swept-sine of \cref{sec:plant-id-drive} after adjusting the boards' position-loop gains
        with the vendor tool: left thigh 0.86\,$\to$\,3.35\,Hz, cam 0.85\,$\to$\,2.88\,Hz, with
        the deliberately untouched abduction axis as the in-run control (0.74\,$\to$\,0.76\,Hz).
        The uniform-3\,Hz assumption is flagged: abduction is a different actuator and has not
        been re-measured, so the roll milestones may need re-baselining. The retune softens, but
        does not retire, the current-mode conclusion of \cref{sec:plant-id-drive} — 3\,Hz is
        still below the 4\,Hz top of the gait-frequency range.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{The bandwidth bracket that justified the retune.} Whether drive bandwidth was really
what stood between the robot and walking was settled by a three-arm experiment before the ladder
was relaunched: identical cold m2 runs at 0.8, 3 and 12\,Hz drive bandwidth, everything else
fixed, measured 25\,ms delay and workspace kill retained. At 0.8\,Hz — the robot as it was — the
policy \emph{shuffles and never walks} (duty factor 1.00, then falls). At 3\,Hz it \textbf{walks}:
20.75\,m at 0.69\,m/s with genuine per-foot flight. At 12\,Hz — effectively the unlimited drive
the whole 2.55\,m/s sprint record was trained in — it is \emph{worse} than 3\,Hz, folding one leg
into the workspace kill. So the measured retune sits, fortuitously, near the sweet spot; the old
unlimited-bandwidth plant was not just optimistic but qualitatively misleading; and the check
cannot be done by replaying an 0.8\,Hz-trained policy at higher bandwidth (tried: it moves faster
and falls) — the policy has to be \emph{trained} there.

On this rebuilt plant the scripted controller of \cref{sec:scripted-baseline}, re-tuned against a
full mission schedule (stand $\to$ walk forward $\to$ stand $\to$ walk backward $\to$ stand),
completes the mission in one 35\,s episode on 3 of 3 seeds with a drive-delivery ratio of 0.98
(against 0.31 on the 0.8\,Hz drive) — a small deliverable, but the first controller of any kind
to demonstrate command-following locomotion on the plant the robot actually is. Tuning against
the full mission mattered: a constant-velocity tune produced a walker that could not stand, the
scripted twin of the RL command-curriculum lesson. \textbf{The m3 pitch wall survives the
retune} — still $\approx$1.0\,s, still free fall, for both controller families — confirming it
structural rather than bandwidth-limited.

A second, more ambitious scripted controller (a capture-step state machine) was built on the
rebuilt plant and \emph{failed}, measuring on the way down four plant facts that any future
controller — learned or not — inherits: at 96\,\% of reach, commanded leg \emph{extension} does
not exist (a $-65$\,mm vertical command moves the toe $+85$\,mm \emph{forward} and 8\,mm down
through the IK, so weight support must be joint-space, not task-space); capture steps land on air
(the swung foot hovers 1--5\,cm high through an entire fall, from rear-workspace IK error plus
pitch lift — a ground-seeking touchdown helps but does not suffice); the 4-bar loop-closure
constraint itself yields $\sim$15\,cm of ride height under single-leg load with every actuated
joint on target, visible only to a forward-kinematics height loop that includes the passive
joints; and the plant's soft modes, measured by impulse response, put a weakly damped
$\sim$1\,Hz vertical body bounce right where discrete stepping at 2.3\,Hz pumps it — the
architectural reason the shuffling gait beats ``smarter'' stepping on this machine.

\subsection{Deployment}
\label{sec:deploy}

The policies of \cref{sec:rl} ultimately have to run on the Raspberry Pi described in
\cref{sec:compute-choice}, against real drives, with no simulator to reset. Four questions decide
whether that is viable: can a non-realtime Linux close a control loop fast enough (\cref{sec:deploy-linux});
can the bus carry the traffic, and what happens when it cannot (\cref{sec:deploy-can-runtime});
what does the motor controller actually accept and report (\cref{sec:deploy-drive}); and --- the one
this project learned the hard way --- when something breaks on hardware, is the failure
\emph{investigable} (\cref{sec:deploy-blackbox})? The first three were settled by measurement.
The fourth was settled by an incident.

\subsubsection{Linux without a real-time kernel}
\label{sec:deploy-linux}

The Pi runs a stock, non-realtime kernel. The loop-timing measurement of \cref{sec:plant-id-timing}
established that this is sufficient: 200\,Hz holds at half budget with millisecond-free jitter, and
the drives broadcast at exactly 200\,Hz anyway, so there is nothing for a faster loop to read.

One Python-specific detail is load-bearing enough to record. CPython hands the interpreter lock
between threads at most every \texttt{switchinterval} seconds --- 5\,ms by default, which is
\emph{exactly} one control period, so the web-interface thread could legally hold the interpreter
across an entire control tick. Dropping the interval to 0.5\,ms
(\texttt{sys.setswitchinterval(0.0005)}) took the daemon's late ticks from roughly 10\,\% to a few
percent. The steady state, measured with the control daemon, the 200\,Hz IMU thread, the web
interface and the flight recorder of \cref{sec:deploy-blackbox} all running, is \textbf{188\,Hz
against a 200\,Hz target, 5.30\,ms mean period, 3\,\% late ticks}. The missing 6\,\% is
operating-system scheduling, and no lever short of a realtime kernel recovers it.

Where the time actually goes was settled by an A/B under \texttt{systemd} overrides, restarting the
daemon between arms on the otherwise idle robot:

\paragraph{A methodological trap, again.} The first loop-rate figures taken during this bring-up
read 60\,Hz with 34\,\% late ticks, and produced a confident (and committed) diagnosis of an
expensive failure path in the CAN layer. The diagnosis was wrong: the A/B above shows the
suspected path costs nothing. The 60\,Hz was the \emph{measurement disturbing the measured} ---
\texttt{scp}, remote compilation, \texttt{journalctl} and HTTP polling all running on the Pi's four
cores while it was being sampled. This is the same failure mode as the IMU noise figures taken on
a swaying rig (\cref{sec:sensors}): the number was real, the attribution was not. The rule
that follows: never benchmark the robot over the same SSH session that is deploying to it, and
keep the retraction in the history rather than rewriting it.

\paragraph{Process supervision.} The daemon runs under \texttt{systemd}: started at boot,
restarted on crash (bounded to five restarts per minute, so a crash loop stays visibly down instead
of power-cycling the motors), stdout in the journal, and stopped by \texttt{SIGINT} so the shutdown
path runs --- which streams zero current to every motor and writes the recorder's clean-stop
record, whose \emph{absence} is how a power cut is later inferred (\cref{sec:deploy-blackbox}).
Before this, the only record of the daemon's death was whatever terminal happened to still be open.

\subsubsection{CAN at runtime: the bus with nothing on it}
\label{sec:deploy-can-runtime}

The protocol itself is described in \cref{sec:deploy-can}; bandwidth is a non-issue (26\,\% of one
of two 1\,Mbit buses). What the protocol description does not cover is the failure mode that
running under \texttt{systemd} made routine: \textbf{the daemon now starts before the motor power
is on}. With nothing on the bus to acknowledge a frame, socketcan's ten-frame transmit queue fills
within one tick and every subsequent \texttt{send()} raises \texttt{ENOBUFS}. On its first boot
under \texttt{systemd}, that exception --- unhandled, in a background thread --- killed the control
daemon outright during its own preflight. The bug predates the service; hand-started sessions had
simply always had the motors powered first.

The fix is that a send failure is an \emph{event, not a death}: sends never raise past the CAN
layer, failures are counted per channel, and a channel that fails ten consecutive sends is retried
at 5\,Hz instead of 200 --- one successful send restores full rate. Two honest measurements
qualify this. First, a failing send costs nothing: with the backoff disabled and three sends per
tick failing, the loop runs at 188.9\,Hz against 188.5 with it enabled. The backoff is kept for
hygiene (600 pointless system calls per second) and for forensics --- ``we did not attempt the
frame'' and ``we attempted it and it failed'' are different facts, and the recorder logs both.
Second, safety does not rest on the backoff: motors on a dead bus report nothing, so the motion
interlocks refuse to command them, and a bus that starts refusing frames \emph{while} the robot is
being commanded trips the safety stop within half a second.

\subsubsection{The motor controller as deployed}
\label{sec:deploy-drive}

Three properties of the CubeMars drives dominate the deployment design, all three measured rather
than documented (\cref{sec:plant-id-drive,sec:deploy-can}).

First, the only writable surface is the servo-mode command pair (\texttt{SET\_POS},
\texttt{SET\_CURRENT}); the internal cascaded gains are board configuration, readable off the
vendor tool but not over CAN, and the position loop they implement is proportional-only with a
0.8\,Hz corner. Second, the drives report no bus voltage, so pack sag under load is unobservable
from the drive side. Third --- and the property with the sharpest consequences ---
\textbf{the multi-turn position origin is not stable}: it re-randomises on every power cycle, and
the incident below is consistent with it moving \emph{between} power cycles as well. Every
absolute position command is therefore only as good as a calibration captured earlier in the same
session, and nothing in the drive signals when that calibration has silently become wrong.

\subsubsection{The safety envelope as an instrument}
\label{sec:safety}

A browser-based control interface runs on the robot and is the only path through which the hardware
is commanded by hand. It is treated here as methodology rather than as tooling, because the safety
envelope it enforces is also an experimental instrument.

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{Measured joint limits, not CAD limits.} Each joint is backdriven by hand through its
  full travel with the motors limp and the excursion recorded, giving a per-joint range that reflects
  the assembled machine including cabling and interference. The 4-bar's assemblable band is a joint
  \emph{pair} constraint, so the recorded reachable region is a box in the foot's fore-aft and lift
  coordinates rather than a set of independent joint ranges.
  \item \textbf{The recorded workspace is reused in simulation.} The same measured box is applied as a
  termination criterion during training: an episode ends the moment a foot leaves the region the real
  robot is known to reach. This began as a safety measure and became a sim-to-sim gate, because it
  makes an entire class of simulator-only exploit impossible rather than merely expensive. It also
  has to be applied carefully, since the criterion is expressed in the base frame and therefore cannot
  by itself distinguish a collapsed ankle from a folded linkage (\cref{sec:sim-hygiene}).
  \item \textbf{Playback is capped by kinematics, not by preference.} After a part failed during a
  high-cadence trajectory playback, the frequency ceiling was reduced from 5\,Hz to 2.5\,Hz, and the
  number was derived rather than guessed. Differentiating the stored waveform gives the peak joint
  speed per unit cadence as a property of the trajectory \emph{shape}: the cam joint demands
  359\,deg/s per Hz and reaches the actuator's no-load speed at 3.5\,Hz. Past that the command is not
  merely hard to track, it is kinematically impossible, and the drive simply saturates current
  chasing a target it cannot reach. At the 5\,Hz previously allowed, the cam was being commanded at
  143\,\% of no-load speed. The 2.5\,Hz ceiling leaves it at 71\,\%. Raising it again requires a
  hardware argument, and because the limit depends on the shape, a gentler trajectory can safely go
  faster.
  \item \textbf{Trip conditions.} Tracking error, joint range and temperature are monitored
  continuously and latch the drives to zero current. The tracking-error trip in particular had to be
  decoupled from the commanded trajectory once \cref{sec:plant-id-drive} showed that a large tracking
  error at speed is the \emph{expected} behaviour of this drive rather than a fault.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Hardware validation: the flight recorder and the pre-move guard}
\label{sec:deploy-blackbox}

On 2026-08-10 a joint destroyed itself on the bench. A zero pose had been captured, the drives'
raw origin no longer matched it by the time an absolute move was commanded, and the drive ---
faithfully executing a position command in the wrong frame --- wound \texttt{left.cam} through
roughly 1.9 output turns on a joint with $\pm86^\circ$ of travel. The investigation was
\emph{impossible}: the only telemetry in the system was a 4096-sample RAM ring at 20\,Hz, of which
the HTTP interface could serve the last 25.6 seconds, and nothing was on disk. By the time anyone
looked, the window had rolled past the event. The remainder of this section describes what was
built in response, because ``make the failure investigable'' turned out to be a design problem with
measurable requirements, not a logging afterthought.

\paragraph{A three-tier flight recorder.} Streaming the full state to disk continuously is not an
option: six motors at 200\,Hz is 35\,kB/s, 3\,GB/day, which fills and then destroys an SD card.
The recorder therefore splits by rate:

\begin{table}[H]
\centering
\small
\begin{tabular}{p{1.2cm}p{6.4cm}p{6.6cm}}
\toprule
\textbf{Tier} & \textbf{Content} & \textbf{Mechanism} \\
\midrule
A & every field, continuously & 20\,Hz, fixed-width binary segments, rotated under a global disk budget \\
B & the black box proper: the full 200\,Hz state & a 40\,s RAM ring, dumped to its own file on any trigger, \emph{pre-trigger history included}, plus a 3\,s post-trigger tail \\
C & one JSON line per event & append-only, \texttt{fsync}'d on write, never deleted \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\caption{The flight recorder. Tier B triggers include every safety trip, e-stop, mode transition,

content-hashed snapshot of the calibration and drive gains live at that instant, so a recording is
self-describing without external context.}
\label{tab:blackbox-tiers}
\end{table}

Two constraints shaped the implementation. The control loop must not pay for its own
instrumentation: the 200\,Hz thread only appends a tuple to a bounded queue ($\approx$2\,$\mu$s,
and \cref{tab:deploy-ab} confirms the end-to-end cost is unmeasurable), a separate writer thread
owns all file I/O, and a full queue \emph{drops and counts} --- the running drop count is stamped
into the next accepted record, because a black box that cannot prove where its own gaps are is
worse than none. And the robot must never depend on the recorder: if the writer thread dies, the
control loop continues and the failure is surfaced in the interface.

\paragraph{Time, without a trustworthy clock.} The Pi has no RTC and its access point has no
internet, so the wall clock after boot is whatever \texttt{fake-hwclock} restored --- plausible and
wrong. Every record therefore carries the monotonic clock as its primary time base, the wall time
plus an explicit \emph{trusted} flag (set only once NTP has actually synchronised), a per-boot
kernel identifier and a per-run session identifier, so records from different sessions can never be
silently interleaved. When NTP does land, the resulting step is itself logged ($+1333.5$\,s on the
first instrumented boot), so earlier stamps can be corrected offline. Power-off is handled by
inversion: a process cannot log its own death, so a heartbeat is \texttt{fsync}'d every 8\,s, and
the last heartbeat before a gap \emph{is} the power-off time, to heartbeat resolution --- an
inference the offline reader states explicitly rather than implying a clean shutdown.

\paragraph{The pre-move guard.} The incident's mechanism --- a full-authority slew against a stale
calibration --- is closed by four mechanisms, none of which adds a step to the normal workflow.
That last clause is a requirement, not a nicety: an early draft forbade homing as the first move
after calibration, and that was exactly backwards, because after a zero capture the legs are limp
and sagging and homing is precisely what catches them.

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{Hold before moving.} The first command after enabling is ``hold the raw position
  you are reporting right now'', for 0.35\,s. It never consults the calibration, so it is safe even
  against a completely wrong zero, and it arrests the sag immediately. Only then does the slew to
  an absolute target begin.
  \item \textbf{Raw-at-rest fingerprint.} The raw encoder pose of all six motors is recorded at
  every successful zero capture. Before leaving the limp state, the live raw pose is compared
  against it: decisive after a restart (the origin re-randomises, so a calibration restored from
  disk is refused until re-zeroed), while within a live session a 200\,Hz continuity watchdog is
  used instead --- an origin \emph{rewrite} moves the reported position without the drive reporting
  speed, which is exactly how it is distinguished from the joint being moved by hand. A range-sanity
  check backstops both: a calibration implying $678^\circ$ on an $\pm88^\circ$ axis is refused
  outright.
  \item \textbf{A homing-specific tracking threshold} ($8^\circ$ rather than the $25^\circ$
  playback value): a slow guided slew should track well, so a stale zero appears as tracking error
  within the first degrees of travel.
  \item \textbf{A per-joint travel budget} as the final backstop: a guided move that sweeps more
  than $1.3\times$ the joint's own range without arriving is stopped. On 2026-08-10 this alone
  would have cut the excursion from 1.9 output turns to under 0.6.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Acceptance.} The incident shape is reproduced in a hardware-free test: the mock bus
grew an \texttt{origin\_shift} fault injection that moves the reported \emph{and} commanded frame
while the shaft stays put and reports zero speed --- which is what a board renumbering its origin
actually looks like, and what makes it invisible to any check that trusts reported velocity. The
test captures a zero, shifts the origin, commands a home, and asserts that the guard refuses with
both raw poses on record, that the triggered dump contains at least ten seconds of pre-trigger
200\,Hz data, and that the offline reader's \texttt{--postmortem} mode states what happened ---
boot and power-loss times, the raw-pose comparison, commanded versus reported position through the
event, the configuration live at that instant, and whether the origin ever moved while the robot
was limp and untouched --- without a human opening a raw file.

\paragraph{What it found in its first hour.} The recorder justified itself before the day ended.
Its first boot on the robot captured its own deployment failure --- the \texttt{ENOBUFS} crash of
\cref{sec:deploy-can-runtime}, complete with traceback, which had previously been invisible ---
and its steady-state telemetry established that the left-leg bus was electrically dead (zero frames
transmitted, all three motors silent), turning ``the robot feels wrong'' into a specific,
timestamped fact. It also ended a longer-standing blindness: nothing in the stack had ever
measured the control loop's actual rate, so a loop running at a third of its nominal frequency had
been indistinguishable from a healthy one. The tick counter the recorder stamps into every sample
is what made every figure in \cref{tab:deploy-ab} measurable at all.

\subsection{Hardware experiments}
\label{sec:hw-experiments}

Everything in this section ran on the physical robot, through the web instrument of
\cref{sec:deploy}; the heavy measurement results are reported in \cref{sec:plant-id} and are only
situated here.

\paragraph{In-air gait demonstration and teach-and-replay.} The first hardware milestone was a
hand-authored straight-walking gait with the body clamped in the air (no balance): a pure-NumPy
gait generator streaming servo-mode position commands over CAN. Its safer successor is
teach-and-replay: the motors are held limp by streaming zero current, a leg is backdriven by
hand through the desired cycle, takes are recorded per side, and a pure-NumPy pipeline resamples,
phase-aligns, detects the left/right mirror, FFT-low-passes the loop closed, and replays it —
either as drive-side position commands or through a software PID onto current commands with a
hard ampere clamp as the torque limit. Replaying on the robot exposed the compliance problem
that later measurements explained: at low proportional gain the leg drew only $\sim$2\,A against
a 20\,A allowance and moved with stick--slip judder; the practical fix (raise $k_p$, add $k_i$
against the gravity lag, derivative on target-minus-actual velocity) was an early, unquantified
encounter with the drive-bandwidth ceiling of \cref{sec:plant-id-drive}.

\paragraph{Workspace calibration.} The safe joint-space workspace was recorded by backdriving
each leg through its physically assemblable region and eroding the recorded polygon; it does
triple duty as the training-time constraint, the runtime watchdog, and the offline deploy gate.
Calibration on hardware corrected the model in one substantial way: \textbf{the cam is a full
crank}. The CAD-guessed joint range of $\pm86^\circ$ is wrong — recorded sweeps span
$\sim$245$^\circ$ of cam rotation, the 4-bar assembling at every angle — and the calibrated zero
pose sits $\sim$1$^\circ$ outside the eroded safe band (so a naive hold-at-zero is refused by
the safety net while a guided homing move is allowed through the physical-feasibility check
instead; \cref{sec:deploy-blackbox}).

\paragraph{The identification campaign.} Quasi-static and swept-sine excitations, captured at
200\,Hz through the measurement mode of the web instrument, produced the plant corrections of
\cref{sec:plant-id}: the known-mass $K_t$ measurement, the 0.8\,Hz drive Bode, the gravity-model
residual and its rejected explanations. The campaign also demonstrated the instrument itself:
runs are self-describing (calibration, drive gains and weighed masses embedded in each capture),
and the excitation planner solves amplitude against travel, no-load speed and predicted tracking
error jointly after an early sweep e-stopped the run six seconds in by asking the drive for more
than its position loop can execute.

\paragraph{Sensor bench.} The IMU characterisation of \cref{sec:sensors} (noise,
latency, filter behaviour, and the motion-gating methodology that its first wrong numbers
forced) was performed in place on the robot, with the provenance-labelling scheme described
there.

\paragraph{The incident, and what it built.} On 2026-08-10 a stale calibration met a
full-authority absolute move and destroyed a joint (\cref{sec:deploy-blackbox}). The
investigation being impossible was itself the finding: it produced the flight recorder, the
pre-move guard, and the systemd supervision of \cref{sec:deploy}, all validated by reproducing
the incident against the mock CAN bus. The recorder's first hour on hardware then found a
daemon-killing crash at boot (unpowered CAN bus) and established that the left-leg bus was
electrically dead — the current state of the machine as this is written: right leg fully
functional under the 200\,Hz stack at 188\,Hz measured, left leg awaiting repair of the failed
joint.

\subsubsection{Static end-effector force map}
\label{sec:hw-force-map}

The cam actuator reaches the knee through the pushrod 4-bar, so the transmission from motor torque to
toe force is a strong function of posture. Whether the strong direction is the one running needs had
never been checked.

The constrained toe Jacobian differentiates the loop closure out, so it carries the linkage's
transmission ratio, and statics against the actuator torque box gives the feasible force set at every
reachable toe position. Self-tests check loop closure against the stance keyframe, the analytic
Jacobian against central differences, and a fast analytic ankle column against the full Jacobian.

Three findings, all statics-only and therefore bounding capability rather than predicting behaviour:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{The leg stands at 96\,\% of its reach}, with about 3\,mm of further extension available
  anywhere near the stance position. Everything else follows from sitting that close to the
  leg-extension singularity.
  \item \textbf{Force there is 19:1 anisotropic}: 16.4 body weights straight down, 0.85 body weights
  horizontally. This is the same near-singular corner the speed-ceiling analysis found from the power
  side, seen from the force side. It is a property of the fully-extended pose rather than of the whole
  workspace: over the running stance region the median tilted capacity recovers to 5.95\,BW, because
  compressed postures rotate their strong axis.
  \item \textbf{The motors are not the binding constraint; the ankle is, by a factor of eight.} Over
  the stance region the actuators give a 4.30\,BW median vertical capacity, and 77\,\% of the region
  clears the 3.5\,BW running requirement. The passive ankle gives a 0.42\,BW median, and
  \textbf{0.0\,\%} of the region clears it.
\end{itemize}

The last point is a third independent route to the conclusion of \cref{sec:rl-ankle-overturned} and
\cref{sec:speed-ceiling}, reached from statics with no learning and no dynamics: the linkage and
motor sizing are adequate to generous, and the foot spring is the design defect.
