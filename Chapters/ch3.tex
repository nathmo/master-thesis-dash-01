\section{Development Methodology}

\subsection{Overall methodology}
\label{sec:overall-method}

The project inherited a partially designed mechanical platform and proceeded in the following
order, each stage gating the next:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{Modelling and hardware selection.} Re-assembly of the CAD, export to a simulable
        model (\cref{sec:modelling}), and selection of the non-mechanical hardware: compute,
        sensing, battery (\cref{sec:hw-selection}).
  \item \textbf{Model validation.} Workspace calibration on the physical robot, then static
        validation, then, much later and with better instrumentation, full dynamic identification
        (\cref{sec:plant-id}). This stage carries the lesson of the project. It was done too late
        and too lightly at first, and every correction that followed moved the plant in the
        pessimistic direction.
  \item \textbf{Capability analysis.} A first-principles gait-feasibility sweep on the validated
        model (\cref{sec:speed-ceiling}), so that learned results have a ceiling to be judged
        against.
  \item \textbf{Toy problem.} A single-motor inverted pendulum carrying the complete
        train--export--deploy loop (\cref{sec:toy-problem}) before committing the 12-DOF robot
        to the same pipeline.
  \item \textbf{RL policy development.} Three action-space generations, a reflex architecture, a
        base-DOF-release curriculum, and a generator A/B, trained on the EPFL Izar cluster
        (\cref{sec:rl}).
  \item \textbf{Deployment.} The 200\,Hz control stack, calibration and safety machinery, and the
        flight recorder on the robot itself (\cref{sec:deploy}), plus the measurement campaign
        that fed the corrections of \cref{sec:plant-id} back into every earlier stage.
\end{enumerate}

Two cross-cutting disciplines proved more important than any single stage.

The first is \emph{adversarial review of anything that produces a number}. Rewards, simulators,
estimators and evaluators each produced a confident wrong answer before being caught
(\cref{sec:sim-hygiene,sec:plant-id-sysid}).

The second is \emph{measurement before assumption}. Three project-level decisions rested on assumed
numbers: the abandonment of torque control, the choice of a 50\,Hz control rate, and the question
of whether the ankle needed an actuator. Later measurement reversed or reframed all three.

\subsection{Hardware selection}
\label{sec:hw-selection}

\subsubsection{Motor}
\label{sec:hw-motor}

\paragraph{The selection instrument.} Actuator datasheets are not comparable as published. The
choice was therefore made against a purpose-built component database and explorer, rather than
against a spreadsheet of vendor headline
figures.\footnote{\url{https://github.com/nathmo/actuatorReview}} The database holds 140 commercial
integrated actuators, 121 motors, 121 gearboxes and 9 standalone drives, as four joined tables.
Every unit is decomposed into its motor layer, its gearbox layer and its drive layer, so that a
candidate can be \emph{composed} rather than only looked up. The modelling follows Lee et al.,
\emph{How to Model Brushless Electric Motors for the Design of Lightweight Robotic Systems}
(arXiv:2310.00080).

Two ideas from that paper are what make a selection possible.

\textbf{The motor constant is the only portable figure of merit.} The motor constant is
$K_m = K_t/\sqrt{R} = \tau/\sqrt{P_{\text{copper}}}$. It is invariant to operating current and to
rewinding, because rewinding $n$ turns gives $K_t \propto n$ while $R \propto n^2$. Unlike $K_t$ or
$K_v$, it can therefore be compared across vendors. It also sets continuous torque through the
thermal limit, as $\tau_{\text{cont}} = K_m\sqrt{\Delta T/R_{th}}$. Ranking candidates by $K_v$ is
the intuitive move, because $K_v$ sets top speed, but it compares winding choices rather than
motors.

\textbf{Every electrical constant is meaningless without its reference frame.} A $K_t$, $K_v$ or
$R$ can be quoted phase, line-to-line, q-axis, bus or RMS, in wye or in delta, and each vendor
picks a different convention. The same physical motor differs by up to $\sqrt{3/2}$, or by a factor
of 3, depending on which convention is used. The database therefore stores a raw value plus an
explicit frame tag, and normalises on the fly. Conversions that stay under-determined are flagged
rather than guessed. The point is not pedantic: \cref{sec:hw-motor-critique} shows that it lands
directly on this project's own $K_t$ measurement.

Each leg carries three actuators. All six are CubeMars quasi-direct-drive units with an integrated
drive that speaks a servo-mode CAN protocol.

The no-load speeds follow from the 48\,V bus. The AK60-39 is a KV80 motor, so
$80\,\mathrm{rpm/V} \times 48\,\mathrm{V} / 39 = 10.3$\,rad/s at the joint. The AKE90-8 is KV35,
which gives $35 \times 48 / 8 = 210$\,rpm, or 22\,rad/s. The sagittal pair dominates the robot's
capability envelope. The capability analysis of \cref{sec:speed-ceiling} finds 87\,\% of feasible
gaits limited at the cam actuator's peak-power corner, which makes \emph{no-load speed} rather than
peak torque the axis a successor should buy. The low reduction ratios also keep reflected inertia
low enough for the legs to be backdriven by hand. The teach-and-replay gait recording of
\cref{sec:hw-experiments} depends on that property.

The pattern in the table is the design lesson, not the winner. Peak mechanical power per kilogram
saturates around 450--670\,W/kg whatever the vendor or the price. What varies is \emph{where on the
torque--speed line} a product places that power, and the reduction ratio is the knob. Strain-wave
and high-ratio planetary units buy torque density and positioning accuracy at a catastrophic cost
in speed. The eRob110i carries a peak torque comparable to the AKE90-8's, costs three times as
much, weighs 1.8 times as much, and delivers one twelfth the mass-specific power, because 50:1 puts
its no-load speed at 6.3\,rad/s. For an industrial arm that is the right trade. For a running leg
it is fatal, and it is the trade most of the actuator market is built around.

\paragraph{The choice was correct, and correct for the stated reason.} That distinction matters,
because buying the same part for the wrong reason would have been easy. A selection made on
\emph{torque} density would have chosen the MyActuator RMD-X15, at 129\,Nm/kg peak against the
AKE90-8's 121, and lost 49\,\% of joint speed. A selection made on \emph{rated} torque density
would have taken the database's own headline ranking (the MAB MA-p-100-30 at 45.5\,Nm/kg
continuous, or the MyActuator EPS-RH-25-100 at 44.6) and lost 54\,\% and 86\,\% of joint speed
respectively. That headline ranking is a trap for this application, and it is the first thing the
tool shows. The one metric that selects the AKE90-8 is the product $\tau\omega$, and
\cref{sec:speed-ceiling} independently confirms that $\tau\omega$ is what binds.

\paragraph{The coupled torque--speed curve, now that $R$ is known.} An earlier draft of this
section recorded that the simulation applies independent torque and speed caps but \emph{no
coupled torque--speed curve}, on the grounds that the phase resistance was unavailable. That claim
was wrong. The resistance is published and the database ingested it: $R_{ll} = 0.164\,\Omega$ for
the AKE90-8 and $0.60\,\Omega$ for the AK60-39. Both sit in the same line-to-line frame as the
quoted $K_t$, and that pairing makes $K_m = K_t/\sqrt{R}$ exact without knowing the winding.
CubeMars' own published $K_m$ of 0.674 reproduces $K_t/\sqrt{R_{ll}}$ to five digits, which
cross-validates both numbers.

With $R$ in hand the envelope can be stated rather than bounded. Back-EMF plus $IR$ exhausts the
48\,V bus at $K_b\omega_m = 48 - (72 \times 0.164) = 36.2$\,V. That puts the corner at
$36.2/0.272 = 133$\,rad/s at the motor, or \textbf{16.6\,rad/s at the joint}. Below the corner the
actuator is current-limited and the torque cap is flat. Above it, torque falls linearly to zero at
22\,rad/s. Every operating point the capability analysis actually visits lies on the flat branch
with roughly a factor of two in torque to spare, the worst case being 64\,N$\cdot$m at 13.7\,rad/s.
The constant force range in simulation is therefore not merely defensible but correct, and the
omission previously flagged as ``optimistic in the fast-running regime'' is smaller than feared.
What remains genuinely unmodelled is the fall-off between 16.6 and 22\,rad/s. That fall-off is now
a five-line addition to the model rather than an unmeasurable unknown.

\subsubsection{What the actuator selection got wrong}
\label{sec:hw-motor-critique}

Three criticisms survive the vindication above. They are given in increasing order of consequence.

\textbf{The hip-roll actuator was chosen on the wrong axis, harmlessly.} Its class is 45 to
130\,N$\cdot$m under 1.1\,kg, and within that class the AK60-39 ranks 10th of 18 on mass-specific
power, at 246\,W/kg against 472\,W/kg for the CubeMars AK10-9. It was bought for torque density, at
96\,Nm/kg peak, which is genuinely near the top of the class. Hip roll is not a propulsive axis, so
its low speed costs nothing measurable. The real cost lies elsewhere and was not anticipated. The
39:1 reduction gives the part 0.046\,kg$\cdot$m$^2$ of reflected armature inertia, more than double
the sagittal pair's, plus a published 1.4\,N$\cdot$m of back-drive torque. The teach-and-replay
workflow of \cref{sec:hw-experiments} depends on backdriving the legs by hand, and the hip-roll
axis is the one that resists. A 16:1 part, the AKH70-16 at 78\,N$\cdot$m, 0.88\,kg and EUR\,525,
would have kept the torque and cut the ratio-squared inertia amplification by
$(39/16)^2 \approx 6$, for EUR\,132 more per side. The error is small, real, and cheap to fix.

\textbf{The measured 85\,\% ``gearbox efficiency'' may not be gearbox efficiency.} The database's
central methodological warning applies with force to this project's own measurement. The datasheet
$K_t$ is tagged line-to-line. The drive reports a current over CAN whose reference frame is
documented nowhere and was never established. If the drive reports q-axis current, then the
correct comparator is $K_t^q = \sqrt{3/2}\,K_t^{ll} = 0.333$, and the measured effective constant
is 69\,\% of datasheet rather than 85\,\%. No planetary gearbox explains 69\,\%. If the drive
reports bus current instead, the discrepancy moves the other way. A single-stage 8:1 planetary
should run at 94--97\,\%, so 85\,\% was always slightly suspicious, and it was accepted only
because it sat comfortably inside a plausible range. The data already recorded cannot separate the
two hypotheses. The experiment that separates them is trivial: command a known current into a
stalled motor and measure the DC bus current with a clamp meter. Until that is done, the peak
torques of \cref{tab:motors} carry an unquantified frame risk, on top of the geometric uncertainty
\cref{sec:plant-id} already acknowledges. \textbf{The correction, if it is one, is pessimistic.}

\textbf{The drive was the mistake, and it was a separable purchase.} The single largest sim-to-real
gap in this project is the drive's proportional-only 0.8\,Hz position loop with unwritable gains
(\cref{sec:plant-id-drive}). Whose fault that is deserves precision. The database records the
AKE90-8 as a bare quasi-direct-drive module, sold with no encoder and no drive board. The drive
electronics were therefore an independent decision, not an inseparable consequence of choosing the
best motor in the field. The database's drive table lists at least two alternatives that were
available at the time and would have removed the problem entirely. The mjbots moteus-n1 offers
100\,A peak, 10--54\,V, 14.6\,g, EUR\,137, open-source firmware and an integrated absolute encoder.
The MAB MD80 v3.0 offers 80\,A peak, 48\,V or 60\,V variants, 15\,g, EUR\,220, FDCAN at 8\,Mbit,
and position, velocity, impedance and torque modes with writable gains. Either would have cost
\emph{less} than the bundled CubeMars electronics and exposed a torque mode with a known loop. The
60\,V MD80 would also have raised the bus to 60\,V, for a 25\,\% gain on the no-load speed that
\cref{sec:speed-ceiling} identifies as the binding axis.

The counter-argument is real and should not be waved away. A bare module plus a third-party drive
requires mounting and calibrating an encoder, commissioning firmware, and tuning a commutation loop
per joint. That is six times over, before a single line of control code runs, on a one-person
thesis with a fixed end date. The integrated option was plug-and-play with a documented CAN
protocol, and it bought perhaps three weeks of schedule. Against a nine-month project that is a
defensible trade.

What is \emph{not} defensible is that the trade was made without asking the question. No vendor
publishes a servo-loop bandwidth, so the figure could not have been looked up. It could have been
measured on a single unit in an afternoon, with the swept-sine rig that eventually measured it a
year later. And the cost of being wrong was not three weeks. It was every policy trained in the
project. The database captures peak torque, rated torque, speed, mass, price, IP rating and
communications. \textbf{It has no column for control-loop bandwidth or for gain writability},
because nobody knew to ask when it was built. That omission is the most useful thing this project
learned about buying actuators. It is recorded here as the schema's first missing field, not as a
datasheet the vendor should have written.

\paragraph{A flagged inconsistency for the corrected sweep.} The capability analysis of
\cref{sec:speed-ceiling} caps the cam actuator at $P \approx \tau_0\omega_0/4 \approx 935$\,W. That
expression is the maximum of a linear torque--speed line running from stall torque to no-load
speed, which is the envelope of a voltage-limited brushed motor. It is not the envelope of a
current-limited FOC drive. That envelope is flat at $\tau_{\text{pk}}$ up to the 16.6\,rad/s corner
derived above, so it peaks near $170 \times 16.6 \approx 2.8$\,kW. CubeMars' own peak-power curve
for the part is annotated 1500\,W. The burst figures of \cref{tab:speed-ceiling} may therefore be
\emph{pessimistic} by a factor between 1.6 and 3, which is the only correction in this entire
chapter that moves in the optimistic direction. Two cautions keep it from being good news. The
55\,N$\cdot$m continuous rating sets the thermally sustained figure, that figure does not move at
all, and it is the sustained figure that decides whether a record is reachable
(\cref{sec:speed-ceiling-corrected}). The 0.8\,Hz drive bandwidth also caps achievable cadence far
below any of these power limits. The discrepancy is recorded rather than resolved, because
resolving it means re-running the sweep with a proper envelope.
\Cref{sec:speed-ceiling-corrected} lists that re-run among the outstanding items.

\paragraph{Cost, delivery and assembly.} Four non-performance constraints bounded the search, and
they are recorded here for completeness.

\emph{Cost.} Six actuators at EUR\,424 and EUR\,393 come to EUR\,2482, roughly half the hardware
budget. The strain-wave alternatives in \cref{tab:actuator-field} would have cost EUR\,7000 to
EUR\,12000 for strictly worse running performance, so price and performance pointed the same way
for once.

\emph{Delivery.} CubeMars ships to Switzerland in two to three weeks, against quote-only lead times
of two to three months for Maxon's HEJ series. A slipped actuator delivery is a slipped project,
which is exactly why the database records list price and stock channel.

\emph{Assembly.} The integrated units bolt to a face and expose a four-wire CAN and power harness.
A bare module instead needs an encoder mount, a drive enclosure and a thermal path at every joint.
With 12 joints' worth of harness already in the base, that was a real consideration, and it pulled
the same way as the schedule argument above.

\emph{Effort.} The database itself cost about a week to build and populate across eight vendors.
Such a cost is justifiable only because the result is reusable. It is published as a standalone
tool, and anyone who disagrees with its verdict on this robot's actuators can reproduce that
verdict.

\subsubsection{Battery pack}
\label{sec:hw-battery}

The motor selection fixes the electrical requirement: six drives on a nominal 48\,V bus, with
short, violent current peaks. That is a sprint robot's duty cycle, not a marathon robot's. Usain
Bolt needs under ten seconds for 100\,m, and this robot would be delighted with thirty. The pack is
therefore optimised for \emph{power} density rather than for energy density.

\paragraph{The selection instrument.} As with the actuators, the choice was made against a
purpose-built database and explorer rather than against a
shortlist.\footnote{\url{https://github.com/nathmo/batteryReview}} It holds roughly 430 distinct
cell models, 54 BMS modules and 110 commercial packs, and every row carries a source URL. It also
synthesises packs from \texttt{cell $+$ BMS $+$ (nS, mP)}, so that ``what I could build'' plots on
the same axes as ``what I can buy''. Two of its conventions matter for what follows.

\textbf{Current is a curve, not a number.} The convention is the direct analogue of the actuator
database's rated, peak and momentary torque tiers. Every current rating carries a duration stamp
and falls into one of three tiers. \emph{Continuous} is the thermal steady state. \emph{Pulse at
$t$} is the thermal transient, interpolated as $I(t) = \sqrt{I_{\text{cont}}^2 +
(I_{\text{pulse}}^2 - I_{\text{cont}}^2)\,t_{\text{pulse}}/t}$. \emph{Fault} is the protection
trip, which is a disconnection event and never usable power. A rating quoted without its duration
fits no tier and is not a specification.

\textbf{Estimated values are marked as such.} Roughly half the cell rows carry at least one field
inferred rather than read off a datasheet. Such a field is written \texttt{ESTIMATED<n>}, so that
it still parses and can be filtered out. Every figure quoted below comes from a datasheet-sourced
row.

\paragraph{What the database says about the pack that was built.} The pack is \textbf{13S2P} of
\textbf{EVE INR21700-50PL}: 48.1\,V nominal, 10\,Ah, 468\,Wh and 1.87\,kg of cells. Its BMS is
rated 200\,A continuous, and a software cap limits the peak to 250\,A.
% TODO(nathann): confirm BMS make/model if you want it named.
The database corrects the specification the pack was sized against, and the correction is not
cosmetic. The 50PL is a 5.0\,Ah cell at \textbf{50\,A continuous} and \textbf{180\,A for 2\,s}.
This project sized the pack on a 125\,A figure, which is a marketed peak with \emph{no published
duration}. That is precisely the class of number the tier convention exists to reject. The pack's
real ratings are therefore those of \cref{tab:pack-tiers}.

Two consequences follow. First, the BMS is \emph{not} the binding element at continuous, as was
assumed. The cells are, at half the BMS's rating, so the pack is over-specified on the BMS and
under-specified on the cells. Second, the 250\,A software cap is a 2-second-tier number enforced
with no duration logic. A policy drawing 250\,A for thirty seconds runs the cells at 25\,C against
a 10\,C continuous rating, and nothing in the stack notices. That is a real safety gap, and the fix
is cheap: an $I^2t$ accumulator in the control daemon rather than a scalar clamp.

The question the sizing should have asked is whether 4.8\,kW continuous suffices. Six actuators at
the capability analysis's worst observed operating point draw a few kilowatts electrical, so the
pack is adequate. The margin is near 1.2 to 1.6$\times$, however, not the 3$\times$ the 250\,A
figure implied.

\paragraph{Chemistry: right answer, wrong reasons.} The original rationale ran as follows. LTO and
high-rate LiPo are nominally more power-dense, but neither could be sourced as bare cells from a
reputable supplier, LTO surfacing only on AliExpress and LiPo being unavailable packaged at 48\,V.
High-rate 21700 NMC was therefore the practical space. Sweeping the supplier landscape shows both
halves of that reasoning to be wrong, and they fail in opposite directions.

\textbf{LTO was rejected for a bad reason, and the rejection was correct anyway.} EU-warehouse LTO
specialists do exist, and the survey found them. SeLian Energy and BatteryFinds both stock Yinlong
cells in Germany and Poland with published prices, and ELERIX and GWL sell into the EU from
Czechia. The provenance objection therefore does not hold. What kills LTO is the number nobody
checked: on the axis that matters it is \emph{not} more power-dense. The best LTO cell in the
database is the Toshiba 20\,Ah SCiB at 932\,W/kg and 93\,Wh/kg, while the chosen NMC cell delivers
2500\,W/kg and 250\,Wh/kg. LTO's reputation for power comes from its C-rate, and C-rate is a ratio.
Its 2.3\,V cell voltage and heavy steel construction throw the advantage away in absolute terms. A
48\,V LTO pack needs 21S at 2.3\,V, and at 1.24\,kg per Yinlong cell that is a \textbf{26\,kg}
battery on a 15\,kg robot. The 21S floor makes the outcome inescapable. The lightest LTO cell in
the database is a 1.5\,Ah 18650 at 45\,g, and 21S of those carries only 76\,Wh, so reaching 480\,Wh
at 48\,V still costs 6.6\,kg. No LTO configuration in the database clears the requirement under
6\,kg. The chemistry was excluded on sourcing when it should have been excluded on mass. That
matters, because the sourcing argument would have collapsed under thirty seconds of scrutiny while
the mass argument is unanswerable.

\textbf{LiPo was rejected for a bad reason too, and this one changes the answer.} High-rate LiPo is
sourceable as bare 1S cells, from \textbf{Swaytronic in Niederlenz}. That is a Swiss domestic
supplier: no import, no customs, no VAT handling, and two-day delivery. It is the \emph{easiest}
supplier in the entire survey rather than the hardest. Its 1S cells are rated 25\,C continuous and
50\,C burst. Because the cells are 1S, the ``no packaged 48\,V module'' objection dissolves, since
series count is then a free parameter. \Cref{tab:pack-alt} puts the two builds side by side.

On the project's own stated optimisation criterion, the built pack was the wrong pick.

Three honest counterweights exist, and none of them was the actual reason at the time.

\emph{Cycle life.} High-rate LiPo runs a few hundred cycles against 500 to 1000 for a 21700, and
this robot is a lab platform that will see thousands of trials.

\emph{Assembly.} Twenty-six cylindrical cells spot-weld into a rigid, self-supporting brick that
carries load in the base. Thirteen pouch cells instead need a compression fixture, a case, and tabs
joined without cooking them. That is more work, and it fails worse.

\emph{Lab safety.} High-rate LiPo attracts storage and charging restrictions that a cylindrical NMC
pack behind a certified BMS does not. A battery that cannot be left charging overnight in the lab
carries a schedule cost that no datasheet shows.

Taken together, the three are a genuine defence of the built pack. They are not the defence that
was recorded. ``We chose NMC because LiPo was unsourceable'' and ``we chose NMC because pouch cells
are a fire-safety and assembly liability we did not want on a shared bench'' differ by the
difference between an error and a trade-off.

\paragraph{Build versus buy.} Building was necessary, and the database establishes that rather than
assuming it. Of 110 commercial packs surveyed, the 48\,V space holds almost only two things. The
first is stationary storage: 16S LFP rack and wall units from 5 to 32\,kWh, weighing 29 to 370\,kg,
useless here. The second is e-bike packs. The closest commercial match to the requirement is a
Swaytronic 48\,V 13S3P 10.35\,Ah at 497\,Wh, 2.2\,kg and EUR\,485. That is essentially the pack
this project built, at twice the cost and with no assembly labour. It fails on exactly one axis: as
an e-bike pack it is rated in the 40\,A class, roughly 2.5$\times$ short of the requirement.
\textbf{Nothing in the commercial 48\,V market is built for a sprint duty cycle.} That finding is
what justifies building a pack at all, and it took a 110-row survey to state rather than assume.

\paragraph{Cost, delivery and assembly.} Four constraints again bounded the choice.

\emph{Cost.} EUR\,96 of cells, plus roughly EUR\,90 to EUR\,165 for a 13S 200\,A smart BMS (JBD and
JKBMS both list suitable units), plus nickel strip and holders. Call it EUR\,250 against EUR\,485
for the nearest commercial pack. That is a saving, but it was not the reason.

\emph{Delivery.} The surveyed supplier space is stratified by border, not by price. A Swiss
supplier ships in two days. EU sellers ship in under a week, with Swiss import VAT at the border.
China-direct suppliers ship from EU warehouses in one to three days, but air freight frequently
bars loose cells. US shops offer the best transparency and the lowest minimum order quantity of
anyone, but they cannot legally air-freight loose lithium to Europe, and were excluded on that
basis alone.

\emph{Minimum order quantity.} Single cells are available for 18650 and 21700 NMC. LTO ships in
six-cell matched sets and large-format LFP in four-packs. The minimum order quantity, not the
price, is what actually gates a prototyper out of a chemistry.

\emph{Assembly.} Twenty-six spot welds of nickel strip take an afternoon with a lab welder, and
yield a structural element. That constraint favoured cylindrical cells most quietly of all, and it
was never written down as a criterion.

\paragraph{Voltage sag is probably the largest unmeasured quantity on the robot.} One consequence
of the \emph{drive} selection became visible only once the CAN traffic was sniffed
(\cref{sec:deploy-can}). The drives publish position, speed, current, temperature and an error
flag, but \textbf{no bus voltage}. Pack sag under a sprint is therefore not observable from the
drives at all. Observing it would need external sensing on the Pi or on the BMS. Until such sensing
exists, the simulation models voltage droop as a randomised within-episode torque derating
(\cref{sec:sim2real-lineage}) rather than as a measured quantity.

The database's internal-resistance model shows that sag is not a second-order effect, and connects
it to the actuator argument above. A pack's sag ceiling is
$(V_{\text{nom}} - V_{\text{cutoff}})/R$, and a 13S2P arrangement multiplies cell resistance by
$13/2 = 6.5$. The 50PL row carries \emph{no} datasheet internal resistance, and the database flags
that gap rather than filling it. A high-drain 21700 sits around 12 to 15\,m$\Omega$, which puts the
pack near 80 to 100\,m$\Omega$. At the 100\,A continuous tier that is 8 to 10\,V of sag. At the
184\,A ten-second tier it is 15 to 18\,V, which takes a nominally 48\,V bus down toward 30\,V.

If that estimate is even approximately right, it is the largest unmeasured effect on the
robot. \Cref{sec:speed-ceiling} establishes that no-load speed is the binding axis, and no-load
speed is \emph{linear in bus voltage}. A 30\,\% sag is therefore a 30\,\% loss of joint speed,
arriving exactly when the policy asks for speed, on a robot that cannot see it happen. Sag also
sharpens the LiPo comparison of \cref{tab:pack-alt} in the same direction. A 13S1P pack multiplies
cell resistance by 13 rather than by 6.5, but its cells carry roughly an order of magnitude less
resistance each, so its sag would be a fraction of the figures above.

The figures in this paragraph are an inference from a typical cell resistance, not a measurement,
and they are presented as such. The number is not the point. The point is that the cheapest
remaining experiment in the project addresses a plant parameter with more leverage on top speed
than most of what \cref{sec:plant-id} did measure. That experiment is a voltmeter across the pack
during a sprint, or a single datasheet lookup for the 50PL's DC internal resistance. That it went
unmeasured is the failure mode this chapter documents everywhere else: the quantity nobody
instrumented is the quantity that moved.

\subsubsection{Compute}
\label{sec:compute-choice}

Most of the laboratory's exoskeletons run on a BeagleBone Black. The board supports CAN natively
and runs Linux, but its SSH connection was too slow to work with, so it was rejected. Its main
selling point is the real-time coprocessor, a separate microcontroller that runs alongside the
main CPU for time-critical tasks. That coprocessor needs custom firmware, and its performance
benefit for this application is still to be demonstrated.

A Raspberry Pi already in the laboratory was used instead, because I had far more experience with
that board. The choice required buying a CAN hat that talks over SPI. A second hat carrying an
accelerometer, the Waveshare Sense HAT (B), was bought for ease of integration.

% TODO(nathann): record the pin-mapping check needed when the two hats are stacked, here or in the
% deployment section.

The board is a \textbf{Raspberry Pi 3 Model B Rev 1.2}: four Cortex-A53 cores, aarch64, kernel
6.12, running a stock non-realtime Linux. An \texttt{mcp251xfd} controller over SPI provides CAN,
configured as \textbf{two independent 1\,Mbit buses}. Bus \texttt{can0} serves the right leg and
\texttt{can1} serves the left, with three motors each: CAN id 104 for hip roll, 105 for cam and 106
for thigh. The arbitration id encodes both node and command, as \texttt{id | (command << 8)}.

The Pi also hosts a WiFi access point and a web interface, which drives the robot during bring-up
and removes the need for a tethered console. That interface is not only a convenience. It is the
instrument through which most of the measurements in \cref{sec:plant-id} were taken.

This section originally left the loop-rate and jitter question open, and \cref{sec:plant-id-timing}
settles it by measurement. The Pi sustains a closed 200\,Hz control loop at half its time budget,
and 200\,Hz is the correct deployment rate.

estimates. Contact sensors were considered, because the literature suggests that impact timing and

Two properties matter more for control than the noise figures do.

\textbf{Latency.} The path from a physical event to a published value takes 10 to 20\,ms. It
comprises the DLPF group delay (5 to 10\,ms, from the datasheet, not verified on this part),
output-rate quantisation (0 to 4.3\,ms), the I2C read (1.7\,ms) and the poll period (0 to 5\,ms).
Against a 5\,ms control period, that is two to four whole control steps. The effect is larger than
the sensor noise, and the simulation models it as such.

\textbf{The attitude filter dominates, not the sensor.} A 6-axis Madgwick filter with
$\beta = 0.06$\,rad/s runs at the poll rate. Its accelerometer correction is rate-limited to
roughly $3.4^\circ$/s whatever the size of the error. The published attitude is therefore
overwhelmingly gyro-driven, and the accelerometer acts as a slow trim. Roll and pitch are
gravity-referenced and absolute. Yaw is pure gyro integration and drifts, so the gyro zero must be
captured on a stationary robot each session. A policy that consumes fused attitude rather than raw
rates forces the simulation to model this filter, not merely the sensor.

The magnetometer is deliberately not fused. It sits centimetres from two brushless motors and the
battery, and its reading moves when they do.

\paragraph{A methodological trap worth recording.} Noise figures taken while the robot swayed on
its test rig measure the sway, and nothing in the resulting number says so. That mistake produced a
yaw-axis noise figure roughly $8\times$ too high. It also produced a filter sweep in which a
\emph{narrower} low-pass appeared to measure \emph{more} noise. Both results looked like plausible
sensor data. The benchmark tool now gates every noise figure on a peak-to-peak motion check. Every
published figure also carries a provenance label: measured at rest, motion-independent,
datasheet-unverified, or uncharacterised. The uncharacterised list is published rather than guessed
at. It holds the temperature dependence of the gyro bias, vibration rectification under footfall
impact, accelerometer cross-axis sensitivity, and clipping behaviour at $\pm4$\,g during real
impacts.

\paragraph{Getting the rate the sensor was configured for.} The IMU loop initially reached only 190
to 194\,Hz against a nominal 200\,Hz. The missing ticks were not the IMU's. The air, pressure,
light and ADC channels were being read inline on the same thread, and those drivers spend nearly
all their time asleep waiting for a conversion. The humidity sensor alone sleeps 15\,ms, which is
three whole control ticks. Moving the slow chips to a second thread, with a lock serialising only
the bus transfers, brought the IMU to 199.5\,Hz. As a side effect it reduced the CAN daemon's late
ticks from 11.5\,\% to 9.8\,\%. The remaining slips are operating-system scheduling.
\subsection{Robot modelling}
\label{sec:modelling}

The robot was re-assembled in Autodesk Inventor, chosen for fluency. Each limb was exported as
STEP with its origin centred on its joint, then assembled in Fusion~360 and exported to URDF with a
plugin. The path was not smooth. The detours are worth recording, because they are the normal
experience of CAD-to-simulation work.

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{Inertia matrices from the mechanical engineer's SolidWorks model arrived in the
        wrong orientation}, and after repeated attempts the SolidWorks URDF export never yielded a
        usable file. The working pipeline therefore validates every CAD-supplied inertia tensor
        against its reference frame explicitly, rather than trusting the export.
  \item \textbf{URDF cannot express this robot at all} (\cref{sec:simulation}). The parallel knee
        closes a kinematic loop, so a URDF is at best an open-tree approximation with a floating
        pushrod. The actual model is MJCF with an explicit \texttt{<equality><connect>}. Both
        files are exported and kept, and only the MJCF is simulated.
  \item \textbf{Validation is a tool, not an inspection.} A dedicated tool discovers plausible
        inertia matrices from the physical robot, by moving the legs in the air with the base
        fixed, inside the calibrated safe workspace, then compares them against the CAD values.
        \Cref{sec:plant-id-sysid} reports the full dynamic identification this grew into. Its
        bottom line for the model has two halves. The CAD centres of mass \emph{survive}
        scrutiny, because a free 18-parameter fit explains at most 11 to 19\,\% of the observed
        gravity residual, so the CAD values were kept. The link inertia \emph{tensors} remain CAD
        placeholders scaled by measured mass ratios, which is why the domain-randomisation range
        on inertia stays at $\pm$25\,\% while the mass ranges narrowed.
  \item \textbf{The kinematic model is consistent with the measured robot.} The MJCF closes the
        4-bar at 100\,\% of the backdriven-workspace poses recorded on the real robot: 1383 of
        1383 on the left, 1406 of 1406 on the right, at a residual of $10^{-14}$. The test is
        one-sided, since it would catch only a model band \emph{smaller} than reality, but it is
        reassuring.
  \item \textbf{The exported model is not left/right symmetric, and symmetrising it was tried and
        reverted.} An audit found 17 of 30 mirror-compared quantities off, including a
        $1.2^\circ$ rotation in one hip frame and sub-millimetre centre-of-mass offsets. The
        near-singular 4-bar amplifies those into $6.5^\circ$ of hip-roll difference in the
        standing keyframe. A tool that mirrors the model exactly was built, reducing the worst
        error from $1.2^\circ$ to $2\times10^{-5}$, and it was \emph{not} applied. Three findings
        stopped it. The crooked stance survives symmetrisation, because the gravity settle
        bifurcates into a lean of about $7^\circ$ even from a symmetric start, so the asymmetry
        only chose \emph{which way}. An unexplained regression in the pitch-reflex kick-arrest
        test failed the smoke gate. And the deployment lineage had already chosen to
        \emph{randomise} the asymmetry rather than deny it (\cref{sec:sim2real-lineage}). The tool
        is kept. The premise that the asymmetry was the bug was measured to be wrong.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Simulation}
\label{sec:simulation}

Simulation runs in MuJoCo. The robot's topology forced that choice more than preference did.

\subsubsection{URDF cannot describe this robot}

The parallel knee closes a kinematic loop. The cam actuator drives a pushrod whose distal end ties
to the shin below the knee, so hip, cam, pushrod, shin and thigh form a planar 4-bar. URDF is a
\emph{tree} format and cannot express that closure. The exported URDF therefore describes a
different robot, one with a floating pushrod. Any simulator that consumes it directly produces
dynamics that are plausible and wrong.

MuJoCo's MJCF closes the loop with an explicit \texttt{<equality><connect>} constraint. Both an
open-tree URDF and a closed MJCF are exported and kept, and only the MJCF is used for physics. The
practical consequence for control is discussed further in \cref{sec:rl-cpg}. Cam
and thigh are \emph{not} independent. The loop assembles over only a diagonal band of the nominal
joint box, and a target commanded outside that band is not merely hard to track but physically
impossible.

\subsubsection{Compute backend}

Physics runs on the CPU throughout, because this stack has no GPU physics backend. Training
nonetheless runs measurably faster with the policy network on a GPU: 2900 environment-steps per
second against 1530 on the same cluster node, a factor of 1.9. Matrix throughput is not the cause.
The network is a two-layer MLP whose forward pass costs 117\,$\mu$s even on the robot's Raspberry
Pi. The cause is core contention. PyTorch on the CPU steals cores from the parallel MuJoCo workers,
and moving the network off the CPU returns those cores to physics.

\subsubsection{Establishing that a simulation result is real}
\label{sec:sim-hygiene}

The reward function demanded a particular discipline, described in \cref{sec:rl-position}, where
the policy exploited every loophole it was offered. The \emph{physics} turns out to demand the same
discipline. A simulation bug does not usually announce itself. It produces a clean, converged,
entirely plausible learning curve for a robot that does not exist. Four examples follow. All four
were found while setting up a single ankle study, and each would have silently produced a wrong
answer.

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{Joint limits are not rigid by default.} MuJoCo's default limit softness let body
        weight push $3.4^\circ$ past the stop of a supposedly rigid strut. That is a rubber bar,
        not a rigid one, and it would have quietly given the study's rigid arm a compliant ankle.
        Stiffening the constraint reduced the penetration to $0.08^\circ$.
  \item \textbf{A constraint that ships inactive is inactive.} The rigid arm re-settled its stance
        with a \emph{free} ankle, because the locking equalities switch on only at episode reset.
        The arm was being initialised in a pose its episodes never visit, 268\,mm lower than
        intended.
  \item \textbf{A safety criterion measured in the wrong frame.} The workspace termination
        measures the toe in the \emph{base} frame, so it cannot distinguish a collapsed ankle from
        a folded 4-bar. One arm stood outside the threshold from its own reset pose, and every
        episode terminated 0.1\,s in. The criterion had to be re-referenced, per arm, to that
        arm's own settled stance.
  \item \textbf{A stale keyframe.} The shipped stance is an equilibrium of one particular ankle
        law. Arms with a softer ankle start away from equilibrium and lurch at every reset.
\end{itemize}

A fifth class is environmental rather than physical. The local development machine ran MuJoCo
3.7.0 and the cluster ran 3.3.7. A weld constraint that held on the newer solver \emph{gave way} on
the older one, drifting 0.86\,rad over a 60-step rollout. The ``rigid'' arm was silently compliant
on the cluster alone. Anything solver-marginal must therefore be tuned against the older version.

The countermeasures are structural rather than heroic. A smoke-test suite builds and briefly runs
all 123 training presets before any long launch. Self-tests inside the analysis tools check
analytic Jacobians against finite differences, and loop closure against the model's own keyframe.
Assertions fail loudly when a plant does not match what its preset claims it is.

\subsubsection{Mass}

The weighed masses of \cref{tab:masses} were baked into the simulation model, replacing the CAD
placeholders. The inertia tensors were scaled by the per-body mass ratio, and the stance keyframe
was re-settled under the new load.

The decision worth recording is that the correction was \emph{not} made an experimental variable.
An earlier arrangement kept a CAD plant and a measured plant as two selectable model files. Every
preset that had not explicitly opted in therefore kept training on the 12.83\,kg robot, and that
was all of them but two. Splitting the plant in two had made the wrong one the default. There is
one robot, it weighs 15.14\,kg, and the model file says so.

The accepted cost is that warm-starting a new run from a pre-correction checkpoint is now a
cross-plant transfer. Those weights, and their observation normalisation statistics, were fitted to
a robot 18\,\% lighter, with a shin 2.6 times too light.

\subsubsection{Peak torque is what the gearbox delivers, not what the datasheet quotes}

The actuator force ranges in the model carried the datasheet peak torque, which is the torque at an
ideal torque constant. The gearbox does not deliver it.

$K_t$ was measured by clamping a known 1.552\,kg mass to the shin and repeating a quasi-static
sweep with the mass and without it. The method matters. Fitting the two runs \emph{together} with
an indicator column makes the mass term's coefficient exactly $1/K_t$, because a known mass on a
known kinematic lever arm involves no link centre of mass anywhere. That breaks a degeneracy which
had defeated the identification until then. Otherwise $K_t$ and the gravity-model gain are
perfectly confounded, and only their product is observable.

The effective joint $K_t$ came out at 82 to 89\,\% of datasheet, consistent with a gearbox at
roughly 85\,\% efficiency. Peak torque scales with $K_t$ at the same peak current, so the model was
updated. The measured cam and thigh actuators moved from 170 to 144.5\,N$\cdot$m. The hip-roll
actuator moved from 72 to 61.2\,N$\cdot$m. The second figure is the same efficiency
\emph{transferred}, not measured, because no datasheet $K_t$ is published for that part to fit
against. It is probably optimistic, because a 39:1 reduction needs more gear stages than an 8:1.

The remaining uncertainty is geometric rather than physical. The mass was clamped by hand, $K_t$
trades against its position at about 1.9\,\% per centimetre, and the perpendicular standoff of the
clamp is known only to 5 to 10\,cm. Across that box $K_t$ spans 82 to 98\,\% of datasheet. Settling
the figure needs the mass on the shin centreline at a measured distance, not more analysis of the
existing data.

\label{sec:plant-id-drive}

invisible until a swept-sine excitation was run on the assembled robot.

The simulation models these actuators as position servos with $k_p = 200$ and $k_v = 5$, which is
critically damped at roughly 13\,Hz. The real drive rolls off at 0.8\,Hz. That is a bandwidth
mismatch of more than an order of magnitude, against a learnable gait frequency range whose top is
4\,Hz. At 2.5\,Hz the hardware executes 30\,\% of the commanded amplitude, $94^\circ$ late.
\textbf{Every policy in \cref{tab:rl-results} assumes authority the hardware cannot deliver.}

Three consequences follow.

First, the unexecuted part of a command \emph{is} the tracking error, and it tends to the full
commanded amplitude as frequency rises. Any high-frequency content therefore caps the usable
excitation amplitude near $15^\circ$, whatever the workspace allows. That cap is why an early
identification sweep tripped the safety limit six seconds in, on the joint with the most room.
Excitation amplitude now solves against travel, no-load speed and tracking error together.

Second, \textbf{inertia cannot be excited in position mode at all}. Obtaining real joint
accelerations needs either negligible amplitude at high frequency, or current-mode excitation. The
quasi-static runs are the valuable data, and always were.

Third, and most consequential for the project, the fix is architectural rather than a gain change.
The drive's cascaded position loop is proportional-only, and nothing in the runtime can write its
gains. The measurement points to current-mode commands with the position PD closed on the Pi at
200\,Hz. It also points to a torque-source actuator in simulation, with a proper torque--speed
envelope, in place of a fictional stiff servo. \Cref{sec:rl-torque} discusses why that partially
reverses a decision taken at the start of the project.

An existing policy cannot be adapted to the measured drive simply by switching the drive model on.
Applying 0.8\,Hz in one step to a converged walking policy collapsed it, with nothing else changed:
episode length fell from 1448 to 251, and return from $+511$ to $-78$. The drive model is therefore
ramped in as a curriculum, logarithmically in frequency. A linear-in-Hz ramp would do almost
nothing for most of its length and then fall off a cliff, because the equivalent filter coefficient
is strongly nonlinear in bandwidth.

\subsubsection{The ankle spring}
\label{sec:plant-id-ankle}

The fabricated spring measures $k = 41.4$\,N$\cdot$m/rad with \textbf{no preload}. The model assumed
$k = 28.65$ with 12.8\,N$\cdot$m of preload at stance. \Cref{sec:rl-ankle-overturned} treats the
consequences, because they overturn a conclusion drawn from the reinforcement-learning experiments.

\subsubsection{System identification: including what it could not resolve}
\label{sec:plant-id-sysid}

Beyond the individual parameters above, a full dynamic-parameter identification was attempted. Its
limits are as informative as its results.

\paragraph{Method.} Inverse dynamics through the closed loop was rejected. The 4-bar is
near-singular over part of the operating region, and the resulting inverse blows up. Identification
is instead a per-joint linear regression of measured current against four terms: a gravity-torque
model, viscous and Coulomb friction, an inertia term and an intercept. A constrained Jacobian
differentiates the loop closure out.

\paragraph{Four estimator bugs, each of which produced a confident wrong answer.} A missing sign
convention on the current column made every $K_t$ come out negative. The joint mapping never
mirrored the right leg, which put the reconstructed configuration outside the assemblable band and
left a loop residual of 0.78\,m instead of $10^{-8}$. That residual reproduced identically on three
separate recordings, so it was not a bad capture. Two further faults each flipped the sign
of $K_t$ independently: pooling rows from joints a given excitation run did not move, and omitting
the intercept. Both flip the sign because the drive's DC current offset then lands in the gravity
column. The fourth bug was over-regularisation of the loop Jacobian. The problem is well
conditioned in this pose region, and the damping alone inflated the fitted gravity gain by 20\,\%.
With the first two bugs fixed, the two legs agree to 5 or 6\,\% rather than disagreeing in sign,
and that cross-validation is what says the fixes are right.

\paragraph{The rejected result.} With $K_t$ pinned by the known-mass test, the model still
under-predicts gravity torque by a factor of 1.3 to 1.75. The obvious explanation is that the CAD
centre-of-mass offsets are wrong. That hypothesis was tested and \textbf{rejected}. Gravity torque
is linear in each body's centre of mass, so the test is a single linear solve. With all 18
parameters free, no regularisation and no plausibility constraint, moving mass explains at most 11
to 19\,\% of the residual. Ten of the eighteen directions are pure null space, and that null space
is where physically absurd fitted offsets come from. The CAD centres of mass therefore stand.
Per-body mass, load-dependent stiction and base tilt were tested and rejected on the same grounds.
One decisive test ruled out friction of any origin. Friction opposes motion and therefore reverses
sign with the direction of travel. Fitting the gravity gain separately on up-strokes and
down-strokes puts both on the same side of unity, for all six joints. The residual is conservative,
and no friction model can produce it.

What remains lies outside the span of every rigid-body inertial parameter. That points at the
kinematic model rather than at the mass distribution, and it has not been resolved.

\paragraph{Why this is worth reporting.} An identification that returns a number is easy to
over-trust. This one returns two things. The first is a measured, repeatable, per-joint gravity
gain, which can be applied as a correction or as a randomisation range without being understood.
The second is an explicit statement that the mass distribution is not the explanation. Knowing
which parameters the experiment \emph{could not} resolve, and why, is what stops the next person
fitting the same null space again.

policy forward, and writes six commands. The measurements were taken with the web interface
running, because that is the realistic case: the interface consumes roughly 70\,\% of a core.

Three results follow, and all three contradict assumptions made earlier in the project.

\textbf{The drives broadcast their state at exactly 200.0\,Hz, unprompted.} All six motors do so,
on both buses, at a period of 5.00\,ms and a p99 of 5.03\,ms. The broadcast is free-running and
continues with the control software stopped, so it is not poll-triggered. The sensing ceiling is
therefore exactly 200\,Hz, and it matches the control stack. A 50\,Hz loop would discard three of
every four sensor frames.

\textbf{200\,Hz is clean, and 50\,Hz is reproducibly worse.} The 50\,Hz row above is not frame
loss, because the interface counters show nothing dropped. It is a scheduling artefact of the
longer sleep, combined with a drain three times more expensive because four times as many frames
have accumulated. The case for dropping to 50\,Hz had been compute headroom, and compute is not the
constraint. Policy inference costs 117\,$\mu$s on average and 144\,$\mu$s at the 99th percentile,
which is 2.3\,\% of a 5\,ms budget.

\textbf{The CAN bus was never near its limit.} Six motors, counting command plus reply, occupy
about 26\,\% of one 1\,Mbit bus at 200\,Hz, spread across two buses.

The conclusion is to deploy at 200\,Hz, and no 50\,Hz fallback is worth having. None of this helps
with the real bottleneck, and that deserves emphasis. Running the outer loop at 200\,Hz cannot
compensate for an actuator that executes a quarter of the command $94^\circ$ late
(\cref{sec:plant-id-drive}). What the measurement does establish is that the Pi holds ample budget
to close the position loop itself, which is the proposed fix.

\subsubsection{The CAN protocol, as measured}
\label{sec:deploy-can}

The protocol was sniffed on both live buses rather than read from documentation, and the difference
mattered. Exactly two frame types exist per motor. Outbound is the command frame. Inbound is a
single feedback frame at command byte \texttt{0x29}, at exactly 200\,Hz, using all eight bytes for
position, speed, current, temperature and an error flag.

The VESC-style extended status frames that would report input voltage and duty cycle are absent.
One hypothesis held that these drives are a VESC derivative with the extra status packets
available, and that hypothesis is measurably false. The command byte is \texttt{0x29} rather than
VESC's 9, so the vendor renumbered rather than inheriting. Two consequences follow. The electrical
route to $K_t$ is closed, which is why the known-mass test of \cref{sec:plant-id} was necessary.
And pack sag cannot be observed from the drives.

One process note is worth recording. Nothing in the codebase decoded the command byte, because
every consumer masked it off and keyed on the node id. A comment asserting the wrong command number
therefore sat unnoticed in dead code for months. An assumption that is never exercised is never
falsified.

\subsection{Achievable gait: actuator and mass-distribution limits}
\label{sec:speed-ceiling}

The robot's maximum running speed was estimated independently of reinforcement learning, by a
first-principles analysis of the mechanical system. RL demonstrates a lower bound on performance.
This analysis provides the upper bound that the robot's kinematics, dynamics and actuator
capabilities impose.

\subsubsection{Method}

The analysis runs directly on the MuJoCo model (\texttt{training/model/dash01.xml}), which keeps it
consistent with the model used for policy training. Candidate gaits are parameterised by duty
factor $D$, contact length $L_c$, ride height $h$ and cadence.

For each candidate gait, a simplified running model gives the stride length, flight time, swing
duration and ground reaction forces. The leg Jacobian then gives joint torques and velocities
throughout the gait cycle.

A gait counts as feasible only if every joint stays inside the admissible 4-bar assembly region
while respecting the actuator torque, speed and power limits. The parameter space was explored
exhaustively to find the maximum feasible running speed.

The implementation was validated against MuJoCo. Static equilibrium predictions agree within 1\,\%,
and the analytic Jacobian matches \texttt{mj\_jacGeom} to better than $10^{-10}$.

At the practical optimum of six steps per second or fewer, the predicted gait has a duty factor
near 0.45, a stride length of 1.1 to 1.3\,m, and a contact length of 0.25 to 0.30\,m. Its peak
vertical ground reaction force is about 3.5 body weights, or 440\,N, and its ground contacts last
between 36 and 81\,ms.

The predicted continuous-speed limit of 1.8\,m/s is already lower than the 2.6\,m/s the RL policy
achieved, which shows that the learned controller relies on burst actuator capability. Motor
temperature must therefore be validated experimentally before any sustained hardware operation. On
the corrected plant of \cref{sec:speed-ceiling-corrected} the margin disappears entirely.

The unconstrained optimum falls at 12.4 steps/s. That is the same high-cadence solution
reinforcement learning discovered repeatedly. The agreement confirms that the high-cadence
behaviour originates in the system dynamics rather than in a simulator artefact.

\subsubsection{Limiting factors}

Swing-leg actuator power limits maximum speed, not kinematic reach.

The dominant constraint is the cam actuator during swing. It demands about 878\,W, namely
64\,N$\cdot$m at 13.7\,rad/s, against a theoretical maximum output of 935\,W. Raising actuator
no-load speed would therefore improve achievable running speed more than raising peak torque would.

Stance loading is the second limiting factor. Peak ground reaction forces demand about
167\,N$\cdot$m of the available 170\,N$\cdot$m, which leaves minimal torque margin.

Kinematic reach does not limit the result. The leg spans a theoretical fore--aft workspace of
1.06\,m, but only 0.20 to 0.35\,m of that span is usable in practice. Near the edge of the assembly
region the 4-bar's transmission ratio deteriorates. Joint velocities then rise rapidly, and
achievable running speed falls.

Introducing a flight phase improves performance substantially. Reducing the duty factor from 0.85
to 0.45 raises the predicted speed from 4.6 to 6.9\,m/s, and demands only a 1.2\,cm vertical
excursion of the centre of mass. Lower duty factors demand ground reaction forces above 4.5 body
weights, and are therefore infeasible.

Running speed is considerably more sensitive to distal leg mass than to torso mass. Mass added at

On the current design, removing 0.22\,kg from each distal leg raises predicted top speed by about
0.5\,m/s. Adding 5\,kg to the torso produces a comparable reduction. The result validates the
heuristic that guided the leg design, namely that distal inertia should be minimised.

\subsubsection{Ankle stiffness}

A static load analysis independently reproduced the ankle stiffness threshold that the
reinforcement-learning experiments had already shown.

The passive ankle spring, at $28.65\,\mathrm{N\,m/rad}$, supports only about half a body weight
before it collapses under positive mechanical feedback. The actuators meanwhile operate well below
their torque limits. Raising the stiffness progressively improves load capacity, and about
$350\,\mathrm{N\,m/rad}$ is needed to support two body weights and hold a stable stance.

The analytic load model and the reinforcement-learning experiments agree, which validates the
stiffness threshold independently. A separate static end-effector force map
(\cref{sec:hw-force-map}) reproduces the same conclusion from a third direction.

\subsubsection{The corrected ceiling}
\label{sec:speed-ceiling-corrected}

The figures above were computed at 12.83\,kg with a 170\,N$\cdot$m peak torque. \cref{sec:plant-id} shows both to be wrong, and wrong in the direction that hurts most:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
  \item Peak torque falls from 170 to 144.5\,N$\cdot$m. Since 87\,\% of feasible gaits are limited at the cam actuator's peak-power corner, this is a direct hit on the binding constraint.
  \item Distal leg mass rises by roughly 0.70\,kg across both legs (the shin body from 222 to 573\,g), at a sensitivity of $-0.93$\,(m/s)/kg from \cref{tab:mass-sensitivity}.
\end{itemize}

Propagating both corrections through the sensitivity figures gives roughly \textbf{2.7\,m/s
credible burst and 1.3\,m/s thermally sustained}, against 3.7 and 1.8\,m/s on the CAD plant. That
changes the headline comparison of the whole project. The learned policy's 2.6\,m/s no longer sits
comfortably inside the envelope. It sits essentially \emph{at} the burst ceiling, and at roughly
twice the sustained figure. For context, the bipedal 100\,m record averages about 4\,m/s, and a
100\,m dash is about 25\,s of continuous running against a continuous torque rating. The
\emph{sustained} number is therefore the one that decides whether a record is reachable.

The honest conclusion is that this chassis is a walking and jogging demonstrator with an unusually
good measurement pedigree, not a record contender. The design implications for a successor fall
directly out of the analysis above. Bus voltage comes first, because speed scales linearly with
no-load speed and not at all with peak torque. Distal mass comes second.

These corrected figures propagate the sensitivity coefficients. They are not a re-run of the full
feasibility sweep. The sweep should be repeated properly on the corrected plant before submission,
and the tooling to do so now exists in validated form.

\subsubsection{Limitations}

The analysis provides an optimistic upper bound on performance. It neglects torso attitude
stabilisation, lateral dynamics, impact losses, control bandwidth limits and the coupled actuator
torque--speed envelope. The last of those is no longer a small omission.
\Cref{sec:plant-id-drive} measures the drive's position loop at 0.8\,Hz, so the assumption that a
commanded joint trajectory is actually executed fails well below the cadences this analysis
assumes.

Inertia tensors also remain approximate. The segment \emph{masses} are now weighed
(\cref{tab:masses}), and the model's tensors were rescaled by the per-body mass ratio. The tensor
\emph{shapes} are still CAD, and the identification of \cref{sec:plant-id-sysid} could not resolve
the mass distribution any further. \Cref{tab:mass-sensitivity} shows the result is most sensitive
to exactly that quantity, which makes it the largest remaining uncertainty in the analysis.

\subsection{Toy problem: one joint, end to end}
\label{sec:toy-problem}

Committing a 12-DOF robot to an unproven pipeline is expensive. The complete chain (simulate,
train, export, deploy on embedded Linux, close a loop over CAN) was therefore exercised once at
minimum stakes first, on a single-motor inverted pendulum.

The environment is a MuJoCo pendulum wrapped in a Gymnasium interface. Its observation is
deliberately \emph{the hardware telemetry and nothing else}: $[\text{position (turns)},
\text{velocity (turns/s)}, \text{torque (N$\cdot$m)}]$, exactly as the drive reports it. The policy
outputs a torque command clipped to $\pm$1\,N$\cdot$m. Success means reaching the upright band
within 5\,s, then holding $\pm$0.1\,turn and $\pm$0.1\,turn/s to the end of a 20\,s episode.
Training is SB3 PPO with a curriculum. The export bakes the observation normalisation statistics
\emph{into} the ONNX graph, a detail that returns as a hard requirement in
\cref{sec:rl-validation}. The policy then runs on a Raspberry Pi under \texttt{onnxruntime},
closing the loop on a moteus drive over CAN at 50\,Hz with a watchdog. The sim-to-real transfer
succeeded, and the exercise bought exactly what it was designed to buy.

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
  \item The \textbf{train$\to$ONNX$\to$embedded-inference path} existed and was debugged a year
        before the biped needed it. Transport initialisation on the CAN adapter was already
        non-trivial and cost real iterations at this stage, where iterations were cheap.
  \item The discipline of \textbf{building the observation from raw hardware telemetry} rather
        than from simulator conveniences. That discipline reappears later as the ``no privileged
        observations'' rule of \cref{sec:sim2real-lineage}.
  \item Early evidence on the \textbf{compute question} of \cref{sec:compute-choice}. The
        BeagleBone-class board originally envisaged was rejected partly on the experience gained
        here.
\end{itemize}

The rig also survives as a candidate student laboratory exercise. It demonstrates the entire
RL-for-hardware loop with one motor and one afternoon of training.

\subsection{RL policy design and validation}
\label{sec:rl}

The control policy went through three distinct action-space designs over the project. This section
documents all three, the reflex structure that ended up wrapped around the final one, and the
results obtained so far. The learning algorithm was held fixed throughout: PPO from
Stable-Baselines3, an actor--critic MLP, trained on CPU-parallel MuJoCo environments. The
comparisons are therefore about the \emph{action representation}, not about the optimiser.

\subsubsection{Attempt 1: direct joint torque}
\label{sec:rl-torque}

The first framework commanded joint torque directly. A fixed torque scale on the actuators mapped
the policy output to motor setpoints. The work used a simplified bipedal robot, because the robot
developed in this thesis was still being designed. The formulation is the textbook one, and it is
what most legged-locomotion papers use in simulation.

It was abandoned for two reasons.

\textbf{It did not converge.} The training history from that period is an unbroken sequence of
reward-shaping attempts. The fall penalty rose because the robot learned to belly-flop. The alive
bonus changed because the robot learned to dawdle. The feet-air-time thresholds were re-tuned
because the robot learned to vibrate in place. After a few iterations of that whack-a-mole exploit
patching, the underlying problem became clear. A raw torque action space forces the policy to
synthesise two things from scratch at every control step: the gravity compensation that keeps the
robot standing, \emph{and} the periodic structure that makes it walk. The reward landscape between
those two is dominated by ways of falling over that convinced the reward the robot was doing well.

\textbf{More decisively, it was judged undeployable.} The CubeMars drives expose a servo mode whose
internal position and velocity loop runs on the drive itself. Commanding torque means bypassing
that loop and streaming current setpoints over CAN, from Python, on a Raspberry Pi. The timing
budget was estimated rather than measured, and the estimate was pessimistic on three counts. It
assumed the achievable loop rate would be low and jittery. It assumed the CAN bus could not carry
the traffic needed to go faster. And it assumed that a missed deadline in torque mode leaves the
drive holding the last commanded torque blindly, which on a loaded leg integrates into a runaway
rather than a pause. Only the third assumption still holds. On top of that, the actuator's
output-side torque constant was unknown, so the commanded newton-metres would have been approximate
in any case.

The two arguments pointed the same way. The policy class that most needs high control bandwidth and
an accurate actuator model appeared to be exactly the class for which neither was available. Torque
control was dropped, and position control was adopted in its place.

\paragraph{The measurement campaign partially reverses this decision.} The reversal is one of the
clearest results of the project, because instrumentation overturned a design decision that had
already been acted on. Every quantitative premise of the bandwidth argument was measured a year
later (\cref{sec:plant-id-timing}). The first two premises were both wrong, and wrong in the same
direction.

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
  \item The Raspberry Pi holds a full 200\,Hz control loop (drain both CAN buses, run the policy
  forward, write six commands) at a jitter of 0.141\,ms standard deviation and with \textbf{half
  its time budget spare}, while the web interface competes for CPU. A 400\,Hz loop also holds.
  Policy inference costs 117\,$\mu$s, or 2.3\,\% of a 5\,ms budget. Far from being marginal,
  50\,Hz is reproducibly \emph{worse} than 200\,Hz.
  \item The CAN bus sits at 26\,\% of one 1\,Mbit line, and the drives already broadcast their
  state at exactly 200.0\,Hz whether asked to or not.
\end{itemize}

The compute and the bus were the two things assumed to be the constraint, and both hold ample
headroom. The actual bottleneck lies \emph{inside the servo mode that was kept instead}. The
drive's own position loop rolls off at 0.8\,Hz (\cref{sec:plant-id-drive}), and that is the single
largest sim-to-real gap in the project.

The current direction is therefore \texttt{SET\_CURRENT} commands, with the position PD closed on
the Pi at 200\,Hz, and a torque-source actuator carrying a real torque--speed envelope in
simulation. That is the Cheetah-lineage standard, the drives support it, and the timing measurement
shows the Pi has the budget for it. The change is not a full return to Attempt 1. The policy would
still emit position targets, and the PD loop that closes them would simply move from the drive to
the Pi, where its bandwidth is known and its gains are writable. What is reversed is the premise,
not the action space.

\subsubsection{Attempt 2: joint position targets (PD)}
\label{sec:rl-position}

The second design made the action six \emph{position} targets, one per actuated joint, at 50\,Hz.
The knee and the ankle follow passively, through the parallel linkage and the ankle spring. In
simulation these targets drive MuJoCo \texttt{<position>} actuators, with gains and force ranges
taken from the CubeMars datasheets: \texttt{kp}~120, \texttt{kv}~4 and 72\,N$\cdot$m at hip roll,
and \texttt{kp}~200, \texttt{kv}~5 and 170\,N$\cdot$m at cam and thigh. On hardware the action maps
one to one onto what the drives accept.

\paragraph{What worked.} Standing and balancing converged, after a fair amount of effort and reward
shaping. The contact model was switched to a point foot, in which only a 2.5\,cm sphere at each toe
collides. A policy therefore cannot rest a shin on the ground and damage the real robot. After that
switch the policy held full-length episodes on two toe points. The switch also surfaced a
floor-penetration exploit. With only the toe sphere colliding, the non-colliding foot and shin
meshes could pass \emph{through} the floor. The first apparently perfect balancing policy was
largely exploiting that, by sinking its centre of mass below the floor into a stable equilibrium.
The fix terminates the episode on either of two conditions: an upper-hemisphere toe contact,
meaning the sphere has sunk, or any foot or shin vertex below the floor plane.

\paragraph{What failed: skating.} Walking did not converge. After 32~million steps the policy
reached a perfectly healthy-looking reward curve while \emph{sliding} rather than stepping. Both
feet were grounded on 57 to 66\,\% of steps, at a duty factor of 0.74 to 0.83. The median ``swing''
lasted one control step, which is contact chatter at 5 to 10 touchdowns per second. In-contact toe
slip of 0.11 to 0.24\,m/s accounted for essentially all of the translation, and the mean forward
velocity was $-0.09$\,m/s under a $+0.5$\,m/s command.

Three separate causes were found and fixed. First, nothing priced foot motion while in contact, so
a slip penalty was added, with a grounded predicate that survives the micro-hop and ghost-drag
dodges. Second, the air-time term could only ever punish stepping, because credit was paid at
touchdown and never lifting scored zero. That term was made one-sided and capped, then paired with
a per-foot stance-time cap and a swing-clearance term, to give PPO an immediate gradient toward
lifting a foot. Third, the contact physics silently made skating free: \texttt{condim=3} disables
torsional and rolling friction, which turns the toe spheres into ball casters.

Two reward-plumbing bugs compounded the problem. Observation normalisation was also normalising the
\emph{reward}, dividing the carefully balanced raw scale by a return standard deviation of about
110, so a 200-point fall penalty reached the optimiser as $-1.8$. And the entropy coefficient on a
clipped Gaussian policy amounted to free entropy, so the action standard deviation ratcheted up
until training behaviour and deterministic evaluation were two different controllers.

The lasting methodological lesson is the first one. \textbf{The system will happily exploit any
loophole in the reward function.}

\paragraph{Why the design was abandoned.} The fundamental problem survived every fix. A per-step
position policy must rediscover the periodicity of a gait from scratch at every control step,
because nothing in the representation says that locomotion is cyclic. A plant change compounded
that. Updating the six motor masses, which total 7.1\,kg and more than half the robot, and
re-settling the stance keyframe under gravity, moved the plant far enough that the earlier standing
policy no longer transferred. The walking policy that had been improving slowly stopped improving
at all. Rather than re-tune the same representation against a heavier plant, I changed the
representation.

\subsubsection{Attempt 3: a central pattern generator tuned by the policy}
\label{sec:rl-cpg}

The third design puts the periodicity into the action space itself. The policy no longer emits joint
targets. It emits the \emph{coefficients of a Fourier series} that describes one gait cycle, and
the environment reconstructs the joint targets from those coefficients at the current gait phase.
The result is a central pattern generator whose shape is learned rather than hand-authored. The
network no longer has to invent periodicity. It only has to choose which periodic signal it wants.

The idea has a literature, and this design arrived at it independently rather than first. Truncated
Fourier series have parameterised humanoid gaits since 2009, with the coefficients fixed offline by
evolutionary search~\cite{shafii2010tfs} or adapted online around a fixed
series~\cite{yang2010tfs}; rhythmic dynamic movement primitives~\cite{ijspeert2013dmp} carry the
same Fourier-type basis over phase; and Castillo et al.~\cite{castillo2020hzdrl} had already placed
trajectory-basis coefficients directly in an RL action space, using the B\'ezier polynomials of the
hybrid-zero-dynamics virtual constraints. The BioRob oscillator line~\cite{cpgrl2022} and the
Fourier latent representation of~\cite{fld2024} were both found after this design emerged.
\Cref{sec:bg-prior} places the design against that body of work and states what is and is not
claimed here. This section records how the choice was reached and what it cost on this plant.

\paragraph{Formulation.} For the two sagittal joints of the left leg (cam and thigh, the pair that
drives the parallel 4-bar and therefore propulsion), the deviation from the nominal stance posture
at gait phase $\varphi$ is
\begin{equation}
  \Delta q_j(\varphi) \;=\; A_j \left[ w_0 a_0 + \sum_{k=1}^{N} w_k \bigl( a_k \cos k\varphi + b_k \sin k\varphi \bigr) \right],
  \qquad w_k \propto \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{k}},
  \label{eq:fourier}
\end{equation}
with $N = 3$ harmonics, which is seven coefficients per joint. The harmonic weights $w_k$ are
normalised to sum to one, which bounds the waveform. The $1/\sqrt{k}$ schedule was chosen
deliberately over the more obvious $1/k$. A $1/k$ schedule biases the series so strongly toward its
fundamental that every gait it can express is near-sinusoidal, which is to say walking. The
push-off of a running stride is impulsive and needs high-harmonic authority. The result is clipped
to $\pm A_j$ around the nominal posture. That bound is \emph{relative}, because the nominal posture
is always validated and assemblable, and the coupled 4-bar assembles over only a diagonal band of
the joint box.

The phase $\varphi$ advances at a frequency that is itself part of the action, so the policy
chooses its own cadence. The right leg is not emitted at all. It is constructed as the mirror of
the left, half a cycle out of phase, as $\Delta q_{R,j}(\varphi) = -\Delta q_{L,j}(\varphi + \pi)$.
Mirroring halves the action dimension, guarantees an alternating gait by construction, and matches
the convention the hand-authored gait demo already used. It carries one consequence worth flagging,
because that consequence cost time later. A mirror-symmetric gait can only travel in a straight
line. Steering therefore had to be added afterwards, as two explicit asymmetry channels
(differential stride amplitude and differential stance width) rather than emerging on its own.

\paragraph{First version: per-cycle updates, and why it was not enough.} The initial implementation
made one environment step equal one full gait cycle. The policy chose a gait, the environment ran
that gait to completion, and the macro reward was the mean of the per-step rewards. An episode then
held roughly 30 decisions instead of 1000. The design was immediately better than the per-step PD
policy. A hand-crafted elliptical foot path pushed through the same reconstruction walked at
0.357\,m/s open-loop, against the PD policy's 0.01\,m/s. The first trained policy then walked
properly at about 0.5\,m/s, with no skating signature at all. The structural prior worked exactly
as intended.

The design was nevertheless slow, and it fell over as soon as the pitch degree of freedom was
released. A literature review at that point returned an unambiguous verdict: \textbf{no published
system achieves running with per-cycle-only parameter updates and no intra-cycle feedback.} Every
successful periodic parameterisation keeps a per-step channel. PMTG modulates the trajectory
generator and adds per-step residual corrections. CPG-RL re-selects the oscillator parameters at
100\,Hz. The same review found a genuine semi-Markov bug in this formulation. Taking the \emph{mean}
of the per-step rewards over a macro-step, with a fixed discount per macro-step, makes the total
return scale with cadence. The effective horizon then varies between 4.8\,s and 28\,s, depending on
the gait frequency the policy happened to choose. The review flagged two missing observations as
well. Base velocity was not observable at all, even though the reward maximises it.
Distance-to-the-line was also missing, which left the value function partially observed and made
braking unplannable.

\paragraph{Final version: per-step re-parameterisation plus residuals.} The current stack keeps the
Fourier gait but re-emits the entire specification at \emph{every} control step, and adds a
per-step correction channel on top. The 24-dimensional action is:

\begin{table}[H]
\centering
\begin{tabular}{clp{9.2cm}}
\toprule
\textbf{dims} & \textbf{channel} & \textbf{role} \\
\midrule
14 & Fourier coefficients & cam + thigh gait shape, \cref{eq:fourier}; mirrored to the right leg \\
1  & gait frequency & cadence, 0.5--4.0\,Hz; the phase integrates it \\
3  & hip-roll reflex gains & $k_p$, $k_d$, bias of the lateral balance reflex \\
6  & per-step residuals & direct corrections on the six PD targets, $\pm$0.08\,rad \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\caption{The per-step action. The first 18 dimensions are the gait \emph{specification}; the last
six are the fast-feedback channel.}
\label{tab:action}
\end{table}

Rewriting the gait specification mid-cycle is legal but priced. A penalty proportional to
$\sin^2(\varphi/2)$ bills every change to the specification, so a rewrite is free exactly at the
cycle boundary and most expensive mid-stride. The residuals are deliberately \emph{never} billed by
that penalty, because they exist to be per-step. A one-off stumble correction should not have to
pay for rewriting the whole gait. The separation is therefore clean. The Fourier channel is the
gait, and the residual channel is the correction. A separate magnitude penalty keeps the residual
from quietly becoming the controller.

That pricing is the part of this action space that the nearest published neighbour does not have.
Castillo et al.~\cite{castillo2020hzdrl} also emit basis coefficients as the action, but the
rewrite is unpriced and there is no separate residual channel, so a coefficient change and a
stumble correction are the same event to the optimiser. Here they are different events with
different bills, which is what lets the gait specification stay a gait rather than degenerating
into a per-step joint controller wearing a Fourier basis.

The separation held as a design and only partly held as an outcome, which is worth flagging at the
point the design is stated rather than only in the failure catalogue. After the frequency remap the
gait clock settled near 1\,Hz while the actual footfall rate stayed near 6\,Hz
(\cref{sec:rl-cadence}), so the cadence the robot walked at was not the cadence the Fourier channel
specified. The obvious reading, that the unbilled residual channel was the open fast path, was
tested and refuted: removing the channel entirely made the chatter worse, not better
(\cref{sec:cpg-ab}). Pricing the rewrite is therefore a necessary condition for keeping the gait
channel a gait, and demonstrably not a sufficient one. That is the form the claim takes here.

The observation holds 55 values stacked over 5 frames, or 275 in total. It carries motor position,
velocity and torque; the IMU gravity direction and gyro rates; base velocity; $\sin\varphi$ and
$\cos\varphi$; a task channel with the run/stop flag and the distance remaining; and the previous
action.

\paragraph{Reward.} The sprint objective is a single dash: stand at the line, run, stop. One insight
carries the design, and it is worth stating because it is not obvious. \textbf{A per-step speed
reward cannot price time.} Integrating $w\,v_x$ over a fixed course gives $w \cdot
\text{distance}$, whatever the pace, so a fast run and a slow one score identically. What prices
time is a \emph{constant per-step clock cost}, which integrates to $-w_t T$. Correspondingly, the
alive bonus must be exactly zero, because paying per second of existence rewards dawdling. The
break-even pace is the ratio of the two, at 0.25\,m/s.

Three groups of terms sit around that core. The anti-skate terms are inherited from the PD era. A
Siekmann-style phase-gated contact schedule makes each foot pay for ground contact during its
expected swing window, using the action's own gait clock. As the stance ratio ramps from 0.65 down
to 0.42, the two swing windows begin to overlap, which demands a flight phase explicitly. That is
the one term which asks for running rather than for fast walking. The efficiency terms price
torque, motor velocity and positive mechanical power. They ramp in over 60~million steps, so that
they cannot smother gait emergence early.

Adversarial review of the reward code found four exploits, all fixed before the long runs. A
stop-phase income was net-positive per step, and therefore farmable forever by twitching the hold
timer. A one-sided velocity clip made shuttling back and forth in front of the finish line strictly
better than crossing it. And a cold-start suicide made diving at $-100$ value-optimal for any
policy that could not yet locomote, because the always-on penalties for a standing robot sum to
about $-2.2$ per step. The last was fixed by flooring the per-step total, not just the individual
terms.

\paragraph{Curriculum over base degrees of freedom.} Asking a fresh policy to solve balance and
locomotion simultaneously is too much, so the base is released progressively instead. The model's
free joint was replaced by six explicit scalar joints ($x$, $y$, $z$, roll, pitch and yaw) plus
six equality constraints that can be activated individually. Any subset of the base degrees of
freedom can therefore be rigidly railed at reset. Milestone \texttt{m1} frees forward translation
only, with height railed at a randomised ride height drawn from a measured lookup table. Milestone
\texttt{m2} adds height, \texttt{m3} adds pitch, \texttt{m4} adds lateral translation and
\texttt{m5} adds roll. Milestone \texttt{m6} is the real plant. Rigid equality constraints were
chosen over clamping the state after each step, precisely because they produce the correct reaction
wrench \emph{during} the step, which keeps the leg torques faithful. Critically, the observation
and action dimensions do not change across milestones, so every stage warm-starts from the previous
stage's checkpoint.

\subsubsection{Reflexes: what is learned and what is hard-wired}
\label{sec:rl-reflexes}

Not everything in the controller is a Fourier series, and the split is deliberate. A Fourier series
over gait phase is \emph{feedforward} by construction. It is a function of where the robot is in
the cycle, not of what the robot is doing. Balance is not a periodic problem but a feedback
problem, and encoding it as a phase-indexed trajectory would make it blind. Three feedback channels
therefore sit outside the CPG.

\paragraph{Lateral (abduction) reflex: learned gains, fixed structure.} The hip-roll joints are
not driven by the gait generator at all. They are driven by a linear reflex on base roll,
\begin{equation}
  u \;=\; k_p\,\phi_{\text{roll}} \;+\; k_d\,\dot{\phi}_{\text{roll}} \;+\; b,
  \qquad q_{\text{hip roll},L} = q^{\text{nom}}_L + u, \quad q_{\text{hip roll},R} = q^{\text{nom}}_R - u,
\end{equation}
evaluated at every control step. The left and right roll axes are mirrored, so opposite signs in
joint space move both feet the same physical way. That is the lateral shift of the centre of mass
the reflex is for. The three gains $(k_p, k_d, b)$ are \emph{emitted by the policy}, so the
controller is learned, but only three numbers of it are, rather than a free trajectory. That
decision is what made the original per-cycle formulation tractable at all: a feedforward CPG for
propulsion, plus a feedback reflex for balance, instead of a per-step policy with free hip roll. It
was kept in the per-step version because it works. Nothing assumes which sign stabilises, and the
policy discovers that. The reflex is necessarily inert while roll is railed, across milestones
\texttt{m1} to \texttt{m3}, where the gains are unidentifiable. It becomes live only at
\texttt{m5}. That is expected behaviour rather than a bug, and it is worth knowing before reading
those training curves.

\paragraph{Sagittal (pitch) reflex: hand-tuned, not learned.} A fixed PD on base pitch and pitch
rate drives a \emph{symmetric} fore/aft thigh offset,
\begin{equation}
  u_p = -\operatorname{clip}\bigl(k_p^{\theta} g_x + k_d^{\theta} \dot{\theta} + b^{\theta},\; \pm u_{\max}\bigr),
  \qquad q_{\text{thigh},L} \mathrel{+}= u_p, \quad q_{\text{thigh},R} \mathrel{-}= u_p,
\end{equation}
where $g_x \approx \sin\theta$ is the fore/aft component of the measured gravity direction. The
thigh axes are mirrored here too, so opposite signs move both feet the \emph{same} physical way.
When the robot pitches nose-down, both feet shift forward, under the falling centre of mass. That
is capture-point control in its crudest form, and the gain scales follow that reading:
$k_p \approx h_{\text{CoM}}/\ell \approx 1$ and $k_d \approx \sqrt{h/g} \approx 0.3$.

Why this reflex is \emph{not} learned is worth spelling out, because it is the opposite choice from
the hip-roll reflex. It was introduced when the pitch-free milestone was collapsing in 1.5\,s, and
a learned gain has no useful gradient in that regime. The policy has to survive long enough to
experience the correction before it can learn to tune it, and it was not surviving. A hand-tuned
prior breaks that circularity. The gains were set by a grid search on the pitch-free plant. Values
of $k_d \geq 0.3$ oscillate through the one-step action delay and the target smoothing filter, and
an authority clip above 0.35\,rad starts fighting the gait itself. Those two bounds fix
$k_p = 2.0$, $k_d = 0.2$ and a clip of 0.25\,rad. Measured on a pitch-kick arrest test, the reflex
reduces the final pitch excursion by a factor of 2.6 to 6.7 in both directions. It also lifts
early-retraining survival from about 18 to about 80 control steps.

That last number is the point. The reflex is a \textbf{stabilising prior, not a controller}. The
plant with height free cannot stand at zero action at all, because holding a ride height requires
an active gait, so it collapses in about 1.3\,s whatever the pitch does. The reflex does not fix
that. It buys the policy enough surviving episodes to find a pitch-neutral gait itself. An early
acceptance gate of ``zero action stands for 20 seconds'' was simply wrong for this plant, and the
kick-arrest test replaced it.

\paragraph{Residuals: the fast channel.} The six per-step residuals are the third feedback path,
and the one the literature is most emphatic about. They let the controller respond to something
unmodelled within a single control step, without rewriting the gait. They are kept small, at
0.08\,rad, raised to 0.20\,rad once the control rate moved to 200\,Hz. They are also
magnitude-penalised, so that the Fourier prior stays dominant.

All three channels are implemented in pure NumPy, in the same module that reconstructs the gait,
with no dependency on the simulator. That is not incidental. It is what allows the identical reflex
code to run on the Raspberry Pi against a real IMU, instead of being re-implemented and re-tuned at
deployment time.

\paragraph{A negative result: the emulated ankle reflex.} One more reflex was built and rejected.
The ankles are passive springs with no actuator, so a fixed PD was implemented to apply a
pitch-restoring \emph{torque} at the ankle joints, gated on that foot being grounded. The result is
an emulated actuated ankle, and a shippable controller if a real ankle motor were ever added. At
40\,N$\cdot$m of authority it \emph{destabilised} the running gait outright, collapsing episodes to
28 steps, because a phase-blind torque fights the gait it is trying to stabilise. At a gentle
12\,N$\cdot$m it neither destabilised anything nor solved anything, and gave only a small lift. The
negative result was still informative. It was evidence that ankle authority was the right lever,
and that is what motivated the passive-stiffness experiment described next.

\subsubsection{The pitch-balance wall}
\label{sec:rl-pitch-wall}

Everything through milestone \texttt{m2} trained well, with forward translation and height free and
attitude railed. Releasing pitch at \texttt{m3} produced the hardest failure of the project.
Episodes plateaued at roughly one second, which is the passive fall time, and stayed there. Two
full sessions of work went into the failure. What was ruled out is worth recording, because the
null results are the useful part.

Two methodological lessons came out of that table. First, \textbf{any training aid that holds the
robot up will be leaned on}. The in-training curve then looks wonderful while the unaided policy
gets worse, and only an evaluation with the aid forced to zero tells the truth. That deception
caught me three times before assist-off evaluation became protocol. Second, when every software
lever saturates at the same value, the constraint is not in the software.

\paragraph{The diagnostic that cracked it.} Rather than try a ninth lever, I instrumented the
failure. A tool rolls out a policy and plots, per foot, the fore/aft offset between the toe at
touchdown and the whole-robot centre of mass. The result was unambiguous. The base pitch ramps
monotonically forward into a pure topple. Meanwhile the feet land 8 to 9\,cm \emph{behind} the
centre of mass. Only 11\,\% of touchdowns landed ahead of it, and they fell progressively further
behind as the body ran away forward. The policy had a perfectly serviceable forward-running gait
and had never learned the \emph{capture step}, which plants the foot ahead of the centre of mass to
catch a forward fall. The robot was, quite literally, running out from under itself.

\paragraph{The fix was a plant change.} The diagnostic pointed at the passive ankle. Stiffening the
ankle spring by roughly a factor of twelve, from 28.65 to 350\,N$\cdot$m/rad, gives the passive
foot the stance pitch-restoring authority the plant lacked. The threshold is clean. A stiffness
$k \leq 200$ barely helps, while $k \geq 350$ breaks out of the plateau and climbs steeply. After
the fix, the same diagnostic reports 67\,\% of touchdowns landing ahead of the centre of mass, with
the base pitch pinned near zero.

One implementation detail is easy to get wrong, and getting it wrong cost a wasted run. The standing ankle
sits at about $\pm 0.25$\,rad while the spring reference sits at $\pm 0.7$\,rad, so the spring
carries about 12.8\,N$\cdot$m of \emph{preload} at stance. Raising the stiffness naively balloons
that preload. The foot then slams to its spring reference and the robot flips over. The reference
has to be shifted at the same time, so that $k(q_{\text{stand}} - q_{\text{ref}})$ is preserved.
Only the restoring \emph{gain} then rises, and the posture is unchanged.

The result also \emph{overturned} an earlier recommendation of my own. After the emulated-ankle
experiments I had concluded that the robot needed a policy-controlled actuated ankle: a motor, two
more action dimensions, and a full retrain of the milestone ladder. The stiffness experiment shows
that a passive spring suffices, which removes the motor, the two action dimensions and the
retrain. As \cref{sec:speed-ceiling} notes, the static load analysis independently arrives at the
same 350\,N$\cdot$m/rad threshold.

That left one question open: whether the real ankle could be built that stiff. The answer, when it
was measured, invalidated the result.

\paragraph{The measured spring, and what it does to this conclusion.}
\label{sec:rl-ankle-overturned}

The fabricated ankle measures $k = 41.4$\,N$\cdot$m/rad with \textbf{no preload}. Neither number
matches the model. A spring at $k = 350$ will not be built, and the 12.8\,N$\cdot$m of stance
preload the simulation carried does not exist on the robot.

The second discrepancy matters more than the first, and that is the genuinely useful finding here.
The preload, not the stiffness, was doing almost all of the work. At the same $k$, the modelled
spring sags 19\,mm at stance with its preload, and \textbf{145\,mm without it}. That reframes the
whole ankle question. The parameter the study spent its compute sweeping was not the parameter that
mattered.

Statics disposes of the real spring before any learning is involved. The easiest possible load case
is 0.5 body weights per foot, in double support, against a 3.5\,BW running requirement. Even there,
the no-preload spring deflects $26.6^\circ$, drops the robot 145\,mm, and puts the heel 11.9\,mm
into the floor at the reset pose. Contact forces stay well behaved throughout, so the collapse is
genuine loss of load capacity rather than a solver artefact.

\textbf{Everything in \cref{tab:rl-results} was therefore obtained on an ankle that does not exist.}
The balance result of this section is real as a statement about what the \emph{plant} needed. It is
not a statement about what this robot does.

The live candidate is now neither the fabricated spring nor a stiffer one. It is a tension-only
strut, taut at the flat-foot angle and buckling in compression. It stands like a welded ankle, at
$0.08^\circ$ of deflection and 0.8\,mm of sag, and deleting the spring assembly removes 249\,g from
each shin. \Cref{tab:mass-sensitivity} independently favours that change. At $-0.93$\,(m/s)/kg on
distal mass, 249\,g per leg is worth roughly $+0.5$\,m/s of top speed, which is the largest single
improvement available anywhere on the robot. A design decision reached for one reason turns out to
be supported by an unrelated measurement, which is the closest this project comes to a free result.

\subsubsection{The cadence problem and a cautionary bug}
\label{sec:rl-cadence}

With balance solved, the remaining pathology was cadence. The policies stepped far too fast, on the
order of 11\,Hz, pattering rather than striding. Several external limits were tried: hard velocity
caps, acceleration caps and a cadence penalty. All of them collapsed the warm-started policy
instead of slowing it.

Instrumenting the trained policy's own action channels found the cause. When the control rate was
raised to 200\,Hz, the learnable gait-frequency range had been widened to 0.5--50\,Hz, on the
reasoning that so wide a range would be ``non-binding''. The map from raw action to frequency is
linear over that range, so a \emph{neutral} network output of about zero means 25\,Hz. The policy
simply left that output near zero and inherited a 24\,Hz gait clock. The commanded thigh trajectory
was reversing sign 35 times a second at $\pm 0.45$\,rad, a signal that would destroy the real
actuators. Every external limit had been fighting a broken internal default. Remapping the range to
0.5--4.0\,Hz, so that a neutral action means about 2\,Hz, produced the best controller of the entire
project (\cref{tab:rl-results}).

The general lesson is worth carrying. \textbf{A parameter range widened to be ``non-binding'' can
silently become the operating point.} A network's default output is not a neutral gait. It is
whatever the middle of the range happens to decode to.

One pathology survives the fix. After the remap the gait clock slowed to about 1\,Hz, but the
actual \emph{footfall} rate stayed near 6\,Hz. The policy steps through the fast residual channel
rather than through the clock. A ring-down test placed the passive ankle mode at 1.0\,Hz, not
6.3\,Hz, so the chatter is a forced oscillation rather than a resonance: the policy pumps its legs
at 200\,Hz to balance a top-heavy robot. Five levers failed to remove it (a residual-rate penalty,
a torque-budget curriculum, a reduced pitch-reflex derivative gain, low-pass filtering of the
reflex input, and heavy ankle damping), and every warm-started run stays trapped in the
fast-stepping basin. The honest conclusion is that removing it needs a from-scratch retrain with
cadence pressure present from step zero, or a plant change such as a lower centre of mass. On
hardware, the ankle's real mechanical damping would absorb some of it.

\paragraph{An A/B on the generator itself.} The Fourier series is one of several ways to
parameterise a periodic gait, so it was compared directly against a phase-amplitude coupled
oscillator. That oscillator is the kind used in the biologically inspired locomotion literature,
and its foot trajectory maps through a measured inverse-kinematics table. Sixteen runs shared one plant, one
reward and one schedule, and only the generator was swapped. The verdict is that \textbf{there is
no clear winner}. The oscillator is more sample-efficient from a cold start on the hardest
milestone. The Fourier generator is more reliable across random seeds. The single best controller
measured in the whole experiment was an oscillator run: the only arm of the sixteen that ever
executed the stop phase, at 2.9\,m/s with a left/right asymmetry of 0.08.

One ablation in that experiment is worth reporting, because it refutes a hypothesis stated earlier
in this chapter. Removing the per-step residual channel, which leaves a pure oscillator, produced
\emph{stability without locomotion}. The robot survived most of the episode but travelled 15\,m of
72, at 0.25\,m/s, with the worst cadence in the entire set. The residual channel was the suspected
fast open path behind the 6\,Hz footfall of \cref{sec:rl-cadence}. Removing it made the chatter
worse rather than better, so that explanation is wrong.

\paragraph{Cost.} Training ran on the EPFL Izar cluster. Throughput is about 2900 environment-steps
per second on a V100 node, which is about 1.9$\times$ the CPU-only rate on the same node. The gain
is not down to network size, and that is worth recording. Physics always runs on the CPU, because
this stack has no GPU physics backend. Putting the tiny policy network on the GPU simply stops
PyTorch from stealing cores from the parallel MuJoCo workers. At that rate a 600\,M-step milestone
is a multi-day job, which is why the warm-start chain across milestones matters as much as it does.

\subsubsection{Validation status and known gaps}
\label{sec:rl-validation}

Stating the validation status plainly matters more than the results above, because the gap between
the two is the work that remains.

\textbf{Sim-to-sim} is exercised continuously by the milestone ladder. Each stage is a different
plant, with different constrained dynamics, and every stage loads the previous stage's weights. A
policy that only worked by exploiting a particular rail therefore fails visibly at the next rung.
The ankle-stiffness and centre-of-mass experiments use the same mechanism deliberately, as a
plant-sensitivity probe. The drive-bandwidth correction of \cref{sec:plant-id-drive} is its most
severe instance: a converged walker collapsed from an episode length of 1448 to 251, with only the
ankle and the drive changed.

\textbf{No trained policy has run on the robot.} That is the precise statement, and it is narrower
than ``nothing has run on hardware''. A great deal has. The hand-authored fixed gait plays on the
real motors. The web interface drives all six joints under safety limits. The workspace calibration
and the system-identification excitation runs are recordings taken from the robot, and every
measurement in \cref{sec:plant-id} came off it. What has never happened is a learned controller
closing a loop on the physical robot.

The sprint policies in \cref{tab:rl-results} are not deployable as they stand, independently of the
plant correction. They observe two privileged quantities that do not exist on the robot. One is
base linear velocity, which would require a state estimator that has not been built. The other is
the distance remaining to the finish line. A separate policy lineage was therefore started, for a
drivable demonstrator. That lineage removes both privileged quantities, so velocity must instead be
inferred from a 200\,ms strided observation history. It adds per-episode plant randomisation over
mass, inertia, friction, servo gains, joint damping, ankle spring stiffness, gravity tilt and
actuation delay. It adds a sensor-noise model, including the accelerometer leakage that makes a
real IMU's ``down'' swing by several degrees during push-off. And it adds the steering channels the
mirror-symmetric gait lacks. The lineage cannot warm-start from the sprint milestones, because both
the observation and the action dimensions changed, so it carries a separate training cost.
\Cref{sec:sim2real-lineage} reports its results and the diagnoses that produced them.

Domain randomisation ranges are now set from measurement rather than from taste, wherever a
measurement exists. Ground friction is sampled over 0.35 to 0.60 against a measured $\mu = 0.84$,
from a $40^\circ$ slip angle. That range sits deliberately well below the real value, because the
failure mode being guarded against is planting a foot that then slides. Joint homing error is
sampled at $5^\circ$ against a calibration accurate to 1 or 2$^\circ$, as a safety envelope. Where
no measurement exists, the range stays wide and is flagged as such. Link inertia tensors are
randomised at $\pm 25\,\%$ precisely because \cref{sec:plant-id-sysid} could not resolve the mass
distribution.

Three known gaps remain, recorded deliberately rather than discovered later. There is no policy
export path and no inference loop on the Pi, which is the critical path for everything else. The
actuator model still gives each motor an independent torque limit and speed limit, rather than a
coupled torque--speed envelope, which makes the simulation optimistic exactly in the high-speed
regime the project cares about. And first hardware bring-up remains planned with the base fixed and
the legs in the air. That configuration validates wiring, direction conventions, joint limits and
gait shape, but with no ground contact it says nothing about balance or transfer.

The five subsections that follow report what has happened since this status was written: a generator
A/B (\cref{sec:cpg-ab}), the ankle resolved as a build decision (\cref{sec:ankle-build}), the
drivable lineage's descent onto the measured plant (\cref{sec:sim2real-lineage}), a classical
baseline built to test whether learning was ever the bottleneck (\cref{sec:scripted-baseline}), and
the rebuilt robot (\cref{sec:rebuilt-robot}).

\subsubsection{A second generator: explicit-oscillator CPG versus the Fourier series}
\label{sec:cpg-ab}

The Fourier parameterisation of \cref{sec:rl-cpg} is one member of a family. The other canonical
member is the explicit limit-cycle oscillator of the Ijspeert school, as used by CPG-RL. The
literature gave no basis to prefer one over the other, so the question was measured instead:
sixteen cluster jobs, one plant, one reward, one schedule, and \emph{only the generator swapped}.
Building the oscillator arm forced a foot-space formulation with a measured inverse-kinematics
lookup table. Constructing that table surfaced four plant facts worth recording. From the stance
pose, \emph{both} cam and thigh move the toe mostly fore--aft, and either sign of cam
\emph{shortens} the near-straight leg. The intuitive ``thigh swings, cam lifts'' mapping is
therefore simply wrong, and it produces no step. The 4-bar has a folded solution branch that must be filtered
out. The inverse-kinematics inversion must propagate continuity between cells, or it flips branches
mid-swing. And reachable foot lift at mid-stride is only about 8\,cm, because the leg is
near-straight there.

Two results stand out beyond the tie.

First, the \textbf{pure oscillator with the residual channel removed produces stability without
locomotion}. It survives 9700 of 12\,000 steps, but travels 15\,m of 72 at 0.25\,m/s, with the
worst foot-scuffing cadence of all sixteen arms. That result \emph{refutes} the standing hypothesis
that the per-step residual channel was the open fast path causing the 6\,Hz footfall chatter of
\cref{sec:rl-cadence}, because removing the channel made the chatter worse rather than better. It
is also the empirical form of the literature's verdict that a per-step channel is mandatory.

Second, the full base-DOF ladder was subsequently run on the oscillator generator. It
\textbf{cleared m1 to m4, with a 100\,m dash at m2 and 2.95\,m/s at m3, and walled hard at m5,
which frees roll}. Episode length there was 337 against the Fourier ladder's 3213 at matched
budget, and the plateau was confirmed at 2.5$\times$ budget. On the roll axis the Fourier generator
succeeds where the oscillator does not. Both share the identical 3-parameter roll reflex, so the
difference lies in the generator itself, and it points future investigation at lateral authority.
The pragmatic conclusion adopted was to keep the Fourier generator for the deployment lineage, and
to use the oscillator result as tiebreak evidence that the action-space \emph{family} is right.

Getting this A/B to run also produced a curriculum lesson expensive enough to state as a rule. One
milestone in the ladder took six restarts, five of them self-inflicted. The terminal bug was a
competence gate that held \emph{both} the difficulty ramp and the efficiency-penalty ramp. For a
policy that never reached the gate, the efficiency terms stayed pinned at exactly zero for the
entire run. A run that looked like ``the robot cannot learn this'' was actually ``the reward is not
the one we designed''. What settled it was not another hypothesis but a field-by-field
configuration diff against the last run that worked. \textbf{Never gate the ramp that is supposed
to rescue competence on competence itself, and diff configurations before theorising.}

\subsubsection{The ankle, revisited as a build decision}
\label{sec:ankle-build}

The 350\,N$\cdot$m/rad ankle-stiffness result of \cref{sec:rl-ankle-overturned} answered a
simulation question. The hardware then asked a different one. The spring actually fabricated
measures $k = 41.4$\,N$\cdot$m/rad with \emph{no preload} (\cref{sec:plant-id-ankle}), and a spring
wound to 350 will not be manufactured. The question became what should be \emph{built}: the real
soft spring, or no spring at all. No spring means a rigid strut, which would also delete the 249\,g
spring assembly from each shin, on the axis the sensitivity analysis prices at
$-0.93$\,(m/s)/kg.

\paragraph{The soft no-preload spring is eliminated by statics alone, before any learning.} The
easiest load case there is holds the base and applies 0.5 body weights per foot in double support,
against a 3.5\,BW running requirement. Even there the as-built spring deflects the ankle
$26.6^\circ$ past flat and drops the robot 145\,mm. It also rotates the foot so far that the heel
carries 208\,N while the toe is driven 11.9\,mm \emph{into} the floor at the reset pose. No
controller fixes that. A policy chooses where to put a foot. It cannot make a spring hold seven
times its load. The instructive split is preload against stiffness. The same $k=41.4$ sags 19\,mm
\emph{with} the original preload and 145\,mm without it, so \textbf{the preload, not the stiffness,
was doing 7.6$\times$ of the work} in the shipped ankle.

\paragraph{The strut wins the learning screen.} The RL comparison ran cold m3 on the corrected
plant. The compression-capable strut arm reaches episode length 2307 in \emph{one third} the steps
the fully welded ankle needs, at 29\,M against 73\,M, and walks 3$\times$ more symmetrically. Given
time, the welded arm completes the 57\,m dash at 2.55\,m/s in 5 of 6 episodes. A tension-only
strut, with no compression capacity, manages 0.6\,m and stands on one leg. The compression capacity
is the entire story, because the arms are statically identical otherwise.

\paragraph{And the as-built part fails by arithmetic.} The fabricated strut is a solid PETG rod,
500\,mm long and 3\,mm across. Its slenderness is $L/r = 667$ and its Euler buckling load is
\textbf{0.24 to 0.33\,N}. That is thirty-five times below the 10\,N the simulation arm assumed. It
is also ten times less moment at the ankle than the foot's own gravity torque, so the rod cannot
even hold the foot flat in swing. Its tension capacity of 283\,N also falls below the 360\,N
walking peak. Two parts reproduce the simulated one: a 3\,mm pultruded carbon rod, at
$P_{cr} = 14.1$\,N, 4241\,N in tension and 5.7\,g, or a 6/4\,mm carbon tube at 181\,N.
\textbf{The ankle question closed as a build instruction: make it a stiff link, in carbon, and
delete the spring mass.}

The instruction was executed (\cref{sec:rebuilt-robot}). The ankle is now a rigid carbon tube
weighing 40\,g. It removes a net 209\,g per shin, taking the shin from 573 to 364\,g and the robot
to 14.72\,kg, on the axis worth $-0.93$\,(m/s)/kg. Taking 209\,g off each shin buys more predicted
speed than taking 1.8\,kg off the base would.

\subsubsection{The drivable-demonstrator lineage: closing on the measured plant}
\label{sec:sim2real-lineage}

The deployable lineage announced in \cref{sec:rl-validation} has since accumulated results. They
are the closest thing this project has to a sim-to-real verdict, short of untethered hardware. The
lineage removes both privileged observations, so velocity must be inferred from a 200\,ms strided
history. It adds steering, and it layers on the full sensor and plant randomisation package. Its
history is a sequence of measured diagnoses.

\paragraph{Steering was broken twice over, and both causes were in the plant model.} The first
policy could not turn right at all. An open-loop authority test isolated plant from policy, using a
hand-built gait with roll and pitch railed, and it found two faults. First, the \emph{model} is not
left/right symmetric: two linkage anchor coordinates differ by about 1\,mm in the wrong axes, a
CAD-export artefact worth $+0.42$\,rad/s of intrinsic yaw. Second, the two steering channels were
scaled inversely to their measured authority. Differential stance width is about 6.6$\times$
stronger per unit than differential stride, and the weak channel's entire authority, at
0.26\,rad/s, was smaller than the intrinsic bias. The fix randomises the anchor asymmetry by
$\pm$1.5\,mm rather than symmetrising the file. The real robot will be asymmetric too, and on a
perfectly symmetric plant the policy has no reason to learn the correction that transfers. After
the fix, the yaw-response slope went from 0.19 to 1.22, with both turn directions reachable.

\paragraph{Domain randomisation was the thing breaking the policy, and that was measured rather
than assumed.} A fixed evaluation over 20 seeded episodes removed one adversity at a time from a
trained policy. Pushes changed nothing, at 1.0$\times$ the mean time between falls. Sensor noise
gave 1.5$\times$ and trips gave 1.8$\times$. \textbf{Plant randomisation gave 19.8$\times$}: 95.4\,s
against a 4.8\,s mean time between falls, where a clean plant gives 196\,s and zero falls. The
policy walked fine. Full-width plant randomisation from step zero was what kept it down, while
every other axis already had a curriculum. Randomisation width now rides a gated, retreating ramp
like everything else. Three evaluation-hygiene rules were paid for here. Training reward across
runs with different curricula is not comparable, so always evaluate on a fixed task. Six evaluation
episodes gave a 5$\times$ spread on fall rate for the \emph{same} policy, so use at least 20 seeded
episodes. And a curriculum that can only advance will ride a policy down, so competence must gate
the \emph{advance}, not just the opening. One run peaked at 113\,M steps and then declined
monotonically for 215\,M more, while the clock-driven ramps kept tightening.

\paragraph{The measured plant kills a working policy when applied all at once, and does not when
applied as a curriculum.} Every measured correction was activated together: weighed masses,
144.5\,N$\cdot$m torque, the strut ankle, the 0.8\,Hz drive with 25\,ms delay, and friction
deliberately below the measured $\mu = 0.84$. The previous best walker collapsed, with nothing else
changed, from episode length 1448 and reward $+511$ to 251 and $-78$. The drive bandwidth was then
ramped logarithmically in hertz over 120\,M steps, because the filter coefficient is violently
nonlinear in bandwidth. The same policy completed the descent to the real 0.8\,Hz drive and
stabilised at \textbf{episode length 4938, peaking at 6833, and reward $+3271$}. That is three
times its parent's score, \emph{on the real drive}, and that checkpoint is the warm-start parent
for everything downstream. The residual gap to a deployable walker is honest but bounded. On the
hardest configuration the current generation survives a median 9.4\,s, with 3 of 12 seeded episodes
reaching the 60\,s cap, walking at about 0.14\,m/s with a limp. That result motivated a
command-range fix, now in the currently training generation: the command box asked for 0.6\,m/s
from a plant that tops out at 0.14\,m/s, forward commands above 0.3\,m/s are fatal, and
\emph{backward} is the safest direction.

\paragraph{The lineage also re-proved the ladder.} It is the one lineage in the project that trains
with all six base degrees of freedom free from step zero. It is also the one that does not
converge, showing three plateaus across roughly 1.4\,G steps. Meanwhile the only controller to
reach 2.55\,m/s on the \emph{measured} plant did so at milestone m3, with roll and yaw railed, in
73\,M steps. Everything that has ever worked on this robot used the base-DOF ladder, and an audit
of the archive, covering 97 runs at m3, makes that an empirical claim rather than a preference. Two
architectural responses are now in training. The first restarts the lineage \emph{through} the
ladder on the rebuilt plant. The second is an asymmetric actor--critic. The critic keeps the
privileged observation, and a separately supervised velocity-estimator head replaces the privileged
velocity for the actor. That head is trained on labels and detached from the policy gradient, so
that PPO cannot bend it toward whatever the actor wishes were true. Its motivation is the
measurement that only $R^2 \approx 0.81$ of base velocity is recoverable from the observation
history.

\paragraph{Two bugs from this lineage belong in every RL practitioner's checklist.} A warm-started
run inherited the \emph{source checkpoint's} RNG seed. Every warm-started ``seed replicate'' in the
project was therefore a bit-identical duplicate, verified across two seeds and 100 rollouts with
identical curves, until the seed was re-asserted after loading. The second bug was in the
evaluator, which rebuilt the environment from the config's \emph{starting} values rather than the
curriculum's final ones. It read a 340\,M-step policy at 32$\times$ below its true performance.
That was the fourth evaluation-restore bug of the project, and it is the reason the rule
``\texttt{curriculum.json} is ground truth for anything a curriculum moved'' now exists. One
related point matters for reporting. Outcomes on the hard plant are \emph{bimodal}: a policy either
falls at about 2\,s or survives 60\,s. Unpaired small-sample comparisons are therefore noise, and
one early comparison ``found'' a 27$\times$ sensor-noise effect that does not exist. Common random
numbers and survivor counts, over at least twelve episodes, are the reporting standard for
everything above.

\subsubsection{A scripted baseline: 21 gains win at m2 and hit the same wall at m3}
\label{sec:scripted-baseline}

Late in the project a classical controller was written for the same plant. It combines a
clock-driven foot trajectory through the measured foot inverse kinematics, once-per-swing Raibert
and capture-point foot placement, low-gain pitch and roll trims, and feedforward inversion of the
0.8\,Hz drive. It uses 21 hand-set gains and no training. It is injected through the environment's
residual channel, so that every plant feature (drive model, delay, randomisation, contact and
termination) is byte-identical to the RL runs. Its purpose was to answer a question the RL curves
cannot. When training stalls, is the \emph{learning} the bottleneck, or the robot?

The controller's structure is itself an argument about the plant. The foot-placement target is
latched \emph{once per swing} rather than tracked continuously. The reason is that the drive is a
0.8\,Hz pole, with $\tau = 199$\,ms, against an inverted-pendulum divergence constant of
$\sqrt{h/g} = 311$\,ms. A continuous pitch loop would need crossover around 1\,Hz \emph{through} a
0.8\,Hz actuator pole, and would spend about $60^\circ$ of phase before acting. Latching at swing
entry instead gives the drive a whole swing, about $1.8\tau$, which delivers about 83\,\% of the
commanded placement by touchdown. The same arithmetic sets the tuned cadence near 1\,Hz, because at
2\,Hz the drive returns 0.38 of the commanded amplitude. It also motivates the one element RL
structurally lacks: explicit feedforward inversion of the drive pole, as
$q_{\text{cmd}} = q + \tau\,\dot q$.

The answer is sharp in both directions. On the height-free, attitude-railed plant at m2, the
scripted controller runs \textbf{60\,s on all 5 seeds}, where the RL reference achieves
20.3\,$\pm$\,21.6\,s. It wins less on the mean than on the variance, because the RL outcome is
bimodal. On the pitch-free plant at m3 it survives \textbf{1.0\,s, on 0 of 5 seeds}, against the RL
reference's 1.15\,s. That is \emph{the same wall, to the decimal}.

\paragraph{The wall is free fall, proved by a formula with no controller in it.} Modelling the
robot as a rigid inverted pendulum about the toe, the passive topple time from initial lean
$\theta_0$ is $t = \sqrt{h/g}\,\cosh^{-1}(60^\circ/\theta_0)$. Sweeping gravity in simulation:

Three mechanisms compose the wall. First, the stance keyframe starts every episode with the toes
9.1\,cm ahead of the centre of mass, which is a $5.67^\circ$ backward lean at $t=0$. The model's own
comment claims ``feet under the CoM'', and measurement says otherwise. Correcting the keyframe buys
exactly the free-fall time of the residual lean, and no more. Second, the single toe-sphere contact
gives \emph{zero} centre-of-pressure authority at any ankle stiffness. The ankle at m3 is not weak,
it is disconnected. Third, foot placement is geometrically outrun. The toe hangs about 0.92\,m below
the centre of mass, so a pitch rate $\dot\theta$ sweeps the contact point forward at $L\dot\theta$.
That sweep was measured to beat the controller's corrective foot motion 2:1, until the
$\pm$0.30\,m fore--aft workspace saturates in under a second. Escaping m3 therefore needs one of
two things. Either a corrected keyframe \emph{plus} a foot with real fore--aft extent and an ankle
strong enough to use it, or step-on-demand triggering in place of a free-running gait clock. That
is a plant conclusion rather than a controller one, which is exactly what the identical RL and
scripted failures imply.

Two further findings transfer beyond the baseline. The stand keyframe's control vector is not a
static equilibrium: the position servo needs about 6\,cm more commanded foot extension to hold
15\,kg. RL policies discover that silently, and hand-built controllers must be told. And
\emph{feedforward beats feedback through this drive}. A constant extension offset stands for 20\,s,
where adding ride-height feedback drops it to 7.8\,s, because a continuous loop closed through a
0.8\,Hz pole against a plant diverging in 0.31\,s destabilises. A methodological rhyme closes the
section. The baseline's automatic gain tuner was given the objective
$t_{\text{alive}} + 3\,\mathrm{dist}$, and it immediately exploited that objective exactly as a
policy would. It drove at 1.6\,m/s against a 0.3\,m/s command, to farm the distance term on the
pitch-railed plant. \textbf{Reward exploitation is a property of optimisation against an objective,
not of neural networks.}

\paragraph{Why the learned controller did not win, and what that says about stability margin.}
\label{sec:margin-analysis}
\Cref{sec:bg-margin} states the theoretical expectation. The scripted controller lies inside the
policy class, so with an exact plant and unbounded training the learned optimum can only match or
exceed it. The measurement disagrees at both milestones, and it disagrees \emph{for different
reasons at each}. That difference is the part worth reporting. Collapsing the two milestones into a
single ``RL underperformed'' loses the whole finding.

\emph{At m2, a margin existed and the learning did not find it.} The scripted controller survives
60\,s on 5 of 5 seeds, against the learned reference's 20.3\,$\pm$\,21.6\,s. That is not a plant
limit. The plant is stabilisable, and one of the two controllers stabilises it. Three mechanisms
account for the gap, and each is a statement about the optimisation rather than about the policy
class.

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{The feedforward inverse is representable but not discoverable.} The scripted
        controller inverts the drive pole explicitly, as $q_{\text{cmd}} = q + \tau\dot q$.
        Nothing prevents a network from expressing that map, because it is linear in quantities
        the observation history contains. Expressing it and \emph{finding} it are different
        problems. The gradient signal for phase lead is indirect. A policy is rewarded for
        surviving, not for leading the plant by 199\,ms, and the credit for a correctly
        anticipated command arrives several control steps after the action that earned it. A
        universal policy class does not confer the credit assignment needed to exploit it.
  \item \textbf{Margin is not in the objective, and the variance is the evidence.} The return
        prices survival time and distance. It prices the distance from the state to the failure
        boundary nowhere. An optimiser given that objective has no reason not to park close to the
        boundary and collect the reward. The signature of a policy that has done so is bimodality
        rather than degradation: it works, or it falls. That is exactly the measured shape.
        A figure of 20.3\,$\pm$\,21.6\,s carries a standard deviation larger than its own mean,
        against 5 of 5 at the episode cap. \textbf{The seed variance is the margin measurement.} A
        controller with margin does not produce a bimodal seed distribution, and a learning curve
        reporting the mean will not show it.
  \item \textbf{``Unbounded training'' is doing real work in the theoretical claim.} A milestone
        here is 600\,M environment-steps and a multi-day cluster job (\cref{sec:rl-results}), and
        the project as a whole spent roughly 3 billion. That is a large budget, and it is not the
        asymptotic one the argument assumes.
\end{itemize}

\emph{At m3, no margin existed for either controller.} Survival equals the analytic free-fall time
of an \emph{uncontrolled} inverted pendulum, to within 3 to 9\,\%, across a 5$\times$ change in the
plant's time constant (\cref{tab:freefall}). The formula that predicts it contains no controller
term. The mechanisms above are the reason. With zero centre-of-pressure authority and foot
placement outrun 2:1, the set of stabilising action sequences from the initial condition is
essentially empty, and every controller drawn from an empty feasible set performs identically.
Reinforcement learning optimises over the space of \emph{controllers}. It has no purchase on the
space of \emph{physics}. The theoretical margin advantage is not wrong at m3. It is vacuous,
because the quantity it promises to enlarge is already zero.

Two further observations tie this to the rest of the chapter.

First, consider the mechanism one would normally use to convert the theoretical advantage into a
real one. That mechanism randomises what is not known, so that the policy is optimal over a set of
plants rather than over one. On this robot it was measured to \emph{cost} more than it bought. Domain
randomisation was the thing breaking the drivable policy (\cref{sec:sim2real-lineage}), and the
measured plant applied all at once killed a working policy that survived the same correction
applied as a curriculum. The robustness randomisation buys is paid for out of performance margin,
and a plant whose margin is already near zero has nothing to pay with. On a robot with margin to
spare the trade is invisible. Here it is the dominant term.

Second, margin is a frequency-domain quantity, and neither method can synthesise phase the actuator
does not have. A 199\,ms drive pole against a 311\,ms divergence constant bounds what \emph{any}
controller can achieve, learned or hand-built. That is why the drive loop appears in
\cref{sec:speed-ceiling-corrected} and \cref{sec:future} as a mechanical priority rather than as a
tuning one.

The claim all of this supports is narrower and more useful than either ``RL beats classical'' or
the reverse. \textbf{The stability-margin advantage of learning scales with how much margin the
plant has to give.} On a plant with margin, learning must still be paid before it collects, in
reward design that prices the boundary and in the training budget to reach the asymptote. On a
plant without margin, the comparison measures the robot, and the correct response is to change the
robot.

\subsubsection{Postscript: the rebuilt robot, and a cap that was never there}
\label{sec:rebuilt-robot}

On 2026-08-10 the hardware caught up with the analysis, and the training plant was re-issued to
match. Four things changed. Three of them are the physical robot converging on the model's
recommendations. One is a modelling gap that had been open the entire project.

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{The actuators' speed was never capped in simulation.} Peak torque had always been
        limited, at the measured 61.2 and 144.5\,N$\cdot$m, but joint \emph{velocity} was
        unconstrained. A position servo could therefore crank a joint arbitrarily fast for free.
        The record shows what that bought. The celebrated k350 pitch-balance winner
        (\cref{sec:rl-pitch-wall}) ``balanced'' partly by pattering one foot at 11\,Hz, with a
        peak thigh speed of 23\,rad/s, \emph{past the motor's 22\,rad/s no-load ceiling}. The cap
        is now set per joint, at the measured no-load output speeds: 10.30\,rad/s at hip roll and
        22.01\,rad/s at cam and thigh. It is per joint because the two actuator families differ by
        2.1$\times$, so any scalar cap is wrong for one of them. The episode is one more entry in
        the pattern of \cref{sec:sim-hygiene}: an unconstrained axis is an exploit waiting to be
        found.
  \item \textbf{The ankle is now the rigid carbon tube} of \cref{sec:ankle-build}. It is stiff,
        symmetric in push and pull, and has no buckling limit. It replaces both the soft spring
        and the PETG rod that modelled as tension-only.
  \item \textbf{The mass moved the right way}: a net $-209$\,g per shin, for a total of 14.72\,kg.
  \item \textbf{The drive was retuned from 0.8 to about 3\,Hz.} The retune was measured by
        repeating the swept-sine of \cref{sec:plant-id-drive}, after adjusting the boards'
        position-loop gains with the vendor tool. The left thigh moved from 0.86 to 3.35\,Hz and
        the cam from 0.85 to 2.88\,Hz. The hip-roll axis was left untouched deliberately, as the
        in-run control, and moved only from 0.74 to 0.76\,Hz. The uniform 3\,Hz assumption is
        flagged, because hip roll uses a different actuator and has not been re-measured, so the
        roll milestones may need re-baselining. The retune softens the current-mode conclusion of
        \cref{sec:plant-id-drive} without retiring it: 3\,Hz is still below the 4\,Hz top of the
        gait-frequency range.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{The bandwidth bracket that justified the retune.} A three-arm experiment settled whether
drive bandwidth was what stood between the robot and walking, and it ran before the ladder
was relaunched. Three identical cold m2 runs used drive bandwidths of 0.8, 3 and 12\,Hz, with
everything else fixed, and with the measured 25\,ms delay and the workspace kill retained. At
0.8\,Hz, which is the robot as it was, the policy \emph{shuffles and never walks}: duty factor
1.00, then a fall. At 3\,Hz it \textbf{walks}, covering 20.75\,m at 0.69\,m/s with genuine per-foot
flight. At 12\,Hz, which is effectively the unlimited drive the whole 2.55\,m/s sprint record was
trained in, it is \emph{worse} than at 3\,Hz, and folds one leg into the workspace kill. Three
conclusions follow. The measured retune sits, fortuitously, near the sweet spot. The old
unlimited-bandwidth plant was not merely optimistic but qualitatively misleading. And the check
cannot be done by replaying a policy trained at 0.8\,Hz at a higher bandwidth, which was tried and
simply moves faster and falls. The policy has to be \emph{trained} at the new bandwidth.

On this rebuilt plant the scripted controller of \cref{sec:scripted-baseline} was re-tuned against
a full mission schedule: stand, walk forward, stand, walk backward, stand. It completes the mission
in one 35\,s episode on 3 of 3 seeds, at a drive-delivery ratio of 0.98 against 0.31 on the 0.8\,Hz
drive. The deliverable is small, but it is the first controller of any kind to demonstrate
command-following locomotion on the plant the robot actually is. Tuning against the full mission
mattered. A constant-velocity tune produced a walker that could not stand, which is the scripted
twin of the RL command-curriculum lesson. \textbf{The m3 pitch wall survives the retune.} It is
still about 1.0\,s, still free fall, for both controller families, which confirms it as structural
rather than bandwidth-limited.

A second, more ambitious scripted controller was built on the rebuilt plant: a capture-step state
machine. It \emph{failed}, and on the way down it measured four plant facts that any future
controller inherits, learned or not. At 96\,\% of reach, commanded leg \emph{extension} does not
exist: a $-65$\,mm vertical command moves the toe $+85$\,mm \emph{forward} and 8\,mm down through
the inverse kinematics, so weight support must be commanded in joint space rather than in task
space. Capture steps land on air: the swung foot hovers 1 to 5\,cm high through an entire fall,
from rear-workspace inverse-kinematics error plus pitch lift, and a ground-seeking touchdown helps
without sufficing. The 4-bar loop-closure constraint itself yields about 15\,cm of ride height
under single-leg load, with every actuated joint on target, and only a forward-kinematics height
loop that includes the passive joints can see it. And the plant's soft modes, measured by impulse
response, put a weakly damped vertical body bounce near 1\,Hz exactly where discrete stepping at
2.3\,Hz pumps it. That last fact is the architectural reason the shuffling gait beats ``smarter''
stepping on this robot.

\subsection{Deployment}
\label{sec:deploy}

The policies of \cref{sec:rl} ultimately have to run on the Raspberry Pi described in
\cref{sec:compute-choice}, against real drives, with no simulator to reset. Four questions decide
whether that is viable. Can a non-realtime Linux close a control loop fast enough
(\cref{sec:deploy-linux})? Can the bus carry the traffic, and what happens when it cannot
(\cref{sec:deploy-can-runtime})? What does the drive actually accept and report
(\cref{sec:deploy-drive})? And, the question this project learned the hard way, when something
breaks on hardware, is the failure \emph{investigable} (\cref{sec:deploy-blackbox})? Measurement
settled the first three. An incident settled the fourth.

\subsubsection{Linux without a real-time kernel}
\label{sec:deploy-linux}

The Pi runs a stock, non-realtime kernel. The loop-timing measurement of \cref{sec:plant-id-timing}
established that the stock kernel suffices. A 200\,Hz loop holds at half budget, with jitter well
under a millisecond, and the drives broadcast at exactly 200\,Hz anyway, so a faster loop would
have nothing to read.

One Python-specific detail is load-bearing enough to record. CPython hands the interpreter lock
between threads at most every \texttt{switchinterval} seconds. The default is 5\,ms, which is
\emph{exactly} one control period, so the web-interface thread could legally hold the interpreter
across an entire control tick. Dropping the interval to 0.5\,ms, with
\texttt{sys.setswitchinterval(0.0005)}, took the daemon's late ticks from roughly 10\,\% to a few
percent. The steady state was then measured with the control daemon, the 200\,Hz IMU thread, the
web interface and the flight recorder of \cref{sec:deploy-blackbox} all running. It is
\textbf{188\,Hz against a 200\,Hz target, at a 5.30\,ms mean period and 3\,\% late ticks}. The
missing 6\,\% is operating-system scheduling, and no lever short of a realtime kernel recovers it.

An A/B under \texttt{systemd} overrides settled where the time actually goes. The daemon was
restarted between arms, on an otherwise idle robot.

\paragraph{A methodological trap, again.} The first loop-rate figures taken during this bring-up
read 60\,Hz with 34\,\% late ticks. They produced a confident, and committed, diagnosis of an
expensive failure path in the CAN layer. The diagnosis was wrong, because the A/B above shows the
suspected path costs nothing. The 60\,Hz was the \emph{measurement disturbing the measured}. While
the Pi was being sampled, \texttt{scp}, remote compilation, \texttt{journalctl} and HTTP polling
were all running on its four cores. That is the same failure mode as the IMU noise figures taken on
a swaying rig (\cref{sec:sensors}): the number was real, the attribution was not. Two rules follow.
Never benchmark the robot over the same SSH session that is deploying to it. And keep the
retraction in the history rather than rewriting it.

\paragraph{Process supervision.} The daemon runs under \texttt{systemd}. It starts at boot and
restarts on crash, bounded to five restarts per minute so that a crash loop stays visibly down
instead of power-cycling the motors. Its stdout goes to the journal. It is stopped by
\texttt{SIGINT}, so that the shutdown path runs. That path streams zero current to every motor and
writes the recorder's clean-stop record. The \emph{absence} of that record is how a power cut is
later inferred (\cref{sec:deploy-blackbox}). Before supervision, the only record of the daemon's
death was whatever terminal happened to still be open.

\subsubsection{CAN at runtime: the bus with nothing on it}
\label{sec:deploy-can-runtime}

\Cref{sec:deploy-can} describes the protocol itself, and bandwidth is a non-issue at 26\,\% of one
of two 1\,Mbit buses. What the protocol description does not cover is the failure mode that running
under \texttt{systemd} made routine: \textbf{the daemon now starts before the motor power is on}.
With nothing on the bus to acknowledge a frame, socketcan's ten-frame transmit queue fills within
one tick, and every subsequent \texttt{send()} raises \texttt{ENOBUFS}. On its first boot under
\texttt{systemd}, that exception went unhandled in a background thread and killed the control
daemon outright, during its own preflight. The bug predates the service. Hand-started sessions had
simply always had the motors powered first.

The fix treats a send failure as an \emph{event, not a death}. Sends never raise past the CAN
layer, failures are counted per channel, and a channel that fails ten consecutive sends is retried
at 5\,Hz instead of 200. One successful send restores the full rate. Two honest measurements
qualify the fix. First, a failing send costs nothing. With the backoff disabled and three sends per
tick failing, the loop runs at 188.9\,Hz, against 188.5\,Hz with the backoff enabled. The backoff
is kept for hygiene, since it removes 600 pointless system calls per second, and for forensics.
``We did not attempt the frame'' and ``we attempted it and it failed'' are different facts, and the
recorder logs both. Second, safety does not rest on the backoff. Motors on a dead bus report
nothing, so the motion interlocks refuse to command them. And a bus that starts refusing frames
\emph{while} the robot is being commanded trips the safety stop within half a second.

\subsubsection{The drive as deployed}
\label{sec:deploy-drive}

Three properties of the CubeMars drives dominate the deployment design. All three were measured
rather than documented (\cref{sec:plant-id-drive,sec:deploy-can}).

First, the only writable surface is the servo-mode command pair, \texttt{SET\_POS} and
\texttt{SET\_CURRENT}. The internal cascaded gains are board configuration, readable through the
vendor tool but not over CAN, and the position loop they implement is proportional-only with a
0.8\,Hz corner.

Second, the drives report no bus voltage, so pack sag under load is unobservable from the drive
side.

Third, and with the sharpest consequences, \textbf{the multi-turn position origin is not stable}.
It re-randomises on every power cycle, and the incident below is consistent with it moving
\emph{between} power cycles as well. Every absolute position command is therefore only as good as a
calibration captured earlier in the same session. Nothing in the drive signals when that
calibration has silently become wrong.

\subsubsection{The safety envelope as an instrument}
\label{sec:safety}

A browser-based control interface runs on the robot, and it is the only path through which the
hardware is commanded by hand. It is treated here as methodology rather than as tooling, because
the safety envelope it enforces is also an experimental instrument.

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{Measured joint limits, not CAD limits.} Each joint is backdriven by hand through
  its full travel with the motors limp, and the excursion is recorded. The result is a per-joint
  range that reflects the assembled robot, including cabling and interference. The 4-bar's
  assemblable band is a constraint on a joint \emph{pair}, so the recorded reachable region is a
  box in the foot's fore--aft and lift coordinates, not a set of independent joint ranges.
  \item \textbf{The recorded workspace is reused in simulation.} The same measured box is applied
  as a termination criterion during training. An episode ends the moment a foot leaves the region
  the real robot is known to reach. The criterion began as a safety measure and became a
  sim-to-sim gate, because it makes an entire class of simulator-only exploit impossible rather
  than merely expensive. It must also be applied carefully. It is expressed in the base frame, so
  by itself it cannot distinguish a collapsed ankle from a folded linkage (\cref{sec:sim-hygiene}).
  \item \textbf{Playback is capped by kinematics, not by preference.} A part failed during a
  high-cadence trajectory playback, and the frequency ceiling was reduced from 5\,Hz to 2.5\,Hz.
  The new number was derived rather than guessed. Differentiating the stored waveform gives the
  peak joint speed per unit cadence, which is a property of the trajectory \emph{shape}. The cam
  joint demands 359\,deg/s per hertz, and reaches the actuator's no-load speed at 3.5\,Hz. Past
  that point the command is not merely hard to track but kinematically impossible, and the drive
  simply saturates current chasing a target it cannot reach. At the 5\,Hz previously allowed, the
  cam was being commanded at 143\,\% of no-load speed. The 2.5\,Hz ceiling leaves it at 71\,\%.
  Raising the ceiling again requires a hardware argument. Because the limit depends on the shape, a
  gentler trajectory can safely go faster.
  \item \textbf{Trip conditions.} Tracking error, joint range and temperature are monitored
  continuously, and any of them latches the drives to zero current. The tracking-error trip in
  particular had to be decoupled from the commanded trajectory, once \cref{sec:plant-id-drive}
  showed that a large tracking error at speed is the \emph{expected} behaviour of this drive rather
  than a fault.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Hardware validation: the flight recorder and the pre-move guard}
\label{sec:deploy-blackbox}

On 2026-08-10 a joint destroyed itself on the bench. A zero pose had been captured, and by the time
an absolute move was commanded, the drives' raw origin no longer matched it. The drive faithfully
executed a position command in the wrong frame, and wound \texttt{left.cam} through roughly 1.9
output turns on a joint with $\pm86^\circ$ of travel. Investigating it was \emph{impossible}. The
only telemetry in the system was a 4096-sample RAM ring at 20\,Hz, of which the HTTP interface
could serve the last 25.6 seconds, and nothing was written to disk. By the time anyone looked, the
window had rolled past the event. The rest of this section describes what was built in response.
``Make the failure investigable'' turned out to be a design problem with measurable requirements,
not a logging afterthought.

\paragraph{A three-tier flight recorder.} Streaming the full state to disk continuously is not an
option. Six motors at 200\,Hz produce 35\,kB/s, or 3\,GB/day, which fills an SD card and then
destroys it. The recorder therefore splits by rate.

\centering
\small
\begin{tabular}{p{1.2cm}p{6.4cm}p{6.6cm}}
\toprule
\textbf{Tier} & \textbf{Content} & \textbf{Mechanism} \\
\midrule
A & every field, continuously & 20\,Hz, fixed-width binary segments, rotated under a global disk budget \\
B & the black box proper: the full 200\,Hz state & a 40\,s RAM ring, dumped to its own file on any trigger, \emph{pre-trigger history included}, plus a 3\,s post-trigger tail \\

\label{tab:blackbox-tiers}
\end{table}

Two constraints shaped the implementation. First, the control loop must not pay for its own
instrumentation. The 200\,Hz thread only appends a tuple to a bounded queue, at about 2\,$\mu$s,
and \cref{tab:deploy-ab} confirms that the end-to-end cost is unmeasurable. A separate writer
thread owns all file I/O. A full queue \emph{drops and counts}, and the running drop count is
stamped into the next accepted record, because a black box that cannot prove where its own gaps are
is worse than none. Second, the robot must never depend on the recorder. If the writer thread dies,
the control loop continues, and the interface surfaces the failure.

\paragraph{Time, without a trustworthy clock.} The Pi has no real-time clock, and its access point
has no internet, so the wall clock after boot is whatever \texttt{fake-hwclock} restored: plausible
and wrong. Every record therefore carries four time fields. The monotonic clock is the primary time
base. The wall time comes with an explicit \emph{trusted} flag, set only once NTP has actually
synchronised. A per-boot kernel identifier and a per-run session identifier ensure that records
from different sessions can never be silently interleaved. When NTP does land, the resulting step
is itself logged, at $+1333.5$\,s on the first instrumented boot, so that earlier stamps can be
corrected offline. Power-off is handled by inversion. A process cannot log its own death, so a
heartbeat is \texttt{fsync}'d every 8\,s, and the last heartbeat before a gap \emph{is} the
power-off time, to heartbeat resolution. The offline reader states that inference explicitly rather
than implying a clean shutdown.

\paragraph{The pre-move guard.} The incident's mechanism was a full-authority slew against a stale
calibration. Four mechanisms close it, and none of them adds a step to the normal workflow. That
last clause is a requirement, not a nicety. An early draft forbade homing as the first move after
calibration, and that was exactly backwards: after a zero capture the legs are limp and sagging,
and homing is precisely what catches them.

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{Hold before moving.} The first command after enabling is ``hold the raw position
  you are reporting right now'', for 0.35\,s. It never consults the calibration, so it is safe even
  against a completely wrong zero, and it arrests the sag immediately. Only then does the slew to
  an absolute target begin.
  \item \textbf{Raw-at-rest fingerprint.} The raw encoder pose of all six motors is recorded at
  every successful zero capture. Before leaving the limp state, the live raw pose is compared
  against that record. The comparison is decisive after a restart, because the origin
  re-randomises, so a calibration restored from disk is refused until the robot is re-zeroed.
  Within a live session a 200\,Hz continuity watchdog is used instead. An origin \emph{rewrite}
  moves the reported position while the drive reports no speed, and that signature is exactly how
  a rewrite is distinguished from the joint being moved by hand. A range-sanity check backstops
  both: a calibration implying $678^\circ$ on an $\pm88^\circ$ axis is refused outright.
  \item \textbf{A homing-specific tracking threshold}, at $8^\circ$ rather than the $25^\circ$
  playback value. A slow guided slew should track well, so a stale zero shows up as tracking error
  within the first few degrees of travel.
  \item \textbf{A per-joint travel budget}, as the final backstop. A guided move that sweeps more
  than $1.3\times$ the joint's own range without arriving is stopped. On 2026-08-10 that check
  alone would have cut the excursion from 1.9 output turns to under 0.6.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Acceptance.} A hardware-free test reproduces the shape of the incident. The mock bus
grew an \texttt{origin\_shift} fault injection, which moves the reported \emph{and} the commanded
frame while the shaft stays put and reports zero speed. That is what a board renumbering its origin
actually looks like, and it is what makes the fault invisible to any check that trusts reported
velocity. The test captures a zero, shifts the origin and commands a home. It then asserts three
things, all without a human opening a raw file. The guard must refuse, with both raw poses on
record. The triggered dump must contain at least ten seconds of pre-trigger 200\,Hz data. And the
offline reader's \texttt{--postmortem} mode must state what happened: boot and power-loss times,
the raw-pose comparison, commanded against reported position through the event, the configuration
live at that instant, and whether the origin ever moved while the robot was limp and untouched.

\paragraph{What it found in its first hour.} The recorder justified itself before the day ended.
Its first boot on the robot captured its own deployment failure: the \texttt{ENOBUFS} crash of
\cref{sec:deploy-can-runtime}, complete with traceback, which had previously been invisible. Its
steady-state telemetry then established that the left-leg bus was electrically dead, with zero
frames transmitted and all three motors silent. That turned ``the robot feels wrong'' into a
specific, timestamped fact. It also ended a longer-standing blindness. Nothing in the stack had
ever measured the control loop's actual rate, so a loop running at a third of its nominal frequency
had been indistinguishable from a healthy one. The tick counter the recorder stamps into every
sample is what made every figure in \cref{tab:deploy-ab} measurable at all.

\subsection{Hardware experiments}
\label{sec:hw-experiments}

Everything in this section ran on the physical robot, through the web instrument of
\cref{sec:deploy}. \Cref{sec:plant-id} reports the heavy measurement results, which are only
situated here.

\paragraph{In-air gait demonstration and teach-and-replay.} The first hardware milestone was a
hand-authored straight-walking gait, with the body clamped in the air and no balance required. A
pure-NumPy gait generator streamed servo-mode position commands over CAN. Its safer successor is
teach-and-replay. The motors are held limp by streaming zero current, a leg is backdriven by hand
through the desired cycle, and takes are recorded per side. A pure-NumPy pipeline then resamples,
phase-aligns, detects the left/right mirror, closes the loop with an FFT low-pass, and replays the
result. Replay runs either as drive-side position commands, or through a software PID onto current
commands with a hard ampere clamp as the torque limit. Replaying on the robot exposed a compliance
problem that later measurements explained. At low proportional gain the leg drew only about 2\,A
against a 20\,A allowance, and moved with stick--slip judder. The practical fix was to raise $k_p$,
add $k_i$ against the gravity lag, and take the derivative on target minus actual velocity. That
was an early, unquantified encounter with the drive-bandwidth ceiling of \cref{sec:plant-id-drive}.

\paragraph{Workspace calibration.} The safe joint-space workspace was recorded by backdriving each
leg through its physically assemblable region, then eroding the recorded polygon. It does triple
duty, as the training-time constraint, the runtime watchdog and the offline deploy gate.
Calibration on hardware corrected the model in one way: \textbf{the cam is a full
crank}. The CAD-guessed joint range of $\pm86^\circ$ is wrong. Recorded sweeps span about
$245^\circ$ of cam rotation, and the 4-bar assembles at every angle. The calibrated zero pose also
sits about $1^\circ$ outside the eroded safe band, so the safety net refuses a naive hold-at-zero
and lets a guided homing move through the physical-feasibility check instead
(\cref{sec:deploy-blackbox}).

\paragraph{The identification campaign.} Quasi-static and swept-sine excitations were captured at
200\,Hz, through the measurement mode of the web instrument. They produced the plant corrections of
\cref{sec:plant-id}: the known-mass $K_t$ measurement, the 0.8\,Hz drive Bode response, the
gravity-model residual and its rejected explanations. The campaign also demonstrated the instrument
itself. Runs are self-describing, because each capture embeds the calibration, the drive gains and
the weighed masses. The excitation planner now solves amplitude against travel, no-load speed and
predicted tracking error jointly. It does so because an early sweep e-stopped the run six seconds
in, by asking the drive for more than its position loop can execute.

\paragraph{Sensor bench.} The IMU characterisation of \cref{sec:sensors} was performed in place on
the robot, with the provenance-labelling scheme described there. It covers noise, latency, filter
behaviour, and the motion-gating methodology that its first wrong numbers forced.

\paragraph{The incident, and what it built.} On 2026-08-10 a stale calibration met a
full-authority absolute move and destroyed a joint (\cref{sec:deploy-blackbox}). That the
investigation was impossible was itself the finding. It produced the flight recorder, the pre-move
guard and the systemd supervision of \cref{sec:deploy}, all validated by reproducing the incident
against the mock CAN bus. The recorder's first hour on hardware then found a daemon-killing crash
at boot, caused by an unpowered CAN bus, and established that the left-leg bus was electrically
dead. That is the robot's state as this is written: the right leg is fully functional under the
200\,Hz stack, measured at 188\,Hz, and the left leg awaits repair of the failed joint.

\subsubsection{Static end-effector force map}
\label{sec:hw-force-map}

The cam actuator reaches the knee through the pushrod 4-bar, so the transmission from motor torque
to toe force is a strong function of posture. Whether the strong direction is the direction running
needs had never been checked.

The constrained toe Jacobian differentiates the loop closure out, so it carries the linkage's
transmission ratio. Statics against the actuator torque box then gives the feasible force set at
every reachable toe position. Self-tests check loop closure against the stance keyframe, the
analytic Jacobian against central differences, and a fast analytic ankle column against the full
Jacobian.

Three findings follow. All three are statics-only, so they bound capability rather than predict
behaviour.

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{The leg stands at 96\,\% of its reach}, with about 3\,mm of further extension
  available anywhere near the stance position. Everything else follows from sitting that close to
  the leg-extension singularity.
  \item \textbf{Force there is 19:1 anisotropic}: 16.4 body weights straight down against 0.85 body
  weights horizontally. That is the same near-singular corner the speed-ceiling analysis found from
  the power side, seen now from the force side. It is a property of the fully extended pose rather
  than of the whole workspace. Over the running stance region the median tilted capacity recovers
  to 5.95\,BW, because compressed postures rotate their strong axis.
  \item \textbf{The motors are not the binding constraint. The ankle is, by a factor of eight.}
  Over the stance region the actuators give a median vertical capacity of 4.30\,BW, and 77\,\% of
  the region clears the 3.5\,BW running requirement. The passive ankle gives a median of 0.42\,BW,
  and \textbf{0.0\,\%} of the region clears the requirement.
\end{itemize}

The last point is a third independent route to the conclusion of \cref{sec:rl-ankle-overturned} and
\cref{sec:speed-ceiling}, reached from statics with no learning and no dynamics. The linkage and
the motor sizing are adequate to generous. The foot spring is the design defect.
