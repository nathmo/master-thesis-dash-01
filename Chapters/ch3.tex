\section{The robot and its digital twin}
\label{sec:robot}

This thesis needed to first finish the Dash-01 robot.
This implied to choose the computer, its actuator and the communication bus.
It also needed a battery.

Once that was done, its digital twin was develop from the CAD data and validated against measured value from the assembled robot.
Finally the model what could not be derived from the cad was measured on the real robot (like actuator bandwidth) to limit the sim2real risk.

\subsubsection{Battery choice}
\label{sec:hw-battery}

A database of roughly 430 commercial cells was built based of the same principle being the actuator database.
Knowing that we need a 48V pack, the database was filtered to cells that can be used in a 13S configuration.
It was also known from the motor section that we needed a pack that could deliver at least 160 A peak with the assumption that only half of the motor would be at maximum torque at once.
From them,a 13S2P pack of EVE INR21700-50PL was selected.
The selection criterion where a good compromise between energy density, power density and cost with the hard contraint of having a 48V pack (highest voltage before requiring different motor drivers).

The pack can be monitored over bluetooth and it's limit configured there using the DALY bms app.
A can interface is also available but not used in this project due to lack of time.

The internal resistance is stated by the manufacturer to be less than 10 mOhm per cell.
When drawing the full 360 A peak, the voltage drop is therefore expected to be up to 23 V which is quite significant.
In practice, the battery will realistically reach peak of 150 A but not much more.
This mean a drop of 10V which should not trip the BMS low-voltage protection given the battery is fully charged.
Due to the lack of voltage feedback on the motor drivers, this figure has not been verified on the real robot.

\subsection{The digital twin}
\label{sec:twin}
A simulation ready model was derived from the CAD data. The model is exported in two formats, URDF and MJCF.
The detail procedure to export the model is described in the appendix \cref{sec:app-pipeline}.
This took a lot of time and effort due to lack of experience and the poor documentation of the plugin used to export the model.
The final recipes consisted into exporting each segment of the robot as a STEP file with the origin at the joint (except for the base which was in its center).
Care was taken to ensure the references axis where aligned in a consistent manner across the segments.
The robot model was reassembled in autodesk Fusion 360 and exported to URDF using the ACDC4Robot plugin\cite{qiu2024describing}.
The kinematic loop where broken in the URDF and closed in the MJCF using an equality constraint between two sites, one on the pushrod and one on the shin.
This was necessary because URDF is a tree format and cannot express kinematic loops.

\subsection{Digital twin validation and plant identification}
\label{sec:plant-id}
The digital twin is validated against the real robot for the kinematic and dynamic properties.
The model is also refined with measured parameters that cant be accurately estimated from the CAD model, to reduce the sim2real gap.

\subsubsection{Joint Limits validation}
\label{sec:sim-hygiene}

The cam and thigh joints close a passive 4-bar loop through the pushrod and knee. Their safe ranges
do not compose into two independent intervals: a per-joint minimum and maximum would allow poses
the mechanism cannot assemble. I derive the safe envelope directly in actuator space instead. I
hold each leg's three drives current-free and backdrive it by hand through its full travel, logging
the raw actuator angles \cite{morand2026runningrobot}.

\Cref{fig:joint-limits-actuator} shows the result for both legs: a plain interval for abduction, and
a two-dimensional band for the coupled (cam, thigh) pair. The demonstrated abduction range spans
42.8\,deg to 126.1\,deg on the left leg and 66.5\,deg to 147.7\,deg on the right; I erode both by
3\,deg for a safety margin and store the eroded interval as the abduction limit. For the knee pair I
rasterise the recorded (cam, thigh) samples onto a 1\,deg grid, dilate it to close small sampling
gaps, then erode it by the same 3\,deg margin. The stored safe region covers 11.0\,\% of its own
bounding box on the left leg and 9.4\,\% on the right, confirming the band is far from rectangular.
The onboard safety layer checks every commanded pose against this grid before it reaches the drives
(\cref{sec:safety}).

\subsubsection{Inertia matrix validation}
\label{sec:inertia-validation}

I identify each actuated joint's torque constant $K_t$, effective inertia and friction (viscous and
Coulomb) from two excitation profiles logged through the web interface \cite{morand2026runningrobot}.
A quasi-static sweep at 0.03\,Hz holds the joint near equilibrium, so gravity dominates the measured
torque. A faster chirp is meant to excite the inertial term instead. Both profiles drive the joint
in servo mode and log the drive's own current as a proxy for torque. A single per-joint
least-squares fit on the pooled data recovers $K_t$, the effective inertia and the friction terms
together. The fit uses the model's gravity torque, computed through the closed 4-bar loop
(\cref{sec:twin}).

A raw torque residual is hard to judge on its own, so I convert it into a mass error. Gravity torque
is linear in the body masses: $\tau = -\sum_b m_b (J_b^\top g)$, where $m_b$ is each body's mass and
$J_b$ its centre-of-mass Jacobian. A uniform fractional error $\varepsilon$ in every mass therefore
scales every joint's gravity torque by exactly $\varepsilon$. I divide the RMS residual by the RMS
gravity torque to get the uniform mass error that would produce a torque error of the same size. It
is an upper bound, since part of the residual is the stick-slip ripple, not a mass error.

\Cref{fig:inertia-validation} shows the slow sweep: predicted torque against measured torque, with
the residual shaded and the mass-equivalent error labelled per joint. The sagittal joints correspond
to a 29\,\% to 62\,\% mass error, and abduction to 43\,\% and 204\,\%. In my view none of these
validate the mass model to a useful tolerance. Even the best joint, right thigh at 29\,\%, is an
order of magnitude looser than the few-percent accuracy a mass-matrix validation would want. The
model tracks the gravity trend's shape and sign, not its magnitude. The worst case, right abduction
at 204\,\%, is consistent with an unverified assumption: the cam and thigh angles use a fitted zero
from the closed-loop kinematics, while abduction assumes its calibrated zero equals the model's zero
pose, an equality that is never checked against data.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.9\linewidth]{Figures/inertiaValidationGravity.png}
\caption{Gravity-model validation on the slow (0.03\,Hz) quasi-static sweep: measured torque
($K_t$ times current) against the model's predicted torque (gravity plus friction), residual shaded,
per joint. The mass-equivalent error is the RMS residual divided by the RMS gravity torque. The fast
ripple is the P-only position loop's stick-slip response under Coulomb friction, not sensor noise.}
\label{fig:inertia-validation}
\end{figure}

The chirp excitation does not fully validate the inertia term. The drive's servo-mode position loop
has a measured closed-loop bandwidth of 1.95\,Hz ($-3$\,dB, \cref{sec:plant-id-drive}), and the
dynamic run's chirp reached 2\,Hz, right at that ceiling. The top of the sweep is attenuated by
roughly half, so the joint falls short of the commanded acceleration there. The regression reflects
the shortfall: the fitted armature converges to exactly zero for three of the six joints, left
abduction, left thigh, right thigh. I also score the fit by predicting torque on data withheld from
the regression, normalized by the measured torque's own RMS. That held-out normalized RMS is
63\,\% pooled across all six joints, against the 20\,\% target I use to accept a fit. Neither the
gravity model nor the inertia term clears that bar. I therefore treat the identified parameters as
a rough, order-of-magnitude plant estimate rather than a validated one. MIT mode's velocity loop
reaches five to eight times the bandwidth of servo mode (\cref{sec:plant-id-drive}); a full
inertia-matrix identification should excite through it instead.

\subsubsection{Actuator bandwidth characterisation}
\label{sec:plant-id-drive}

I measure the AKE90-8's closed-loop bandwidth in both control interfaces the drive exposes: servo
mode's position command, and MIT mode's velocity command \cite{morand2026runningrobot}. The two
loops are not interchangeable, so I measure each through the interface it is actually used with.

For servo mode I command a position chirp to the loaded left thigh through \texttt{SET\_POS}:
0.05\,Hz to 11.5\,Hz, at 13.9\,deg amplitude. I estimate the transfer function $H(f) =
P_{xy}/P_{xx}$ by Welch's method. I keep only frequencies where the coherence exceeds 0.7. For MIT
mode I command a velocity sine at ten frequencies up to 30\,Hz, with the position loop off ($k_p =
0$) and no leg attached. I sweep three derivative gains, $k_d \in \{0.4, 1.0, 2.5\}$, and read gain
and phase from the recorded response.

\Cref{fig:actuator-bode} shows both. Servo mode reaches $-3$\,dB at 1.95\,Hz, with an 18\,ms delay
fitted from the phase slope above 4\,Hz. MIT mode reaches $-3$\,dB between 9.0\,Hz and 15.1\,Hz
depending on $k_d$, five to eight times higher. The comparison is not perfectly matched. Servo mode
is measured on the loaded leg tracking position; MIT mode, on a free rotor tracking velocity. Each
is measured through the interface it is actually commanded with on the robot. MIT phase is
referenced to its 1\,Hz point, which shifts every reported phase by under 9\,deg. I report that as
a limitation of the reference, rather than correcting for it. Cam and thigh (nodes 105 and 106)
agree within 1.3\,dB and 3\,deg everywhere I checked both, so one drive stands for the pair.

\subsection{Deployment stack}
\label{sec:deploy}

The policies of \cref{sec:rl} have to run somewhere. To make streamline the process and simplify the measurement of the plant parameters, a web interface was built on the robot.
A systemd entry as added at boot to start the web interface. The raspberry pi was configured to act as a wifi access point (SSID : "runningmachine", pwd:"runningmachine")
Once connected to the network, the web interface can be accessed at ("http://192.168.4.1:8080/")

\subsubsection{Calibration sequence}
\label{sec:calibration}
The actuator dont hold their position when powered off, so a calibration sequence is needed to zero the joints.
The user need to hold the robot in a known configuration and run the calibration sequence which register the current joint position as zero.
The user then need to move each joint in a specific direction and record that the angle in increase (otherwise, the joint direction need to be inverted by clicking a button in the web interface).
Once the robot is zeroed, the workspace can be registered by backdriving the robot and recording the joint position at the limit of the workspace.
The web UI then clean the measurement to show the boudany and the user can draw / fill the safe workspace in the web interface.
This is used to limit the joint position in the safety layer.

the IMU is also calibrated in the web interface by holding the robot still and recording the bias of the accelerometer and gyroscope.
To ensure the axis are aligned, the user need to move the robot around an given axis and check that the correct axis is increasing.

\subsubsection{The safety layer}
\label{sec:safety}

A safety layer was implemented to prevent the robot from exiting the safe operating envelope.
It limits the joint position, the joint speed, the joint torque. If a tracking error greater than a threshold is detected, the robot is stopped.
The user can also at any time stop the robot by clicking the E-STOP button in the web interface.
This safety layer was toroughly tested in simulation and on the real robot by trying to make the robot exit the safe operating envelope and was proven useful in a few occasion to prevent user error.
The safety layer also implement a black box recorder that record the joint position, speed, torque and the IMU data at 200 Hz if a new failure mode is triggerd, it will be possible to investigate it.
