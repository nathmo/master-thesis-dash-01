\section{The robot and its digital twin}
\label{sec:robot}

This thesis needed to first finish the Dash-01 robot.
This implied to choose the computer, its actuator and the communication bus.
It also needed a battery.

Once that was done, its digital twin was develop from the CAD data and validated against measured value from the assembled robot.
Finally the model what could not be derived from the cad was measured on the real robot (like actuator bandwidth) to limit the sim2real risk.

Six drives on a 48\,V bus with short violent current peaks is a sprint duty cycle. The pack is
therefore optimised for power density. The choice was again made against a purpose-built database,
of roughly 430 cell models, 54 BMS modules and 110 commercial packs (\cref{sec:app-db}). Its
central convention is that \emph{current is a curve, not a number}. Every rating carries a duration
stamp, and falls into a continuous, pulse-at-$t$ or fault tier.

The pack built is 13S2P of EVE INR21700-50PL: 48.1\,V nominal, 10\,Ah, 468\,Wh and 1.87\,kg of
cells, behind a 200\,A BMS with a 250\,A software cap. The database corrects the specification it
was sized against. The 50PL is a 5.0\,Ah cell at 50\,A continuous and 180\,A for 2\,s. The pack was
sized on a 125\,A marketed peak with no published duration. That is exactly the class of number the
tier convention exists to reject. The real ratings are 100\,A continuous (4.8\,kW), 184\,A for
10\,s and 360\,A for 2\,s. The continuous tier is \emph{cell}-limited rather than BMS-limited.

The 250\,A software cap is therefore a 2-second-tier number, enforced with no duration logic. A
policy drawing 250\,A for thirty seconds runs the cells at 25\,C against a 10\,C continuous rating,
and nothing notices. The fix is an $I^2t$ accumulator in the control daemon rather than a scalar
clamp.

Two survey findings change the verdict rather than decorate it. \textbf{Nothing in the commercial
48\,V market is built for a sprint duty cycle.} Of 110 packs surveyed, the space holds stationary
storage and e-bike packs, and the closest match is rated in the 40\,A class. That is roughly
2.5$\times$ short, and it is what justifies building a pack at all. Second, on the project's own
criterion the built pack was the wrong pick. High-rate LiPo was rejected as unsourceable, and a
Swiss domestic supplier stocks 1S high-rate cells. A 13S1P build of those delivers 481\,Wh at
250\,A continuous and 500\,A for 10\,s. The built pack delivers 468\,Wh at 100 and 184\,A, and the
alternative costs 0.67\,kg and EUR\,463 more. Cycle life, pouch-cell assembly and laboratory
storage restrictions are honest defences of the built pack, and none of them was the recorded
reason.

\paragraph{Voltage sag is the largest unmeasured quantity on the robot.} The drives publish
position, speed, current, temperature and an error flag, and \textbf{no bus voltage}
(\cref{sec:app-can}). Pack sag under a sprint is therefore not observable from the drive side. A
13S2P arrangement multiplies cell resistance by 6.5, and a high-drain 21700 typically sits at 12 to
15\,m$\Omega$. That would put the pack near 80 to 100\,m$\Omega$, and imply 15 to 18\,V of sag at
the ten-second tier. Those figures are an inference from a typical cell rather than a measurement.
The reason to record them is that \cref{sec:speed-ceiling} establishes no-load speed as the binding
axis, and no-load speed is linear in bus voltage. A voltmeter across the pack during a sprint is
the cheapest outstanding experiment in the project.

\paragraph{Compute and sensing.} \label{sec:compute-choice} The board is a \textbf{Raspberry Pi 3
Model B}, running stock non-realtime Linux. It was chosen over the laboratory's usual BeagleBone
Black for fluency rather than for merit. An \texttt{mcp251xfd} controller over SPI provides CAN as
two independent 1\,Mbit buses, one per leg. The Pi also hosts a WiFi access point and the web
interface, through which most measurements in \cref{sec:plant-id} were taken.
\Cref{sec:plant-id-timing} settles by measurement that this board holds a 200\,Hz control loop at
half its time budget.

Beyond the motor encoders, the only proprioceptive sensors are the base inertial measurement unit
and the motor current estimates. Contact sensors were considered and never fitted
(\cref{sec:intro-reframe}). The unit is an \textbf{ICM-20948} on a Waveshare Sense HAT\,(B), read
over I2C at $\pm4$\,g and $\pm1000$\,dps, with a 50\,Hz internal low-pass and 200\,Hz sampling.
Measured at rest it gives 1.8\,mg and 0.08\,dps RMS noise. The noise density is
218\,$\mu$g/$\sqrt{\text{Hz}}$ and 0.0096\,dps/$\sqrt{\text{Hz}}$. The gyro turn-on bias is
repeatable at $[1.03, 0.11, -0.53]$\,dps, and the in-run bias stability is 0.010--0.012\,dps.
Quantisation sits about 15$\times$ below the noise floor on both channels, so widening the ranges
to survive footfall impacts costs nothing in effective resolution.

Two properties matter more for control than the noise does. \textbf{Latency} from physical event to
published value is 10 to 20\,ms. It comprises low-pass group delay of 5 to 10\,ms, output-rate
quantisation, the I2C read and the poll period. Against a 5\,ms control period that is two to four
whole control steps, and the simulation models it as such. And \textbf{the attitude filter
dominates}. A 6-axis Madgwick filter at $\beta = 0.06$\,rad/s rate-limits its accelerometer
correction to about $3.4^{\circ}$/s. Published attitude is therefore overwhelmingly gyro-driven,
and the accelerometer acts as a slow trim. Yaw is pure integration and drifts, so the gyro zero is
captured on a stationary robot each session. The magnetometer is not fused, because it sits
centimetres from two brushless motors and its reading moves when they do.

One methodological trap is worth recording. Noise figures taken while the robot swayed on its test
rig measure the sway, and nothing in the number says so. That produced a yaw-axis figure roughly
$8\times$ too high. It also produced a filter sweep in which a narrower low-pass appeared to
measure more noise. Both looked like plausible sensor data. Every published figure now carries a
provenance label: measured at rest, motion-independent, datasheet-unverified, or uncharacterised.
The uncharacterised list is published rather than guessed at. It holds gyro bias temperature
dependence, vibration rectification under footfall impact, cross-axis sensitivity, and clipping at
$\pm4$\,g.

\subsection{The digital twin}
\label{sec:twin}

The cam actuator drives a pushrod whose distal end ties to the shin below the knee. Hip, cam,
pushrod, shin and thigh therefore close a planar 4-bar (\cref{fig:robot}). URDF is a tree format
and cannot express that closure. An exported URDF describes a different robot, one with a floating
pushrod, and any simulator consuming it directly produces dynamics that are plausible and wrong.
MuJoCo's MJCF closes the loop with an explicit \texttt{<equality><connect>} constraint. Both files
are exported and kept, and only the MJCF is simulated.

The consequence for control is that cam and thigh are \emph{not} independent. The loop assembles
over only a diagonal band of the nominal joint box. A target commanded outside that band is
physically impossible rather than merely hard to track.

\Cref{sec:app-pipeline} writes the pipeline as a procedure. Two detours are worth recording.
Inertia matrices supplied from a second CAD system arrived in the wrong orientation, and that
export never yielded a usable file. The pipeline therefore validates every CAD-supplied inertia
tensor against its reference frame explicitly. The second detour is asymmetry. The exported model
is not left/right symmetric, and an audit found 17 of 30 mirror-compared quantities off, including
a $1.2^{\circ}$ rotation in one hip frame. A tool that mirrors the model exactly was built, and
deliberately \emph{not} applied. The crooked stance survives symmetrisation, because the gravity
settle bifurcates into a lean of about $7^{\circ}$ even from a symmetric start. The asymmetry chose
only which way. The deployment lineage instead randomises the asymmetry
(\cref{sec:sim2real-lineage}), because the real robot is asymmetric too.

\paragraph{Validating the twin.} Validation is a chain rather than a claim. The constrained toe
Jacobian matches \texttt{mj\_jacGeom} to better than $10^{-10}$, and self-tests re-check it on
every run. The MJCF closes the 4-bar at 100\,\% of the poses backdriven on the real robot. That is
1383 of 1383 on the left and 1406 of 1406 on the right, at a residual of $10^{-14}$. The test is
one-sided, since it would catch only a model band \emph{smaller} than reality. Static equilibrium
predictions agree with MuJoCo within 1\,\%, and the known-mass experiment of
\cref{sec:plant-id-torque} ties the model's gravity torque to a measured current. Every segment was
weighed rather than fitted (\cref{tab:motors}), which removes those parameters from the
identification problem and leaves only the inertia tensors free.

The residual gap is bounded. The CAD centres of mass survive scrutiny
(\cref{sec:plant-id-sysid}), while the link inertia \emph{tensors} remain CAD placeholders scaled
by measured mass ratios. That is why the domain-randomisation range on inertia stays at
$\pm25\,\%$ while the mass ranges narrowed.

\subsubsection{Establishing that a simulation result is real}
\label{sec:sim-hygiene}

Physics runs in MuJoCo~\cite{todorov2012mujoco} on the CPU, chosen for contact modelling and for
not requiring recent GPU hardware. Training nonetheless runs 1.9$\times$ faster with the policy
network on a GPU, at about 2900 environment-steps per second against 1530 on the same cluster node.
Network size is not the cause, since the two-layer MLP costs 117\,$\mu$s even on the robot's
Raspberry Pi. The cause is core contention, because PyTorch on the CPU steals cores from the
parallel MuJoCo workers. Training ran on the EPFL Izar cluster, which removed throughput as a
bottleneck.

A simulation bug does not usually announce itself. It produces a clean, converged, entirely
plausible learning curve for a robot that does not exist. Four examples were found while setting up
a single ankle study, and each would have silently produced a wrong answer.

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=1pt]
  \item \textbf{Joint limits are not rigid by default.} MuJoCo's default limit softness let body
        weight push $3.4^{\circ}$ past the stop of a supposedly rigid strut. Stiffening the
        constraint reduced the penetration to $0.08^{\circ}$.
  \item \textbf{A constraint that ships inactive is inactive.} The rigid arm re-settled its stance
        with a free ankle, because the locking equalities switch on only at episode reset. It
        initialised 268\,mm lower than intended.
  \item \textbf{A safety criterion measured in the wrong frame.} One arm stood outside its own
        threshold from its reset pose, and every episode terminated 0.1\,s in.
  \item \textbf{A stale keyframe.} The shipped stance is an equilibrium of one particular ankle
        law, so arms with a softer ankle lurched at every reset.
\end{itemize}

A fifth class is environmental. A weld constraint that held on MuJoCo 3.7.0 locally gave way on the
cluster's 3.3.7, drifting 0.86\,rad over a 60-step rollout. The ``rigid'' arm was silently
compliant on the cluster alone.

The countermeasures are structural. A smoke-test suite builds and briefly runs all 123 training
presets before any long launch. Self-tests inside the analysis tools check the analytic Jacobians
and the loop closure. Assertions fail loudly when a plant does not match what its preset claims it
is. \Cref{sec:negative} catalogues the two further artefacts that got through anyway.

\paragraph{Mass, and how a correction should be applied.} The weighed masses were baked into the
model. The inertia tensors were scaled by the per-body mass ratio, and the stance keyframe was
re-settled under the new load. The decision worth recording is that the correction was \emph{not}
made an experimental variable. An earlier arrangement kept a CAD plant and a measured plant as two
selectable model files. Every preset that had not explicitly opted in therefore kept training on
the 12.83\,kg robot, and that was all of them but two. Splitting the plant in two had made the
wrong one the default. There is one robot, it weighs 15.14\,kg, and the model file says so. The
accepted cost is that warm-starting from a pre-correction checkpoint is now a cross-plant transfer.

\subsubsection{Peak torque is what the gearbox delivers, not what the datasheet quotes}
\label{sec:plant-id-torque}

$K_t$ was measured by clamping a known 1.552\,kg mass to the shin. A quasi-static sweep was then
repeated with the mass and without it. Fitting the two runs \emph{together} with an indicator
column makes the mass term's coefficient exactly $1/K_t$. A known mass on a known kinematic lever
arm involves no link centre of mass anywhere. That breaks a degeneracy which had defeated the
identification until then, since $K_t$ and the gravity-model gain are otherwise confounded and only
their product is observable.

The effective joint $K_t$ came out at 82 to 89\,\% of datasheet, consistent with a gearbox at
roughly 85\,\% efficiency. Peak torque scales with $K_t$ at the same peak current. The cam and
thigh actuators therefore moved from 170 to 144.5\,N$\cdot$m, and hip roll from 72 to
61.2\,N$\cdot$m. The second figure is the same efficiency \emph{transferred} rather than measured,
because no datasheet $K_t$ is published for that part. It is probably optimistic, because a 39:1
reduction needs more gear stages than an 8:1.

The remaining uncertainty is geometric. The mass was clamped by hand, and $K_t$ trades against its
position at about 1.9\,\% per centimetre. The perpendicular standoff is known only to 5 to 10\,cm,
and across that box $K_t$ spans 82 to 98\,\% of datasheet. Settling the figure needs the mass on
the shin centreline at a measured distance, not more analysis of the existing data.

\subsubsection{The drive's position loop is 0.8\,Hz}
\label{sec:plant-id-drive}

The drive's internal position loop is the single largest modelling error found in the project. It
stayed invisible until a swept sine was run on the assembled robot.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.86\textwidth]{Figures/fig_drive.pdf}
\caption{Measured closed-loop response of the drive's internal position loop, identical on both
legs to two decimals, against a single-pole model. A gain of 0.71 at $-48^{\circ}$ is a first-order
corner, so one pole at 0.8\,Hz plus about 25\,ms of transport delay fits the whole sweep. The
shaded band is the gait-frequency range the policy can select. At the top of that band the hardware
executes under a third of the commanded amplitude, more than $90^{\circ}$ late.}
\label{fig:drive}
\end{figure}

The simulation had modelled these actuators as position servos with $k_p = 200$ and $k_v = 5$,
critically damped at roughly 13\,Hz. The real drive rolls off at 0.8\,Hz. The bandwidth mismatch is
more than an order of magnitude, against a learnable gait frequency whose top is 4\,Hz. At 2.5\,Hz
the hardware executes 30\,\% of the commanded amplitude, $94^{\circ}$ late. \textbf{Every policy in
\cref{tab:rl-results} assumes authority the hardware cannot deliver.}

Three consequences follow. First, the unexecuted part of a command \emph{is} the tracking error,
and it tends to the full commanded amplitude as frequency rises. Any high-frequency content
therefore caps usable excitation amplitude near $15^{\circ}$, whatever the workspace allows. That
cap is why an early identification sweep tripped the safety limit six seconds in. Second,
\textbf{inertia cannot be excited in position mode at all}, so the quasi-static runs are the
valuable data and always were.

Third, the fix is architectural rather than a gain change. The cascaded position loop is
proportional-only, and nothing in the runtime can write its gains. The measurement points at
current-mode commands, with the position PD closed on the Pi at 200\,Hz. It also points at a
torque-source actuator in simulation, carrying a real torque--speed envelope. \Cref{sec:rl-torque}
records why that partially reverses a decision taken at the start of the project.

An existing policy cannot be adapted by switching the drive model on. Applying 0.8\,Hz in one step
to a converged walking policy collapsed it, with nothing else changed. Episode length fell from
1448 to 251, and return from $+511$ to $-78$. The drive model is therefore ramped in as a
curriculum, logarithmically in frequency, because the equivalent filter coefficient is strongly
nonlinear in bandwidth.

\subsubsection{System identification, including what it could not resolve}
\label{sec:plant-id-sysid}

Inverse dynamics through the closed loop was rejected. The 4-bar is near-singular over part of the
operating region, and the resulting inverse blows up. Identification is instead a per-joint linear
regression of measured current against four terms: a gravity-torque model, viscous and Coulomb
friction, an inertia term and an intercept. A constrained Jacobian differentiates the loop closure
out.

Four estimator bugs each produced a confident wrong answer. A missing sign convention on the
current column made every $K_t$ negative. An unmirrored joint mapping on the right leg left a loop
residual of 0.78\,m instead of $10^{-8}$, reproducibly across three recordings. Pooling rows from
joints a given excitation did not move flipped the sign of $K_t$, and so did omitting the
intercept. Both flip it because the drive's DC current offset then lands in the gravity column. And
over-regularising the loop Jacobian inflated the fitted gravity gain by 20\,\%. With those fixed,
the two legs agree to 5 or 6\,\% rather than disagreeing in sign. That cross-validation is what
says the fixes are right.

The result is two things rather than one. The first is a measured, repeatable, per-joint gravity
gain. The second is an explicit statement of what the experiment cannot resolve. With $K_t$ pinned
by the known-mass test, the model still under-predicts gravity torque by a factor of 1.3 to 1.75.
The obvious explanation is wrong CAD centres of mass, and it was tested and \textbf{rejected}.
Gravity torque is linear in each body's centre of mass, so the test is a single linear solve. With
all 18 parameters free, no regularisation and no plausibility constraint, moving mass explains at
most 11 to 19\,\% of the residual. Ten of the eighteen directions are pure null space, which is
where physically absurd fitted offsets come from. Per-body mass, load-dependent stiction and base
tilt were rejected on the same grounds.

One test rules out friction of any origin. Friction reverses sign with the direction of travel.
Fitting the gravity gain separately on up-strokes and down-strokes puts both on the same side of
unity, for all six joints. What remains lies outside the span of every rigid-body inertial
parameter. That points at the kinematic model rather than at the mass distribution, and it is
unresolved. Knowing which parameters the experiment could not resolve is what stops the next person
fitting the same null space again.

Three results follow, and all three contradict earlier assumptions. \textbf{The drives broadcast
their state at exactly 200.0\,Hz, unprompted.} All six do so, on both buses, free-running with the
control software stopped. The sensing ceiling is therefore exactly 200\,Hz, and a 50\,Hz loop would
discard three of every four sensor frames.

\textbf{200\,Hz is clean, and 50\,Hz is reproducibly worse.} The 50\,Hz row is a scheduling
artefact of the longer sleep rather than frame loss. The case for dropping to 50\,Hz had been
compute headroom, and compute is not the constraint. Policy inference costs 117\,$\mu$s, or
2.3\,\% of a 5\,ms budget. \textbf{The CAN bus was never near its limit}, at about 26\,\% of one
1\,Mbit bus spread across two.

None of this helps with the real bottleneck. Running the outer loop at 200\,Hz cannot compensate
for an actuator that executes a quarter of the command $94^{\circ}$ late. What the measurement
establishes is that the Pi holds ample budget to close the position loop itself, which is the
proposed fix. \Cref{sec:app-can} records the protocol as sniffed rather than as documented.

\subsection{Capability: the speed ceiling before any controller}
\label{sec:speed-ceiling}

Reinforcement learning demonstrates a lower bound on performance. This section supplies the upper
bound that the kinematics, the mass distribution and the actuators impose. Learned results are then
judged against physics rather than against hope.

\paragraph{Method.} The analysis runs directly on the MuJoCo model, which keeps it consistent with
the model used for training. Candidate gaits are parameterised by duty factor $D$, contact length
$L_c$, ride height $h$ and cadence. For each candidate, a simplified running model gives stride
length, flight time, swing duration and ground reaction forces. The leg Jacobian then gives joint
torques and velocities through the cycle. A gait counts as feasible only if every joint stays
inside the admissible 4-bar assembly region, while respecting the actuator torque, speed and power
limits. The parameter space was swept exhaustively. Validation against MuJoCo agrees within 1\,\%
on static equilibrium, and the analytic Jacobian matches \texttt{mj\_jacGeom} to better than
$10^{-10}$.

\paragraph{What limits the result.} Swing-leg actuator power limits maximum speed, not kinematic
reach. The dominant constraint is the cam actuator during swing. It demands about 878\,W, namely
64\,N$\cdot$m at 13.7\,rad/s, against a theoretical 935\,W. 87\,\% of feasible gaits are limited at
that peak-power corner. Raising actuator no-load speed would therefore improve achievable speed
more than raising peak torque would. Stance loading is the second constraint: peak ground reaction
forces demand about 167\,N$\cdot$m of the available 170.

Kinematic reach does not limit the result, but it is not free either. The leg spans a theoretical
fore--aft workspace of 1.06\,m, of which only 0.20 to 0.35\,m is usable. Near the edge of the
assembly region the 4-bar's transmission ratio deteriorates, and joint velocities rise. A flight
phase helps substantially. Reducing duty factor from 0.85 to 0.45 raises predicted speed from 4.6
to 6.9\,m/s, for a 1.2\,cm vertical excursion of the centre of mass. Lower duty factors demand
ground reaction forces above 4.5 body weights, and are infeasible.

Two independent agreements are the checks on this analysis. The unconstrained optimum falls at
12.4\,steps/s, which is the same high-cadence solution reinforcement learning discovered repeatedly
(\cref{sec:rl-cadence}). That comes from physics, with no learning in it. And a static load
analysis reproduces the ankle-stiffness threshold the learning experiments had already shown. The
passive spring at 28.65\,N$\cdot$m/rad supports only about half a body weight before collapsing
under positive mechanical feedback. About 350\,N$\cdot$m/rad holds two body weights in a stable
stance.

\paragraph{The corrected ceiling reclassifies the platform.} \label{sec:speed-ceiling-corrected}
Peak torque falls from 170 to 144.5\,N$\cdot$m, which is a direct hit on the binding constraint.
Distal leg mass rises by roughly 0.70\,kg across both legs, at $-0.93$\,(m/s)/kg. Propagating both
gives roughly \textbf{2.7\,m/s credible burst and 1.3\,m/s thermally sustained}, against 3.7 and
1.8 on the CAD plant. The learned policy's 2.6\,m/s therefore no longer sits comfortably inside the
envelope. It sits essentially \emph{at} the burst ceiling, and at twice the sustained figure. A
100\,m dash at record pace is about 25\,s of continuous running against a continuous torque rating,
so the sustained number decides whether a record is reachable. This chassis is a walking and
jogging demonstrator.

\paragraph{Limitations, and one correction that runs the other way.} The analysis is an optimistic
upper bound. It neglects torso attitude stabilisation, lateral dynamics, impact losses and control
bandwidth. The last is no longer small, because \cref{sec:plant-id-drive} shows the assumption of
an executed trajectory fails well below the cadences assumed here. Inertia tensor \emph{shapes} are
still CAD, and \cref{tab:speed-ceiling} shows the result is most sensitive to exactly that
quantity. The corrected figures propagate sensitivity coefficients rather than re-running the
sweep. The sweep should be repeated on the corrected plant, with the tooling that now exists in
validated form.

One flagged inconsistency runs the optimistic way. The sweep caps the cam at
$P \approx \tau_0\omega_0/4 \approx 935$\,W. That is the envelope of a voltage-limited brushed
motor, not of a current-limited field-oriented drive. With $R$ known, back-EMF plus $IR$ exhausts
the 48\,V bus at 16.6\,rad/s at the joint. The true envelope is therefore flat at
$\tau_{\text{pk}}$ up to that corner, and peaks near 2.8\,kW against a vendor-annotated 1500\,W.
The burst figures may be pessimistic by a factor between 1.6 and 3. Two cautions keep that from
being good news. The 55\,N$\cdot$m continuous rating sets the sustained figure and does not move.
And the 0.8\,Hz drive bandwidth caps achievable cadence far below any of these power limits.

\subsubsection{Static end-effector force map}
\label{sec:hw-force-map}

The cam actuator reaches the knee through the pushrod 4-bar. The transmission from motor torque to
toe force is therefore a strong function of posture, and whether the strong direction is the
direction running needs had never been checked. The constrained toe Jacobian differentiates the
loop closure out, so it carries the transmission ratio. Statics against the actuator torque box
then gives the feasible force set at every reachable toe position. Three findings follow. All three
are statics-only, so they bound capability rather than predict behaviour.

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=1pt]
  \item \textbf{The leg stands at 96\,\% of its reach}, with about 3\,mm of further extension
        available anywhere near the stance position. Everything else follows from sitting that
        close to the leg-extension singularity.
  \item \textbf{Force there is 19:1 anisotropic}: 16.4 body weights straight down against 0.85
        horizontally. Over the running stance region the median tilted capacity recovers to
        5.95\,BW, because compressed postures rotate their strong axis.
  \item \textbf{The motors are not the binding constraint. The ankle is, by a factor of eight.}
        Over the stance region the actuators give a median vertical capacity of 4.30\,BW, and
        77\,\% of the region clears the 3.5\,BW running requirement. The passive ankle gives a
        median of 0.42\,BW, and \textbf{0.0\,\%} of the region clears it.
\end{itemize}

That last point is a third independent route to the conclusion of \cref{sec:ankle-build}. It is
reached from statics, with no learning and no dynamics in it. The linkage and the motor sizing are
adequate to generous. The passive foot spring is the design defect.

\subsection{Deployment stack}
\label{sec:deploy}

The policies of \cref{sec:rl} ultimately have to run on the Raspberry Pi, against real drives, with
no simulator to reset. Measurement settled three questions about that, and an incident settled a
fourth.

\paragraph{Linux without a real-time kernel suffices.} \label{sec:deploy-linux}
\Cref{tab:loop-timing} establishes that a 200\,Hz loop holds at half budget with sub-millisecond
jitter. One Python-specific detail is load-bearing. CPython hands the interpreter lock between
threads at most every \texttt{switchinterval} seconds, and the 5\,ms default is exactly one control
period. The web-interface thread could therefore legally hold the interpreter across a whole tick.
Dropping the interval to 0.5\,ms took late ticks from roughly 10\,\% to a few percent.

The steady state was then measured with everything running: the control daemon, the 200\,Hz sensor
thread, the web interface and the flight recorder. It is \textbf{188\,Hz against a 200\,Hz target,
at 3\,\% late ticks}. An A/B under \texttt{systemd} overrides shows where the time goes. The full
stack runs at 186.7\,Hz, disabling the recorder gives 186.3\,Hz, and disabling the sensor thread as
well gives 194.8\,Hz. The recorder is therefore free, and the sensor thread costs 0.23\,ms per
tick. The missing 6\,\% is operating-system scheduling.

\paragraph{A methodological trap, again.} The first loop-rate figures taken during bring-up read
60\,Hz with 34\,\% late ticks. They produced a confident, committed diagnosis of an expensive
failure path in the CAN layer, and the A/B above shows the suspected path costs nothing. The
60\,Hz was the measurement disturbing the measured. While the Pi was being sampled, \texttt{scp},
remote compilation, \texttt{journalctl} and HTTP polling were all running on its four cores. That
is the same failure mode as the sensor noise figures taken on a swaying rig. The number was real,
and the attribution was not.

\paragraph{The drive as deployed.} Three measured properties dominate the design. The only writable
surface is the servo-mode command pair \texttt{SET\_POS} and \texttt{SET\_CURRENT}, since the
internal cascaded gains are board configuration. The drives report no bus voltage. And \textbf{the
multi-turn position origin is not stable}. It re-randomises on every power cycle, and the incident
below is consistent with it moving between power cycles as well. Every absolute position command is
therefore only as good as a calibration captured earlier in the same session. Nothing in the drive
signals when that calibration has silently become wrong.

\paragraph{CAN at runtime.} Bandwidth is a non-issue, but running under \texttt{systemd} made one
failure mode routine. The daemon now starts before motor power is on. With nothing on the bus to
acknowledge a frame, socketcan's ten-frame transmit queue fills within one tick, and every
subsequent \texttt{send()} raises \texttt{ENOBUFS}. On the first boot under \texttt{systemd} that
exception went unhandled in a background thread, and killed the control daemon during its own
preflight. The fix treats a send failure as an event rather than a death. Sends never raise past
the CAN layer, and a channel failing ten consecutive sends is retried at 5\,Hz until one succeeds.
A failing send costs nothing, at 188.9\,Hz with the backoff disabled against 188.5\,Hz with it
enabled, so the backoff is kept for forensics rather than for throughput.

\subsubsection{The safety envelope as an instrument}
\label{sec:safety}

A browser-based interface on the robot is the only path through which the hardware is commanded by
hand, and the envelope it enforces is also an experimental instrument. Joint limits are
\emph{measured} rather than taken from CAD, by backdriving each joint through its full travel with
the motors limp. The 4-bar's assemblable band is a constraint on a joint \emph{pair}. The recorded
region is therefore a box in the foot's fore--aft and lift coordinates, rather than a set of
independent joint ranges. That same box is reused in simulation as a training termination
criterion, which makes an entire class of simulator-only exploit impossible rather than merely
expensive. It is expressed in the base frame, so by itself it cannot distinguish a collapsed ankle
from a folded linkage (\cref{sec:sim-hygiene}).

Playback is capped by kinematics rather than by preference. A part failed during a high-cadence
playback, and the ceiling was cut from 5 to 2.5\,Hz on a derived number. Differentiating the stored
waveform gives peak joint speed per unit cadence, which is a property of the trajectory shape. The
cam demands 359\,deg/s per hertz, and reaches no-load speed at 3.5\,Hz. At the 5\,Hz previously
allowed it was commanded at 143\,\% of no-load speed; at 2.5\,Hz it sits at 71\,\%. Trip conditions
on tracking error, joint range and temperature latch the drives to zero current. The
tracking-error trip had to be decoupled from the commanded trajectory, once
\cref{sec:plant-id-drive} showed that a large tracking error at speed is expected behaviour rather
than a fault.

\subsubsection{The incident, the flight recorder and the pre-move guard}
\label{sec:deploy-blackbox}

On 2026-08-10 a joint destroyed itself on the bench. A zero pose had been captured, and by the time
an absolute move was commanded the drives' raw origin no longer matched it. The drive faithfully
executed a position command in the wrong frame. It wound \texttt{left.cam} through roughly 1.9
output turns, on a joint with $\pm86^{\circ}$ of travel. Investigating it was impossible. The only
telemetry was a 4096-sample RAM ring at 20\,Hz, and nothing was written to disk. That the
investigation was impossible was itself the finding.

\textbf{A three-tier flight recorder} was built in response. Six motors at 200\,Hz produce
35\,kB/s, or 3\,GB/day, which fills an SD card and then destroys it. The recorder therefore splits
by rate. Tier A writes every field continuously at 20\,Hz, into rotated fixed-width binary
segments. Tier B is the black box proper: a 40\,s RAM ring of the full 200\,Hz state, dumped on any
trigger with the pre-trigger history included and a 3\,s tail. Tier C is one \texttt{fsync}'d JSON
line per event, append-only and never deleted. Triggers include every safety trip, mode transition,
calibration write, warn-level tracking error below the trip threshold, and any encoder-origin jump.
Each dump embeds a content-hashed snapshot of the calibration and drive gains live at that instant,
so a recording is self-describing.

Two constraints shaped it. The control loop must not pay for its own instrumentation, so the
200\,Hz thread only appends a tuple to a bounded queue, at about 2\,$\mu$s. A full queue
\emph{drops and counts}, stamping the running drop count into the next accepted record. And the
robot must never depend on the recorder, so if the writer thread dies the control loop continues.
Time is handled by inversion, because the Pi has no real-time clock. A heartbeat is
\texttt{fsync}'d every 8\,s, so the last heartbeat before a gap \emph{is} the power-off time.

\textbf{The pre-move guard} closes the incident's mechanism, a full-authority slew against a stale
calibration. Four checks do it, and none adds a step to the normal workflow. The first command
after enabling is ``hold the raw position you are reporting right now'', for 0.35\,s. It never
consults the calibration, so it is safe against a completely wrong zero. The raw encoder pose is
recorded at every zero capture, and compared before leaving the limp state. Within a live session a
200\,Hz watchdog looks for the signature of an origin rewrite, which moves the reported position
while the drive reports no speed. Homing uses an $8^{\circ}$ tracking threshold rather than the
$25^{\circ}$ playback value. And a per-joint travel budget stops a guided move that sweeps more
than $1.3\times$ the joint's own range without arriving. On 2026-08-10 that check alone would have
cut the excursion from 1.9 output turns to under 0.6. A hardware-free test reproduces the incident
against a mock bus, with an \texttt{origin\_shift} fault injection.

\textbf{What it found in its first hour.} The recorder's first boot captured its own deployment
failure, the \texttt{ENOBUFS} crash above, which had previously been invisible. Its steady-state
telemetry then established that the left-leg bus was electrically dead, with zero frames
transmitted and all three motors silent. That turned ``the robot feels wrong'' into a timestamped
fact. It also ended a longer-standing blindness, because nothing in the stack had ever measured the
control loop's actual rate. A loop running at a third of its nominal frequency had been
indistinguishable from a healthy one.

\subsection{Hardware experiments}
\label{sec:hw-experiments}

Everything in this section ran on the physical robot, through the web instrument.

\textbf{In-air gait demonstration and teach-and-replay.} The first hardware milestone was a
hand-authored straight-walking gait, with the body clamped in the air. Its safer successor is
teach-and-replay. The motors are held limp by streaming zero current, and a leg is backdriven by
hand through the desired cycle. A pure-NumPy pipeline then resamples, phase-aligns, detects the
left/right mirror, closes the loop with an FFT low-pass and replays the result. Replay exposed a
compliance problem that later measurement explained. At low proportional gain the leg drew only
about 2\,A against a 20\,A allowance, and moved with stick--slip judder. That was an early
unquantified encounter with the drive-bandwidth ceiling.

\textbf{Workspace calibration corrected the model in one way. The cam is a full crank.} The
CAD-guessed range of $\pm86^{\circ}$ is wrong. Recorded sweeps span about $245^{\circ}$ of cam
rotation, and the 4-bar assembles at every angle. The recorded polygon is eroded, and then does
triple duty as training constraint, runtime watchdog and offline deploy gate.

\textbf{Foot arc geometry, measured late and decisive.} The stance leg is a fixed-length pendulum
held at 99.5\,\% of its reach, so every foothold lies on a single arc: forward reach 31\,cm,
\emph{backward reach 2.7\,cm}. The centre of mass sits at the rear edge of the workspace rather
than at its centre, which is why a backward fall can never be caught. Neither the ankle strut nor
the pushrod changes this. But 5\,cm of crouch takes backward reach from 2.7 to 20.2\,cm, for
2\,N$\cdot$m out of 144.5. Re-solving the balanced keyframe on that basis took the scripted
controller of \cref{sec:scripted-baseline} from 0.91\,s to 24.7\,s at the pitch-free milestone.

\textbf{The robot's state as this is written.} The right leg is fully functional under the 200\,Hz
stack, measured at 188\,Hz. The left leg awaits repair of the joint lost on 2026-08-10.
