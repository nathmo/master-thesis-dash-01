\section{Reinforcement learning: attempts and architecture}

\label{sec:rl}

\Cref{sec:robot} supplied a measured plant model.
This chapter develops the controller for it.
Multiple architectures were attempted, starting from raw torque commands and progressively adding structure to the action space.
The main changes were motivated either by training failures or by limitations identified on the real robot.

The learning algorithm itself was kept fixed.
The changes therefore concern the formulation of the task, the action representation, the curriculum and the amount of structure given to the policy.
The final architecture is a Fourier gait generator with impedance parameters and reflexes.

\label{sec:rl-common}

\subsection{Formulation}

The task is a single dash: run as fast as possible.

Several formulations were attempted during the project.
The first formulation only required the robot to run forward, without requiring it to stop at the end of the track.
Later formulations attempted to add velocity command for a joystick input. These formulations were more general, but also substantially harder to train.
Since the objective of this thesis is maximum running speed rather than general locomotion, the final experiments used only the simpler dash formulation.

The learning algorithm was held fixed at PPO~\cite{schulman2017ppo} from Stable-Baselines3, with an actor--critic MLP running on CPU-parallel MuJoCo environments.
Episodes are capped at 60\,s. The control rate is 200\,Hz, which \Cref{sec:plant-id-timing} establishes as both the sensing ceiling and the maximum deployable rate.
Later experiments showed it to be borderline for the final controller and the frequency need to be brought back down to 100Hz as the final policy inference via the web interface with the safety layer proved a bit too slow for the 200Hz control loop on the Raspberry Pi 3B. The final policy therefore needs to be retrained to run at 100Hz.
The computational budget was large but finite and whithin what can be seen in the litterature.
A milestone corresponds to approximately 600\,M environment steps and a multi-day cluster job.
The thesis as a whole used approximately 15\,G environment steps, with an estimated 75 Kg of CO2eq emissions from the cluster usage.
To help put that into perspective, the author exhaled 150 kg of CO2eq during the period of this thesis from respiration or a simple way airplane trip from Geneva to Barcelona.

\subsection{The toy problem}

\label{sec:toy-problem}

Committing a 12-DOF robot to an unproven learning and deployment pipeline is risky.
The complete chain was therefore exercised once at minimum stakes, on a single-motor inverted pendulum.
The purpose of this experiment was not to study locomotion. It was to verify the complete path from simulation to the physical controller.
The chain consists of simulation, training, export, deployment on embedded Linux, and a closed loop over CAN.
The observation space was deliberately restricted to the telemetry available from the real drive.
It contains the motor position in turns, velocity in turns per second and torque.
The observation normalisation statistics used during training are exported as part of the ONNX graph rather than being reconstructed separately on the embedded system.
The resulting policy runs on a beaglebone black using \texttt{onnxruntime}.
The control loop closes over CAN at 50\,Hz.
This experiment therefore tested the software and it's author to help prepare for the full robot before committing the computational and experimental budget.

\subsection{Observation space}

The policy can sees only measurements that can be produced by the hardware.
The observation contains the joint position, joint velocity and joint torque, the IMU gravity direction and angular velocity, the commanded motion and the previous N actions and state.

The observations are expressed in physical units and passed through fixed normalisers.
A history stack covering approximately 200\,ms is used instead of giving the policy access to a simulated base-velocity measurement that would not be available on the physical robot.
Sensor noise, bias drift and CAN staleness are injected according to the measurements from \Cref{sec:robot}.
This makes the observation presented during training closer to the signal available during deployment.
The actor and critic use asymmetric observations.
The actor receives only the deployable observation described above.
The critic additionally receives a privileged simulation-only tail containing quantities such as the true base velocity and contact information.
These privileged quantities are therefore used to improve value estimation but are not available to the deployed policy.

\subsection{Reward design}

The reward was built around forward progress rather than an alive bonus.
A dense forward-progress term rewards the robot for moving towards the end of the track, while a per-step time cost makes standing still increasingly expensive.
This distinction became important during training because an alive bonus rewards the robot for remaining upright even when it has stopped, which the policy learned to game when a joystick driven attempt was made.
For a sprint task this is undesirable: a controller that stands indefinitely can obtain a positive reward without solving the actual task.
The time cost instead makes a stationary solution progressively less attractive.

Termination is also part of the reward structure.
Body contact and leaving the valid workspace terminate the episode.
Consequently, the fall penalty is mainly the future reward that is lost when an episode terminates rather than a single large numerical penalty.

Reward design was repeatedly modified in response to exploitation by the policy.
The robot learned to belly-flop, to move very slowly, to vibrate in place and to skate.
The fall penalty was changed when the robot learned to exploit belly-flopping, the alive-related terms were changed when it learned to dawdle, and foot air-time and action-rate terms were introduced to suppress vibration and other non-locomotive solutions.

This was preferable to repeatedly changing the relative weight of all reward terms.
Each additional term was intended to prevent a specific mechanism that had been observed on the robot or in simulation.
Action-rate penalties where introduced to limit rapid changes in the command sent to the actuator.
Gait-specification-rate penalties limit rapid changes in the parameters of the gait generator.
Torque and energy terms discourage solutions that use unnecessarily large actuator effort.
These terms therefore do not only regularise the policy numerically; they also constrain behaviours that would be undesirable on the physical robot.

\subsection{Evaluation protocol}

Training-time episode length is not used directly as the final performance metric.
During exploration, the policy contains a non-zero action distribution, and a long episode can therefore result partly from the injected exploration noise.
The results are obtained from greedy evaluations, in which the exploration noise is disabled and the policy deterministically executes its preferred action at each timestep.
This measures the behaviour that the learned policy will produces once deployed, rather than its behaviour under exploration noise.

The experiments also showed that performance is not well described by a single mean over a small number of seeds.
Runs were frequently bimodal: a seed either discovered a useful gait or remained in a non-locomotive behaviour for a large part of the available budget.
Comparisons therefore use paired seeds and survivor counts where possible rather than relying on small unpaired averages.

\subsection{The action-space ladder}

\label{sec:rl-ladder}

Several action spaces were attempted, from the least structured representation to the final gait-generator representation.
The progression is not presented as a hierarchy in which each later representation is universally better.
Each one was tried to solve a problem and introduced a different limitation.

\subsubsection{Direct joint torque}

\label{sec:rl-torque}

ADD ARCHITECTURE SCREENSHOT

The first framework commanded joint torque directly on a simplified biped, because the robot was still being designed. It was abandoned for two different reasons: the training did not converge, and the assumed hardware interface was not considered deployable at the required rate.

As written earlier, the training did not converge after many attempt at shaping the reward function.
The fall penalty rose because the robot learned to belly-flop.
The alive bonus changed because it learned to dawdle.
The feet-air-time thresholds were retuned because it learned to vibrate in place.

The underlying difficulty is that a raw torque action space requires the policy to synthesise two separate behaviours simultaneously ;
It has to generate the gravity compensation required to keep the robot upright, while also discovering the periodic structure required for locomotion.
Between these behaviours, the reward landscape contains many ways of falling over that can nevertheless produce a temporarily favourable reward.

The second problem was the assumed deployment path ;
Direct torque control bypasses the drive's internal position or impedance loop and requires current or torque commands to be streamed over CAN.
A simple PID loop was tested over the CAN bus as a practical check.
As expected, the leg could track slow points, but became increasingly jittery when the desired trajectory was played faster.

\subsubsection{Joint position targets}
\label{sec:rl-position}

ADD ARCHITECTURE SCREENSHOT

The policy was now expected to ouput six position targets for it's action vector, one for each actuated joint.
In simulation these commands drive MuJoCo position actuators with gains and force ranges derived from the actuator specifications.
On the physical robot the action maps directly to the position targets accepted by the drives.
Two hardware implementations were considered.
A smart-servo type interface provides its own internal position loop, while the MIT interface exposes position, stiffness and damping parameters.
Measurements in \Cref{sec:plant-id} showed a large bandwidth difference between these interfaces.
And the PID loop that are cascaded in the motor driver are not known which make this approach a liability when it come to sim2real gap.
The MIT mode consequently became the relevant interface for the final experiments.
Furthermore, in one experiment the robot learned to skate while the reward curve continued to improve.
This taught the author that that episode reward alone was insufficient to infer the success of a policy for locomotion.
More reward shapping was attempted to penalize this behaviour with limited success.

\subsubsection{Impedance with static gains}

\label{sec:rl-impedance}

ADD ARCHITECTURE SCREENSHOT

After a significant ammount of time spent to understand how to send command to the CubeMars drives in impedance mode to avoid the actuator bandwith issue in servo mode, the action space was extended to include stiffness and damping.

The MIT command frame exposes $k_p$ and $k_d$, so the position target becomes an impedance command.
With fixed values, the policy can move the target position while the actuator controller provides the corresponding compliance.
This raised the actuator bandwith by an order of magniture but it would not solve the previous issue where the policy was not having a still reward farming and not running.
The limitation is that fixed gains impose the same compliance during all phases of the gait.
A stiffness that is useful during stance is not necessarily appropriate during swing or at foot impact.
The next step was therefore to make compliance part of the gait specification rather than treating it as a constant actuator setting.
This approach was therefore not attempted further and no policy was trained for it.

\subsubsection{A Fourier gait generator tuned by the policy in position and compliance space}

\label{sec:rl-cpg}

ADD ARCHITECTURE SCREENSHOT

The main motivation for introducing a Fourier-based gait generator was to address a limitation observed with direct position control.
In the latter approach, the policy was free to specify the position of each joint independently at every control step.
This provided a large space in which the policy could exploit unintended behaviours, including repeated reward-gaming strategies observed during training.
Rather than requiring the policy to discover the periodic structure of locomotion from scratch, the gait generator was therefore introduced to explicitly impose this structure while retaining enough freedom to adapt the gait.
The policy no longer specifies every joint position independently, but instead controls a lower-dimensional Fourier representation of the gait.
Once the compliance mode was unlocked, it was also natural to extend this parameterization to the stiffness and damping.
Since the required compliance can reasonably be expected to depend on the phase of the gait, these parameters were represented using the same principle as the joint trajectories.
The resulting generator therefore describes not only where the joints should move, but also how strongly they should track the generated trajectory throughout the gait cycle.
This allows, for example, different compliance characteristics during impact and push-off.

To further reduce the dimensionality of the problem, a single generator was initially used for both legs.
The right-leg trajectory was constrained to follow the same Fourier representation as the left leg, with a phase shift of $\pi$.
This imposed the expected left/right alternation of a straight-line running gait while substantially reducing the number of parameters that the policy had to determine.
This assumption, however, proved to be too restrictive.
In particular, the learned policy tended to accept the reward penalty associated with continuously modifying the Fourier coefficients rather than maintaining a fixed set of coefficients.
This behaviour suggests that the two legs could not be adequately described by a single phase-shifted trajectory and that independent corrections were important for maintaining the desired motion.

Despite this behaviour, the Fourier generator produced a working policy.
Although the Fourier coefficients were updated continuously at each policy step, the resulting gait remained approximately periodic, with the generated trajectories varying by only a few percent over successive cycles.
With all degrees of freedom released, the policy achieved stable running at speeds of up to approximately $3,\mathrm{m/s}$.
This observation motivated a further experiment to determine whether the learned gait itself was stable, independently of the policy's continuous corrections.
Static Fourier coefficients found to emulate the gait cycle that the policy was commanding.
The robot was then commanded using only this fixed Fourier gait, without allowing the policy to modify the coefficients during execution.
The robot was able to reach approximately the same running speed, but lost balance after only a few steps.
The resulting behaviour demonstrates that the learned Fourier trajectory does not constitute a dynamically stable open-loop gait.
Instead, the periodic trajectory provides the nominal locomotion pattern, while continuous policy updates are required to compensate for disturbances and state deviations and keep the robot upright.


\subsubsection{Explicit oscillator against the Fourier series}

\label{sec:cpg-ab}

The Fourier parameterisation is one member of a larger family of structured gait representations.
Another canonical representation is an explicit limit-cycle oscillator, as used in the CPG literature~\cite{ijspeert2008,cpgrl2022}.

In the spirit of academic curiosity, the two representations were therefore compared experimentally.
Sixteen cluster jobs shared the same plant, reward and curriculum schedule, with the generator being the main variable.

Building the oscillator arm required a foot-space formulation and a measured inverse-kinematics lookup table.
Constructing this table exposed several properties of the mechanism that were not obvious from the CAD model.
From the stance pose, both the cam and the thigh move the toe predominantly in the fore--aft direction.
In addition, either sign of the cam motion shortens the nearly straight leg.
The four-bar also contains a folded solution branch that must be filtered from the inverse-kinematics lookup table.
The inversion has to preserve continuity between neighbouring cells; otherwise it can switch branches during the swing.
Finally, the reachable vertical foot displacement at mid-stride is only approximately 8\,cm because the leg is close to its straight configuration.
The comparison is an overall tie.
The oscillator appeared more sample-efficient from a cold start on the hardest tested milestone.
At 133\,M environment steps it reaches an episode length of 408 control steps, compared with 237 for the Fourier controller.
The Fourier generator is more reliable across seeds.
On the warm-started pitch-free plant it crosses the evaluation line in 5 of 5 runs at 2.45\,m/s, compared with 3 of 5 runs at 2.40\,m/s for the oscillator.
The single best controller measured in this experiment was an oscillator run.
It was the only one of the sixteen runs to execute the stop phase, reaching 2.9\,m/s with a left/right asymmetry of 0.08.
When run through the full curriculum ladder, the oscillator cleared m1 to m4, including a 100\,m dash at m2 and 2.95\,m/s at m3.
The limitation appeared at m5, when roll was released.
The oscillator reached an episode length of 337 control steps, compared with 3213 for the Fourier ladder at the matched budget.
The plateau was confirmed after increasing the budget by a factor of 2.5.
Both generators use the same three-parameter roll reflex, so the difference is attributed to the gait-generator part of the controller rather than the lateral reflex.
The Fourier generator was nevertheless kept as it more toroughly tested and better understood by the author.
The result suggests that the structured action-space family is appropriate and thus the fourier formulation is not the only optimal one.

\subsection{Curriculum design}

\label{sec:rl-curriculum}

The action-space experiments showed that providing structure to the gait was necessary, but it did not solve the balance problem by itself.
The second major design variable was therefore the difficulty of the simulated plant.

\subsubsection{The base-DOF locking ladder}

The curriculum consists of six milestones, m1--m6.
The base degrees of freedom are progressively released using equality constraints.
The first milestone constrains the robot to a forward/backward rail, the following free the vertical dimmension to let the policy learn to support the weight of the robot.
Subsequent milestones release pitch and yaw before reaching the fully free plant.

The purpose is to postpone the hardest balance problem until a basic locomotion pattern has already been learned.
On the first milestone the robot does not need to solve full-body balance.
A ride-height rail and the measured inverse-kinematics lookup table make this simplified problem sufficiently constrained to be trainable.

Balance is therefore learned last rather than being required from the beginning.
This is a form of curriculum over plant controllability rather than over the command or terrain as it was done in some papers.

\subsubsection{Progression must be gated, not scheduled}

The first versions of the curriculum used fixed, clock-driven progression.
This caused the curriculum to release a new degree of freedom before the policy had learned the previous problem.
In particular, the policy could be released before it had learned sufficient pitch balance, producing immediate falls.
The progression was consequently changed to performance-gated advancement.
The next milestone is unlocked only when the policy satisfies a performance criterion on the current one.
The difficulty therefore follows the competence of the policy rather than wall-clock training time.

\subsubsection{Exploration schedule}

The exploration schedule produced another deadlock.
The action standard deviation remained pinned at its maximum for approximately 150\,M environment steps.
The policy was therefore being trained under a level of exploration that prevented reliable exploitation of a useful gait.

The solution was to combine a forced annealing deadline with a performance gate.
Exploration is allowed to remain high while the policy is improving, but it cannot remain indefinitely at the maximum value.

This change also closed the difference between stochastic and greedy evaluation observed in the earlier experiments.

\subsubsection{Domain randomization from the measured plant}

The domain randomisation ranges are inherited from the measurements in \Cref{sec:robot}, including the mass-equivalent inertia uncertainty, timing jitter and actuation delay.

The purpose of domain randomization here is therefore robustness over a distribution of measured plants.

\subsection{The pitch wall}

\label{sec:rl-pitch-wall}

The hardest curriculum transition occurred when pitch was released.
Once pitch became a free degree of freedom, the policy repeatedly converged either to standing or slow creeping, or fell.
No capture step emerged.

The plant itself cannot remain passively upright in the relevant configuration.
A diagnostic of the learned policy showed that, while falling forward, the feet were placed approximately 8\,cm behind the centre of mass.
The policy was therefore not solving the expected capture-step problem.

This failure could not be attributed to the policy alone.
Scripted controllers were used to separate the plant limitation from the learning problem.

A zero-action controller combined with a re-solved stance keyframe survived for approximately 25\,s.
The required stance lean changed from approximately $5.7^\circ$ to close to zero after the keyframe was re-solved.
This showed that part of the original difficulty came from the reference stance itself.

Several modifications improved the duration of the learned behaviour without solving the wall.
Re-solving the keyframe provided additional stability.
A pitch-reflex prior also helped.
An angular-momentum reward term produced another improvement, and rejuvenating the warm-started policy avoided some previously converged but unproductive states.
None of these modifications produced sustained running.

The final resolution came by reverting the policy from a joystick controller to a "run as fast as possible" one.
The earlier command-tracking objectives rewarded the policy for satisfying a motion target while remaining upright.
Such an objective can be satisfied by standing or creeping when the commanded motion becomes difficult.
The sprint objective instead makes standing unprofitable because progress is continuously rewarded and time is penalised.

With the sprint objective applied to the measured MIT-mode plant, one cold run produced the first true runner.
The detailed performance and the margins of this controller are evaluated in \Cref{sec:results}.
