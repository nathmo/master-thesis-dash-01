\section{Reinforcement learning: attempts and architecture}
\label{sec:rl}

\Cref{sec:robot} supplied a measured plant model. This chapter develops the
controller for it. 

Multiple architectures were attempted from raw torque command to a structured gait generator while trying a variety of action spaces.
The chapter closes with the final architecture, which is a Fourier gait generator.

\subsection{Probleme formulation and general RL architecture}
\label{sec:rl-common}

\paragraph{Formulation.} The task is a single dash: run as fast as possible. 
Multiple formulation where attempted, from a simple dash with no care to stop it to a policy that could be steered with a joystick with the later proven much harder to train.

The learning algorithm was held fixed at PPO~\cite{schulman2017ppo} from Stable-Baselines3, with an
actor--critic MLP on CPU-parallel MuJoCo environments. Episodes are capped at 60\,s. The control
rate is 200\,Hz, which \cref{sec:plant-id-timing} establishes as both the sensing ceiling and the
deployable rate. A milestone is roughly 600\,M environment steps and a multi-day cluster job, and
the project as a whole spent roughly 3\,G steps. That budget is large rather than asymptotic, and
the distinction becomes load-bearing in \cref{sec:margin-analysis}.

\paragraph{The toy problem } \label{sec:toy-problem} Committing a 12-DOF robot to an
unproven pipeline is expensive. The complete chain was therefore exercised once at minimum stakes,
on a single-motor inverted pendulum. That chain is simulate, train, export, deploy on embedded
Linux, and close a loop over CAN. The observation is deliberately the hardware telemetry and
nothing else: position in turns, velocity in turns per second, and torque, exactly as the drive
reports them. The export bakes the observation normalisation statistics \emph{into} the ONNX graph.
The policy then runs on a Raspberry Pi under \texttt{onnxruntime}, closing the loop over CAN at
50\,Hz with a watchdog.

\subsection{The action-space ladder}
\label{sec:rl-ladder}
Here are the different action spaces that where attempted, from the simplest to the most complex.
\subsubsection{Direct joint torque}
\label{sec:rl-torque}

The first framework commanded joint torque directly, on a simplified biped, because the robot was
still being designed. It was abandoned for two reasons, and the two failures are of different
kinds.

\textbf{It did not converge.} The training history from that period is an unbroken sequence of
reward-shaping attempts. The fall penalty rose because the robot learned to belly-flop. The alive
bonus changed because it learned to dawdle. The feet-air-time thresholds were retuned because it
learned to vibrate in place. The underlying problem is that a raw torque action space forces the
policy to synthesise two things from scratch at every control step. One is the gravity compensation
that keeps the robot standing. The other is the periodic structure that makes it walk. The reward
landscape between those two is dominated by ways of falling over that convince the reward the robot
is doing well.

\textbf{It was undeployable} Commanding torque means bypassing the drive's internal loop, and streaming current setpoints over CAN.
The robot was tested with a simple PID loop over the CAN bus out of curiosity and as expected it's way too slow to command the actuator in torque over CAN.
The leg was able to track points but it became jittery as soon as the gait to be tracked became faster.



\subsubsection{Joint position targets}
\label{sec:rl-position}

The second design made the action six position targets, one per actuated joint. The knee and ankle
follow passively. In simulation these drive MuJoCo \texttt{<position>} actuators, with gains and
force ranges from the datasheets. On hardware the action maps one to one onto what the drives
accept.

\subsubsection{Explicit oscillator against the Fourier series}
\label{sec:cpg-ab}

The Fourier parameterisation is one member of a family. The other canonical member is the explicit
limit-cycle oscillator of the Ijspeert school~\cite{ijspeert2008,cpgrl2022}. The literature gave no
basis to prefer one, so the question was measured. Sixteen cluster jobs shared one plant, one
reward and one schedule, with only the generator swapped. \Cref{fig:experiments}(a) shows the cold
start on the pitch-free plant.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{Figures/fig_experiments.pdf}
\caption{Learning curves of three controlled comparisons, episode length in seconds against
environment steps: (a) generator family from a cold start on m3, oscillator against Fourier;
(b) drive bandwidth from a cold start on m2, as built (0.8\,Hz), retuned (3\,Hz) and idealised
(12\,Hz); (c) ankle variants from a cold start on m3.}
\label{fig:experiments}
\end{figure}

Building the oscillator arm forced a foot-space formulation with a measured inverse-kinematics
lookup table. Constructing that table surfaced four plant facts. From the stance pose, \emph{both}
cam and thigh move the toe mostly fore--aft, and either sign of cam \emph{shortens} the
near-straight leg. The intuitive ``thigh swings, cam lifts'' mapping is therefore wrong, and it
produces no step. The 4-bar has a folded solution branch that must be filtered out. The inversion
must propagate continuity between cells, or it flips branches mid-swing. And reachable foot lift at
mid-stride is only about 8\,cm, because the leg is near-straight there.

\textbf{The verdict is a tie, with complementary strengths.} The oscillator is more sample-efficient
from a cold start on the hardest milestone. It reaches an episode length of 408 control steps
against the Fourier arm's 237, at 133\,M steps. The Fourier generator is more reliable across
seeds. On the warm-started pitch-free plant it crosses the line on 5 of 5 episodes at 2.45\,m/s,
against the oscillator's 3 of 5 at 2.40\,m/s.

The single best controller measured in the whole experiment was an oscillator run. It was the only
arm of the sixteen that ever executed the stop phase, at 2.9\,m/s with a left/right asymmetry of
0.08. Run up the full ladder, the oscillator cleared m1 to m4, with a 100\,m dash at m2 and
2.95\,m/s at m3. It then walled hard at m5, where roll is freed: episode length 337 against the
Fourier ladder's 3213 at matched budget, with the plateau confirmed at 2.5$\times$ budget. Both
share the identical three-parameter roll reflex, so the difference lies in the generator, and it
points future work at lateral authority. The Fourier generator was kept for the deployment lineage.
The oscillator result was used as tiebreak evidence that the action-space \emph{family} is right.

One ablation refutes a hypothesis stated earlier in this chapter. Removing the per-step residual
channel, leaving a pure oscillator, produced \textbf{stability without locomotion}. It survived
9700 of 12\,000 steps, but travelled 15\,m of 72 at 0.25\,m/s, with the worst cadence of all
sixteen arms. The residual channel was the suspected fast open path behind a persistent
6\,Hz footfall chatter. Removing it made the chatter worse, so that explanation is wrong.
It is also the empirical form of the literature's verdict that a per-step channel is mandatory. No
equivalent arm was run on the Fourier generator, so the result constrains the family rather than
this exact controller.
