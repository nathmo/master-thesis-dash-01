\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

This chapter measures the distance between the objective introduced in \cref{sec:intro-objective} and the results reported in \cref{sec:results}.
It then discusses the lessons learned from the control methodology and ranks the changes that are most likely to close the remaining performance gap.

\subsection{Method-level lessons}

Several lessons emerged from the development of the learned controller.

\paragraph{Optimisers exploit objectives.}
The policy repeatedly found behaviours that maximised the numerical objective without satisfying the intended locomotion task.
Examples included skating, penetrating the floor, and exploiting high-frequency contact dynamics.

This behaviour is an instance of Goodhart's law.
The limitation of encoding human intents in a cost function have been studied and this is an expected result~\cite{maier2025goodhart}.

\paragraph{The simulator must enforce the physical envelope.}
The final runner provides a second example of the same principle.
Although the commanded torques remain almost entirely inside the measured torque--speed envelope, the swing-leg velocity reaches approximately twice the measured no-load actuator speed.
The resulting gait therefore exploits a missing physical constraint in the simulator.
This does not invalidate the learning framework, but it means that the achieved 3.07\,m/s should be interpreted as an optimistic simulation result rather than a directly deployable hardware speed.

The implication for future training is that constraints which are physically absolute should preferably be encoded directly in the plant rather than only discouraged through the reward.
In particular, actuator velocity limits and thermal behaviour should be included in the simulator before further optimisation of maximum speed.

\paragraph{The policy uses the available control authority.}
The perturbation experiments show that the final controller has little authority remaining above the nominal plant.
Increasing the robot mass or introducing additional delay rapidly degrades performance.
This behaviour is consistent with the optimisation objective: the policy was explicitly trained to maximise speed and therefore has an incentive to use the available actuator authority for propulsion rather than reserve it for disturbance rejection outside of its trained range.

Consequently, robustness should not be expected to emerge automatically.
If robustness to mass uncertainty, latency, impacts, or other plant variations is required, those variations should be represented explicitly in the training distribution.

\subsection{Mechanical limits and performance levers}

The approximately 6\,m/s physical ceiling derived in \cref{sec:speed-ceiling} provides a useful reference for deciding where further development effort should be spent.
From the result of the sensitivity analysis, the following point can be made.

\subsubsection{Increase the usable leg workspace}

The first design objective should be to increase the usable fore--aft workspace of the foot.
The available stroke directly enters the speed-ceiling calculation: for a given stride frequency, a larger usable foot sweep allows a larger stride and therefore a higher forward speed.

The four-bar geometry and segment dimensions should be optimised together to maximise the portion of the workspace that is useful during running along with the level of force anysotropy required.

\subsubsection{Reduce the force anisotropy}

A second and potentially inexpensive lever is to redesign the four-bar linkage so that more of the available actuator torque can be converted into fore--aft ground force.

At the current standing pose, the approximately 19:1 force anisotropy indicates that the mechanism is mechanically well suited to supporting vertical load but poorly suited to producing horizontal propulsion.
Increasing motor torque would increase both capabilities, but would not address this missplacement of control authority that stem from the 4bar linkage anysotropy.

A redesigned linkage could therefore improve effective propulsive force without requiring a proportionally larger actuator.
For this reason, linkage geometry should be explored before actuator scaling.
The design problem should combine at least two objectives: maximising usable fore--aft workspace and maintaining favourable force transmission throughout the stance region.

\subsubsection{Reduce distal leg mass}

The sensitivity analysis also identifies moving leg mass as an important performance lever.
Reducing the leg mass by 30\,\% increases the estimated speed ceiling by approximately 0.7\,m/s.

The practical margin for improvement may, however, be limited because the existing distal links are already lightweight.
Further reduction would therefore need to be achieved without sacrificing stiffness or durability.

Where reductions remain possible, priority should be given to distal mass rather than total robot mass.
Mass located near the foot contributes strongly to the inertia that must be accelerated and reversed during each swing phase, whereas an equivalent mass reduction near the torso has a much smaller effect on swing dynamics.

\subsubsection{Increase actuator torque density}

If linkage optimisation and mass reduction are insufficient, increasing actuator capability is the next direct lever altough a much pricier one.

For a future platform, actuator selection should consequently favour torque density and continuous thermal capability rather than simply the largest available peak-torque rating.
Contemporary record-oriented humanoids appear to use substantially higher torque capability than DASH-01. The author guesstimate them to be around 400 Nm peak but no credible source could be found to confirm nor deny this value.

\subsubsection{Bus voltage is not presently a priority}

Increasing the bus voltage initially appears attractive because it raises the actuator no-load speed.
The sensitivity study, however, indicates that a 30\,\% voltage increase produces essentially no increase in the current speed ceiling.
At the operating point considered here, the mechanism is limited by torque, inertia, workspace, and available stance impulse before it reaches the no-load-speed limit.
Increasing voltage should therefore not be treated as a primary performance lever for the present design.
It may become relevant after other limitations have been removed, but the current results do not justify prioritising it.
