\section{Conclusion and Future Work}

\subsection{Summary of Findings}

This thesis set out to apply reinforcement learning to a custom high-speed biped and delivered,
in the end, three things: a working method (the modelling, training and curriculum stack that
produces a 2.6\,m/s never-falling runner on the free simulated plant), a measured machine (every
load-bearing parameter of DASH-01 replaced by a measurement, with stated uncertainty and stated
un-resolvables), and an honest account of the distance between the two. The distance is not
rhetorical: the measured robot is heavier, weaker and — decisively — an order of magnitude
slower in actuation than the trained-against model, and its corrected ceiling
($\approx$2.7\,m/s burst, $\approx$1.3\,m/s sustained) reclassifies the platform from record
contender to demonstrator. The learning method itself is vindicated where it can be tested: it
matches or is matched by classical control exactly at the plant's own limits, which is what a
healthy learning stack should do.

\subsection{Challenges and Limitations}

The limits of the present work are recorded throughout and summarised once: no policy has yet
run on the hardware (export and inference on the Pi remain the critical path); the sprint
results rest on privileged observations; the link inertia tensors and the $\sim$0.9\,N$\cdot$m
gravity residual remain unresolved by identification, with the kinematic model the remaining
suspect; the actuator model lacks a coupled torque--speed envelope, optimistic exactly at high
speed; the capability analysis's corrected figures are a sensitivity propagation awaiting a full
re-sweep; and the physical robot currently stands with one functional leg.

\subsection{Suggestions for Future Research}
\label{sec:future}

For \emph{this} robot, the priorities are ordered by the record: ONNX export and a Pi inference
loop (everything else waits on it); current-mode control with the position PD closed on the Pi
at 200\,Hz, replacing the 0.8\,Hz drive loop that every measurement identifies as the binding
constraint; the carbon ankle strut; and the from-scratch cadence retrain.

For a \emph{successor} robot, the measured record ranks the design levers:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{Bus voltage before torque}: 87\,\% of feasible gaits are limited at the cam's
        peak-power corner, and speed scales linearly with no-load speed and not at all with
        peak torque.
  \item \textbf{Distal mass}: at $-0.93$\,(m/s)/kg, nine times the leverage of torso mass; a
        carbon shin and a proximally mounted spring on a tendon (a true Achilles) recover
        $\approx$0.5\,m/s for free.
  \item \textbf{Re-proportion or replace the 4-bar}: only 0.20--0.35\,m of a 1.06\,m geometric
        span is usable, and the standing pose sits at 96\,\% of reach where the toe-force
        ellipse is 19:1 anisotropic — strong vertically, weak in exactly the propulsive
        direction. Target a flat transmission ratio over $\sim$0.5\,m with stance at
        $\sim$85\,\% of reach.
  \item \textbf{Size the ankle for 3.5 body weights} — the present passive ankle delivers a
        median 0.42\,BW over the running-stance region, 8$\times$ short, in positive mechanical
        feedback.
  \item \textbf{Cool the cam motor}: sustained speed is what a 100\,m record is made of, and the
        gap between 167\,N$\cdot$m demanded and 55\,N$\cdot$m continuous is thermal.
  \item \textbf{Torque-mode control as an architectural requirement from day one} — the v1
        lesson, twice paid for.
\end{enumerate}

Finally, the validated toe-Jacobian force map and the capability sweep make a
\emph{design search in simulation} — over bus voltage, gear ratio, distal mass, linkage
proportions and ankle stiffness — the natural next use of the training cluster: searching over
robots, rather than over controllers for a robot already known to be the constraint.
