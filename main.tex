\section*{Abstract}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Abstract}

This thesis documents the development of DASH-01, a custom biped with a parallel-linkage leg.
The robot asks how fast a small, low-cost legged robot can run. More centrally, it asks what
transferring reinforcement-learning locomotion methods onto real hardware actually costs.

The work covers the full chain. It models a leg that closes a kinematic loop, simulates that
model in MuJoCo, and runs a measurement campaign that replaces every assumed plant parameter
with a measured one. It develops three successive action spaces for the learned controller:
direct torque, per-step position targets, and a central pattern generator tuned by the policy.
It adds a curriculum that releases the base degrees of freedom one at a time. It ends with the
deployment stack on the robot itself: a 200\,Hz control daemon on a non-realtime Raspberry Pi, a
web-based bring-up instrument, and an always-on flight recorder.

The headline simulation result is a policy that runs at 2.6\,m/s on the fully free plant without
falling. The headline measurement result is less comfortable. The real robot is 18\,\% heavier
and about 15\,\% weaker than the plant every policy trained against, and its actuator position
loop is more than an order of magnitude slower. Those corrections move the robot's credible
ceiling to roughly 2.7\,m/s burst and 1.3\,m/s sustained. DASH-01 is therefore a walking and
jogging demonstrator, not a record platform.

The thesis argues, with evidence, that such a gap is the normal product of simulation-first
development. The countermeasures are methodological: measure early, treat every training aid and
every reward term as an exploit target, evaluate on fixed tasks with paired seeds, and instrument
the hardware so that failures stay reconstructible. A hand-designed classical controller, written
late in the project as a baseline, beats the learned policy on the constrained plant. It then
fails at exactly the same wall on the free plant. The binding constraint is the robot, not the
learning algorithm.
