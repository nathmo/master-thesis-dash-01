\section*{Abstract}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Abstract}

This thesis documents the development of DASH-01, a custom parallel-linkage biped built to
investigate how fast a small, low-cost legged robot can run, and, more centrally, what it
actually takes to transfer reinforcement-learning locomotion methods to a physical robot. The work covers the full chain: mechanical modelling of a
closed-kinematic-loop leg, simulation in MuJoCo, a measurement campaign that replaced every assumed plant parameter with a measured one,
three successive action-space designs for the learned controller (direct torque, per-step position targets, and a policy-tuned central pattern generator), a base-DOF-release
curriculum, and deployment infrastructure on the robot itself. a 200\,Hz control daemon on a
non-realtime Raspberry Pi, a web-based bring-up instrument, and an always-on flight recorder.

The headline simulation result is a policy that runs at 2.6\,m/s on the fully free plant without
falling. The headline measurement result is less comfortable: the real robot is 18\,\% heavier,
$\sim$15\,\% weaker, and its actuator position loop is more than an order of magnitude slower than
the model every policy was trained against, which moves the robot's credible ceiling to roughly
2.7\,m/s burst and 1.3\,m/s sustained — a walking-and-jogging demonstrator, not a record platform.
The thesis argues, with evidence, that this gap is the normal product of simulation-first
development, and that the countermeasures are methodological: measure early, treat every training
aid and reward term as an exploit target, evaluate on fixed tasks with paired seeds, and instrument
the hardware so that failures are reconstructible. A hand-designed classical controller, built
late in the project as a baseline, beats the learned policy on the constrained plant and fails at
exactly the same wall on the free plant — demonstrating that the binding constraint is the machine,
not the learning algorithm.
