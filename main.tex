\section*{Abstract}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Abstract}

This thesis investigates the use of reinforcement learning to develop a high-speed running controller for DASH-01, a 15\,kg bipedal robot designed for fast locomotion. The objective was to determine how far reinforcement learning could push the running speed of a small, lightweight biped while accounting for the limitations of the physical platform.

The work follows the complete path from the robot model to a deployable controller. The robot was reconstructed in MuJoCo from the mechanical design, and the actuator, sensing and communication interfaces were characterised experimentally. These measurements were used to correct the simulation and to establish a first-principles upper bound on the achievable running speed. Under the measured kinematic, inertial and actuator constraints, this bound was estimated at approximately 6\,m/s.

Several reinforcement-learning formulations were then investigated. Direct torque control did not converge within the available training budget and was initially rejected because of an assumed communication-bandwidth limitation. Per-step position control converged to several non-locomotive solutions, including skating. The action space was consequently structured around a periodic gait generator. Fourier-series and explicit oscillator representations were compared, together with impedance parameters and fast balance reflexes. A curriculum progressively released the robot's base degrees of freedom, allowing locomotion to be learned before full-body balance was required.

The experiments show that the formulation of the learning problem was at least as important as the choice of learning algorithm. In particular, reward maximisation could produce apparently successful policies that exploited the simulation rather than running, while external balance assistance could produce policies dependent on the assistance. The final curriculum reached sustained running in simulation, with the best policies reaching approximately 3\,m/s.
The resulting framework provides a measured and deployable RL pipeline for high-speed bipedal locomotion, together with an experimentally grounded estimate of the remaining gap between the current robot and its physical speed limit. The results indicate that further progress is likely to require changes to the mechanical plant and actuator capability rather than simply increasing the reinforcement-learning budget.
